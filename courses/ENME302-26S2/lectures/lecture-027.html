Key concepts

• Structured mesh: A mesh with an organised, regular connectivity pattern.

• Unstructured mesh: A mesh without a regular connectivity pattern; it may contain triangular or quadrilateral elements.

• Delaunay mesh: An unstructured triangular mesh mentioned in the context of Lab 2.

• Mesh refinement: Increasing the local mesh resolution, either throughout the domain or in selected regions.

• Swept mesh: A mesh created by meshing one face and sweeping that mesh through the domain in another direction.

• Finite difference method: A method for replacing derivatives in a PDE with algebraic approximations at discrete grid points.

• Gauss–Seidel method: An iterative method that updates each unknown using the most recently available values.

• Liebmann method: The name used in the lecture for applying a Gauss–Seidel-type iterative scheme to PDEs.

• Diagonal dominance: A matrix property for which the diagonal coefficient is sufficiently large relative to the off-diagonal coefficients. The lecture identifies diagonally dominant systems as well suited to the iterative method discussed.

• Over-relaxation: Modifying an iterative update to move further toward the next estimate, potentially reducing the number of iterations.

• Under-relaxation: Dampening an update when direct or over-relaxed iteration is unstable or converges poorly.

• Convergence criterion: A specified error threshold at which the iteration is stopped.

• Isoline or contour line: A line joining points with the same value of a dependent variable.

• Isotherm: A contour line of constant temperature.

• Heat flux: The rate and direction of heat transfer, represented as a vector.

• Dirichlet boundary condition: A boundary condition that directly specifies the dependent variable, such as temperature.

• Neumann boundary condition: A boundary condition that specifies a derivative or normal flux, such as a temperature gradient.

• Ghost node: An artificial grid point outside the physical domain introduced to impose a boundary condition.

• Central differencing: A derivative approximation using values on both sides of the point of interest.

• Non-uniform grid: A grid in which the spacing between nodes varies.

• Post-processing: Visualising and analysing a numerical solution after solving the governing PDE.

Motivation for iterative methods

A discretised PDE produces a system of algebraic equations:

A T = b

For a small problem, a direct method such as Gaussian elimination or an LU decomposition can be practical. However, the matrix can become very large as the number of degrees of freedom increases.

Examples given in the lecture:

• A previous example had 9 unknowns.

• A larger finite-element example could contain tens or hundreds of thousands of degrees of freedom.

• A 20 × 20 grid would contain 400 unknowns and therefore 400 equations.

• The corresponding dense matrix would have 400 × 400 entries.

Although the coefficient matrix may contain hundreds of thousands of entries, most entries are zero. For the two-dimensional heat-equation example, the non-zero entries form a pentadiagonal pattern because each interior node is coupled mainly to its four neighbouring nodes.

Storing the complete matrix is inefficient because:

• Most of the matrix entries are zero.

• Memory is used to store values that do not contribute to the equations.

• Direct methods may require additional storage and computation during elimination or decomposition.

An iterative method avoids directly solving the entire system in one operation. Instead, it starts with an initial estimate and repeatedly improves the values until the change between iterations is sufficiently small.

Gauss–Seidel/Liebmann iteration on a uniform grid

For the steady two-dimensional heat equation with a uniform grid and no source term, the temperature at an interior node is the average of its four neighbouring temperatures.

Using indices i and j, the update has the form

Tᵢⱼ = (Tᵢ₊₁,ⱼ + Tᵢ₋₁,ⱼ + Tᵢ,ⱼ₊₁ + Tᵢ,ⱼ₋₁) / 4

The lecture described this as being obtained by rearranging the discretised Laplace equation for the point Tᵢⱼ.

In the Gauss–Seidel implementation, the most recent estimates are used immediately. Therefore, as the grid is traversed:

• A value calculated earlier in the current iteration is used when updating later nodes.

• Boundary values remain fixed.

• Interior-node values are updated in place.

• There is no need to retain a separate complete set of previous intermediate values if the code is written in this way.

For the illustrative example, the initial guess for the interior temperatures was zero. The lecture noted that a physically informed initial guess, such as an average temperature, might be better. However, for a Laplace problem, an initial guess far outside the range implied by the boundary conditions is not physically sensible.

Over-relaxation

If basic Gauss–Seidel iteration converges slowly, over-relaxation can move the updated value further in the direction of the new estimate.

The lecture introduces an intermediate value, Tᵢⱼ,new*, calculated using the Liebmann/Gauss–Seidel update. The relaxed value is then

Tᵢⱼ,new = λ Tᵢⱼ,new* + (1 − λ) Tᵢⱼ,old

where λ is the relaxation factor.

For over-relaxation:

1 < λ < 2

Special cases:

• λ = 1 gives the ordinary Gauss–Seidel update:

Tᵢⱼ,new = Tᵢⱼ,new*

• λ closer to 2 applies a stronger correction.

• Values that are too close to 2 can cause oscillation, instability, or very slow convergence.

• Under-relaxation corresponds to using a factor below 1, although the lecture did not develop its equation in detail.

The intermediate starred value is an auxiliary variable. Code may either calculate it explicitly or combine the Gauss–Seidel and relaxation operations into a single expression.

Visualisation and heat flux

Once the temperature field has converged, it can be post-processed and visualised.

Isotherms are contour lines of constant temperature. They are analogous to isobars in weather maps, which represent lines of constant pressure.

The heat-flux vector is given by Fourier’s law:

q̇ = −k ∇T

where:

• q̇ is the heat-flux vector.

• k is the thermal conductivity.

• ∇T is the temperature gradient.

• The negative sign indicates that heat flows in the direction of decreasing temperature.

For a two-dimensional field:

q̇ = qₓ î + qᵧ ĵ

with

qₓ = −k ∂T/∂x

qᵧ = −k ∂T/∂y

The lecture treated k as a constant material property for the plate. The x- and y-components can be calculated from numerical temperature gradients and displayed as arrows, for example using a quiver plot.

The demonstration used NumPy’s gradient operation to estimate the temperature gradients and then multiplied the gradients by −k to obtain the heat-flux components. The plotted temperature field was reported as having values around 56 °C, with a displayed colour range approximately from 54 °C to 63 °C.

Central differencing

For a uniform grid, the first derivative of temperature with respect to x is approximated using a central difference:

∂T/∂x |ᵢⱼ ≈ (Tᵢ₊₁,ⱼ − Tᵢ₋₁,ⱼ) / (2Δx)

The y-direction equivalent is

∂T/∂y |ᵢⱼ ≈ (Tᵢ,ⱼ₊₁ − Tᵢ,ⱼ₋₁) / (2Δy)

These are first derivatives with second-order accuracy. The truncation error scales as O(Δx²) or O(Δy²), respectively.

For the x derivative:

• The j index remains constant.

• Only the i index changes.

• The temperature difference is taken across two grid spacings, hence the denominator 2Δx.

For the y derivative:

• The i index remains constant.

• Only the j index changes.

• The temperature difference is taken across two grid spacings, hence the denominator 2Δy.

Neumann boundary conditions and ghost nodes

A Neumann boundary condition specifies a derivative or flux at a boundary. For example, a boundary may specify

∂T/∂x = constant

A fully insulated boundary has zero normal temperature gradient:

∇T · n̂ = 0

where n̂ is the outward unit normal to the boundary.

For a rectangular domain, the outward unit normals are:

• Top boundary: n̂ = ĵ

• Bottom boundary: n̂ = −ĵ

• Right boundary: n̂ = î

• Left boundary: n̂ = −î

The transcript states that a left-hand insulated boundary has

∂T/∂x = 0

Ghost nodes are introduced outside the physical domain to apply the central-difference approximation at the boundary. For a node on the left boundary, an artificial node is placed to the left and labelled approximately as T₀ⱼ, with the physical boundary node represented by T₁ⱼ.

The central-difference approximation at x = 0 is

( T₂ⱼ − T₀ⱼ ) / (2Δx) = (∂T/∂x)ₓ₌₀

Rearranging gives

T₀ⱼ = T₂ⱼ − 2Δx (∂T/∂x)ₓ₌₀

For an insulated boundary, the prescribed derivative is zero, so

T₀ⱼ = T₂ⱼ

This ghost-node value is substituted into the general finite-difference equation. The resulting equation incorporates the boundary condition while eliminating the ghost-node unknown from the final system.

The same procedure can be applied to other edges. At a corner, two boundary conditions may need to be incorporated.

Curved and irregular boundaries

Standard finite differences are simplest on structured, mapped grids with uniform spacing. They can also be adapted to curved or irregular boundaries, but the algebra and bookkeeping become more complicated.

For a non-uniform grid, the second derivative in x can be constructed by:

1. Estimating first derivatives at the midpoints between neighbouring nodes.

2. Taking a finite difference of those midpoint gradients.

3. Dividing by the distance between the midpoint locations.

The lecture labels the neighbouring x spacings using factors such as α₁Δx and α₂Δx. The distance between the midpoint locations is represented by γ, approximately

γ = (α₁Δx)/2 + (α₂Δx)/2

The first derivative at one midpoint is estimated from the temperature difference between Tᵢⱼ and Tᵢ₊₁,ⱼ:

(∂T/∂x)ₐ ≈ (Tᵢ₊₁,ⱼ − Tᵢⱼ) / (α₂Δx)

The derivative at the other midpoint is estimated from Tᵢ₋₁,ⱼ and Tᵢⱼ:

(∂T/∂x)ᵦ ≈ (Tᵢⱼ − Tᵢ₋₁,ⱼ) / (α₁Δx)

The second derivative is then approximated by the change in the midpoint gradients divided by γ:

∂²T/∂x² ≈ [ (∂T/∂x)ₐ − (∂T/∂x)ᵦ ] / γ

An analogous construction is used in the y direction. The resulting expressions are substituted into the Laplace equation to obtain a discretised equation for a node near the curved boundary.

The final non-uniform-grid equation is more complicated than the four-neighbour average used for a uniform grid, but the same finite-difference principle still applies.
