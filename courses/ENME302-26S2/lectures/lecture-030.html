Key concepts

• Mesh convergence: numerical results should approach a stable value as the mesh is refined.

• Parameter sweep: varying mesh resolution systematically to assess convergence.

• Non-dimensional variables: variables scaled by characteristic length, time, and temperature scales.

• Characteristic diffusion time:

\[

t_0=\frac{L^2}{\alpha}

\]

• Thermal diffusivity, \(\alpha\), has units of \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\).

• Reynolds number: an example of a dimensionless parameter used to characterise flow behaviour.

• Similarity variable for one-dimensional transient diffusion:

\[

\eta=\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}

\]

• Similarity solution: a solution in which the spatial and temporal dependence is combined into a single variable.

\[

\operatorname{erf}(\eta)

=

\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}

Dimensional consistency

The heat equation was discussed in one spatial dimension in the general form

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}

=

\alpha

\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}

\]

where:

• \(T\) is temperature.

• \(x\) is position.

• \(t\) is time.

• \(\alpha\) is thermal diffusivity, with units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\).

The units of each term must be consistent. The time derivative has units of temperature per second, and the product of \(\alpha\) and the second spatial derivative must have the same units.

Non-dimensionalisation

Dimensionless variables were introduced using characteristic scales:

\[

\tilde{x}=\frac{x}{L}

\]

\[

\tilde{t}=\frac{t}{t_0}

\]

\[

\tilde{T}=\frac{T}{T_0}

\]

where:

• \(L\) is a characteristic length.

• \(t_0\) is a characteristic time.

• \(T_0\) is a characteristic temperature scale.

• Tildes denote dimensionless variables.

The characteristic length might be the length of a rod, the diameter of a cylinder, or the width of a channel, depending on the physical problem.

For the rod example, the lecturer mentioned a length scale of approximately \(80\ \mathrm{cm}\) and a temperature scale of approximately \(100^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Substitution into the heat equation gives

\[

\frac{T_0}{t_0}

\frac{\partial \tilde{T}}{\partial \tilde{t}}

=

\alpha

\frac{T_0}{L^2}

\frac{\partial^2\tilde{T}}{\partial\tilde{x}^2}

\]

After cancelling \(T_0\),

\[

\frac{\partial \tilde{T}}{\partial \tilde{t}}

=

\frac{\alpha t_0}{L^2}

\frac{\partial^2\tilde{T}}{\partial\tilde{x}^2}

\]

Choosing

\[

t_0=\frac{L^2}{\alpha}

\]

removes the remaining dimensional coefficient:

\[

\frac{\partial \tilde{T}}{\partial \tilde{t}}

=

\frac{\partial^2\tilde{T}}{\partial\tilde{x}^2}

\]

This dimensionless equation contains no dimensional parameters.

The lecturer emphasised that the choice of characteristic scales is not always unique. The scales should be selected to represent the important physical dimensions of the problem and to simplify the resulting equation.

Advantages of non-dimensionalisation

Non-dimensionalisation can:

• Reduce the number of independent parameters.

• Reveal the dominant physical mechanisms.

• Allow one dimensionless solution to represent many dimensional problems.

• Make results easier to compare between systems with different sizes or units.

• Reduce problems caused by very large or very small numerical values.

• Improve numerical behaviour by bringing terms closer to comparable orders of magnitude.

The Reynolds number was used as an example from fluid mechanics. Different flows can have similar behaviour when they have similar relevant dimensionless parameters, even if their dimensional scales differ.

Boundary and initial conditions in dimensionless form

Boundary and initial conditions must also be scaled consistently.

For example, a zero-temperature condition remains zero under scaling. An initial condition such as a sine profile can become

\[

\tilde{T}(\tilde{x},0)=\sin(\pi\tilde{x})

\]

for a suitably chosen temperature scale.

The dimensionless and dimensional temperature profiles have the same shape, but the dimensionless profile is normalised. The peak dimensionless temperature is one, and the position is represented using a dimensionless coordinate.

For the example discussed, the dimensionless analytical solution was of the form

\[

\tilde{T}(\tilde{x},\tilde{t})

=

\sin(\pi\tilde{x})

e^{-\pi^2\tilde{t}}

\]

This corresponds to the first spatial mode for a one-dimensional heat equation problem with appropriate boundary and initial conditions.

Similarity solution for a semi-infinite slab

The second analytical method considered a semi-infinite slab occupying

\[

x\geq 0

\]

The slab initially has a uniform temperature \(T_1\). At \(t=0\), the surface at \(x=0\) is suddenly brought to a fixed temperature \(T_\infty\).

The problem is:

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}

=

\alpha

\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}

\]

with boundary conditions

\[

T(0,t)=T_\infty

\]

\[

T(x,t)\rightarrow T_1

\quad\text{as}\quad x\rightarrow\infty

\]

and initial condition

\[

T(x,0)=T_1

\quad\text{for}\quad x>0

\]

There is no fluid flow or convection in this model. Heat transfer occurs by diffusion only.

Immediately after the surface-temperature change, the largest temperature gradient occurs near \(x=0\). As time increases, heat diffuses farther into the slab and the temperature profile changes progressively farther from the surface.

Normalised temperature

The dimensional temperature was normalised using the two relevant temperatures:

\[

\tilde{T}

=

\frac{T-T_\infty}{T_1-T_\infty}

\]

This gives:

• \(\tilde{T}=0\) at the surface temperature \(T_\infty\).

• \(\tilde{T}=1\) at the initial slab temperature \(T_1\).

If \(T_1>T_\infty\), the slab cools from its initial temperature toward the imposed surface temperature. If the ordering is reversed, the same mathematical solution can describe heating, but the temperature difference must be retained with the correct sign.

Similarity variable

Because the domain is semi-infinite, there is no obvious finite characteristic length \(L\). Instead, the spatial coordinate and time are combined into a single dimensionless similarity variable:

\[

\eta

=

\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}

\]

The factor of \(2\) is a mathematical convenience that simplifies the resulting integration.

Dimensional checking gives:

• \(x\) has units of metres.

• \(\alpha t\) has units of \(\mathrm{m^2}\).

• \(\sqrt{\alpha t}\) has units of metres.

Therefore, \(\eta\) is dimensionless.

The similarity variable reflects the diffusive length scale

\[

x_{\text{diffusion}}\sim\sqrt{\alpha t}

\]

As time increases, the region affected by diffusion grows approximately with \(\sqrt{t}\).

Reduction from a PDE to an ODE

The normalised temperature is treated as a function of \(\eta\) only:

\[

\tilde{T}(x,t)=\tilde{T}(\eta)

\]

Applying the chain rule to the heat equation reduces the PDE to an ordinary differential equation. In standard notation, the resulting equation is

\[

\frac{d^2\tilde{T}}{d\eta^2}

+

2\eta

\frac{d\tilde{T}}{d\eta}

=0

\]

This is the main benefit of the similarity substitution: the original problem depends on both \(x\) and \(t\), whereas the transformed problem depends only on \(\eta\).

Let

\[

U=\frac{d\tilde{T}}{d\eta}

\]

Then

\[

\frac{dU}{d\eta}

+

2\eta U=0

\]

or

\[

\frac{1}{U}\frac{dU}{d\eta}

=

-2\eta

\]

Integrating gives

\[

\ln U=-\eta^2+C_1

\]

and hence

\[

U=C e^{-\eta^2}

\]

Integrating again,

\[

\tilde{T}

=

C

\int_0^\eta e^{-z^2}\,dz

+

C_2

\]

where \(z\) is a dummy integration variable.

The surface boundary condition gives

\[

\tilde{T}(0)=0

\]

so \(C_2=0\).

The far-field condition is

\[

\tilde{T}(\infty)=1

\]

Using

\[

\int_0^\infty e^{-z^2}\,dz

=

\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}

\]

gives

\[

C=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}

\]

Therefore,

\[

\tilde{T}(\eta)

=

\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}

\int_0^\eta e^{-z^2}\,dz

\]

This is the Gaussian error function:

\[

\tilde{T}(\eta)=\operatorname{erf}(\eta)

\]

Dimensional temperature solution

Substituting back for \(\tilde{T}\) and \(\eta\),

\[

\frac{T-T_\infty}{T_1-T_\infty}

=

\operatorname{erf}

\left(

\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}

\right)

\]

Equivalently,

\[

T(x,t)

=

T_\infty

+

(T_1-T_\infty)

\operatorname{erf}

\left(

\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}

\right)

\]

This solution satisfies:

• At \(x=0\), \(\operatorname{erf}(0)=0\), so \(T=T_\infty\).

• As \(x\rightarrow\infty\), \(\operatorname{erf}(\eta)\rightarrow1\), so \(T\rightarrow T_1\).

• At a fixed \(x>0\), the temperature changes as time increases because \(\eta\) decreases.

The family of temperature profiles is self-similar: profiles at different times have the same functional form when plotted against the similarity coordinate \(\eta\).

Numerical evaluation of the similarity solution

The lecturer demonstrated two implementation approaches:

1. Nested loops:

• Loop over spatial positions.

• Loop over time values.

• Evaluate the analytical solution at each pair of coordinates.

• Store the results in a two-dimensional temperature array.

2. Array-based evaluation:

• Create arrays for space and time.

• Expand them into compatible two-dimensional arrays.

• Evaluate the similarity solution directly over the arrays.

• Plot the result without explicitly writing nested Python loops.

The analytical solution is valid for all positions and times in its domain. Unlike a finite-difference time-marching method, it does not need to calculate one time step before calculating the next.

Because the physical domain is semi-infinite, a finite upper limit must be selected for plotting. The lecturer used an upper spatial plotting limit of approximately \(x=2\) in the demonstration.

Comparison of analytical and numerical solutions

The lecturer used COMSOL to compare a numerical solution with an analytical solution for a separation-of-variables problem.

The process was:

• Define the analytical solution \(u_A(x,y)\).

• Compare it with the numerical dependent variable \(u(x,y)\).

• Compute the pointwise difference:

\[

u-u_A

\]

• Integrate a squared difference over the domain or surface to obtain an error measure.

The numerical difference was approximately \(10^{-5}\) compared with a solution magnitude of order one in the demonstrated case. Refining the mesh should reduce the difference.

Squaring the difference:

• Prevents positive and negative errors from cancelling.

• Produces a non-negative error measure.

• Penalises larger errors more strongly.

This is analogous to a quadratic or \(L^2\)-type error measure.

Separation of variables with mixed boundary conditions

The final section briefly considered a rod of length \(L\) with:

• A Dirichlet boundary condition on the left:

\[

T(0,t)=0

\]

• A Neumann boundary condition on the right:

\[

\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}=0

\]

The zero-gradient condition corresponds to zero heat flux at the right-hand end because heat flux depends on the temperature gradient.

A general separated solution was written in the form

\[

T(x,t)

=

\left[

A\cos(\lambda x)

+

B\sin(\lambda x)

\right]

e^{-\alpha\lambda^2 t}

\]

Applying the left boundary condition at \(x=0\):

\[

T(0,t)=

A e^{-\alpha\lambda^2t}=0

\]

Since the exponential factor is not zero, this requires

\[

A=0

\]

The remaining solution is therefore proportional to

\[

\sin(\lambda x)e^{-\alpha\lambda^2t}

\]

Applying the zero-gradient condition at \(x=L\):

\[

\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right|_{x=L}=0

\]

gives a condition involving

\[

\cos(\lambda L)=0

\]

Therefore, the admissible eigenvalues satisfy

\[

\lambda L

=

\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi,

\qquad

n=0,1,2,\ldots

\]

or

\[

\lambda_n

=

\frac{(n+\tfrac12)\pi}{L}

\]

The transcript’s spoken examples of the half-integer multiples were unclear, but the cosine-zero condition unambiguously leads to the half-integer eigenvalue sequence above.
