Consistent initialisation and boundary ramps

The direct application of T∞ at x = 0 creates a conflict with the initial condition T = T1 everywhere, including at x = 0. This produced overshoot in the numerical solution, including at the initial time and around approximately 0.5 hours in the demonstration.

One approach was to apply a ramp function to the boundary condition. The ramp:

• Starts at zero.

• Increases smoothly to one over a selected time interval.

• Was demonstrated over approximately the first 0.5 seconds.

• Can be adjusted through its slope, location, cutoff, and smoothing parameters.

The boundary temperature was then constructed so that it initially equalled T1 and gradually transitioned to T∞. This makes the initial boundary value consistent with the initial field and reduces the effect of the initial singularity.

The ramp reduced, but did not entirely eliminate, the overshoot. The instructor noted that the finite ramp itself still causes a small temperature change during the first time interval.

The time-dependent solver also had a consistent-initialisation option. With this enabled, the solver performed a small initial step to determine compatible values. Turning consistent initialisation off was demonstrated as another way to reduce the initial discrepancy. The instructor noted that the difference was small and might not significantly affect an overall cooking-time result, but it could matter when seeking highly accurate results.

Adaptive time stepping and solver tolerance

The solver uses adaptive internal time stepping. The time steps are selected based on the residuals and the prescribed solver tolerance.

The lecture distinguished between:

• Output times: times at which the solution is saved or reported.

• Internal solver time steps: adaptive steps selected during the calculation.

The output times do not necessarily reveal all internal steps. A log-sampling or reporting setting was adjusted so that intermediate time steps could be inspected.

The reciprocal of the step size was shown in a convergence or solver log. The initial interpretation of the plotted values was corrected during the demonstration: the quantity represented reciprocal step size, so a larger value corresponded to a smaller time step.

Reducing the tolerance, with 10^-5 given as an example, caused the initial time step to become much smaller, approximately 0.02 seconds in the demonstration. This improved the solver’s ability to resolve the initial transient.

The instructor emphasised that time-step control is an important source of numerical accuracy, in addition to spatial mesh resolution.

Temperature-dependent conductivity

When thermal conductivity κ varies with temperature, the value at a midpoint between two nodes should be evaluated using an appropriate midpoint temperature.

The simplest method discussed was:

1. Take the temperatures at neighbouring nodes i and i + 1.

2. Average those temperatures.

3. Evaluate κ at the average temperature.

This is equivalent to a linear interpolation of temperature at the midpoint. Evaluating κ separately at the two nodes and averaging the conductivity values is another possible approach, but because κ is nonlinear in temperature, it can produce a slightly different result.

Hyperbolic PDEs and the wave equation

The second part of the lecture introduced hyperbolic wave equations. Examples mentioned included:

• Vibrations of a string.

• Guitar or violin strings.

• Ocean waves.

• Tsunami modelling through shallow-water equations.

The instructor noted that undamped wave equations can be challenging numerically because waves continue propagating indefinitely and do not naturally decay.

The model considered a vibrating string with:

• Horizontal coordinate x.

• Time t.

• Vertical displacement u(x,t).

• Constant tension in magnitude along the string.

• Tension forces tangent to the string.

• A small string element between x and x + Δx.

Horizontal force balance

The tension forces at the two ends of the element were resolved into horizontal and vertical components.

Because the string element has no net horizontal acceleration, the horizontal components balance. This leads to the conclusion that the horizontal component of the tension is constant along the string.

The tension magnitude may have different directions at the two ends, but its horizontal component is the same. This constant was subsequently denoted by T.

Vertical force balance

The vertical force balance was written using the difference between the vertical components of the tensions:

T2 sin β − T1 sin α = m ∂²u/∂t²

The mass of the small element was represented as the linear density multiplied by its length:

m = ρ Δx

where ρ is the mass per unit length of the string.

Using the horizontal force balance, the vertical tension components were divided by the constant horizontal component. The trigonometric ratios were then related to the local slopes of the string:

• tan α corresponds to ∂u/∂x evaluated near x.

• tan β corresponds to ∂u/∂x evaluated near x + Δx.

As Δx tends toward zero, the difference between the two slopes becomes a second spatial derivative. The resulting continuous equation is the one-dimensional wave equation.

Wave-equation model

The resulting wave equation was given in the form:

∂²u/∂t² = c² ∂²u/∂x²

where the wave-speed parameter was defined as:

c² = T/ρ

The automated transcript captures the symbols inconsistently in places, but the physical meaning is clear: wave speed increases with tension and decreases with linear mass density.

The equation is:

• Second order in time.

• Second order in space.

• Hyperbolic.

For a string of length L with fixed ends, typical boundary conditions are:

u(0,t) = 0

u(L,t) = 0

Two initial conditions are required:

u(x,0) = f(x)

∂u/∂t (x,0) = g(x)

Here:

• f(x) is the initial displacement profile.

• g(x) is the initial velocity profile.

The instructor described a standing-start example in which the initial velocity was zero and the initial displacement was triangular and piecewise linear.

Separation of variables

The assumed solution form was:

u(x,t) = F(x)G(t)

Substitution into the wave equation gives separate spatial and temporal factors. After division by the product of the functions and by c², one side depends only on x and the other only on t. Since the equality must hold for every x and t, both sides must equal a constant.

The separation constant was denoted by α in the lecture. The cases α < 0 and α > 0 lead to different families of ordinary differential equations and general solutions. The instructor indicated that the case producing sinusoidal spatial modes is generally the practical one for a string with fixed ends.

Applying the boundary conditions:

• The condition at x = 0 removes one integration constant.

• The condition at x = L restricts the allowable values of the separation parameter.

• These allowable values produce discrete modes indexed by an integer n.

Applying the zero-initial-velocity condition removes the temporal sine terms in the example.

The remaining solution is a sum of spatial sine modes multiplied by temporal cosine terms. The coefficients are determined by representing the initial displacement f(x) as a Fourier sine series.

The exact coefficient expressions and numbering of the equations were not fully captured in the transcript, but the method is:

1. Obtain the separated spatial and temporal solutions.

2. Apply the two fixed-end boundary conditions.

3. Apply the initial velocity condition.

4. Expand the initial displacement as a Fourier sine series.

5. Combine all modes to form the complete solution.

• t is time.

Ramped boundary condition

A ramp function R(t) was described as changing from 0 to 1 over a finite time interval. A boundary temperature can therefore be represented conceptually as:

Tboundary(t) = T1 + R(t)(T∞ − T1)

with:

• R(t) = 0 initially.

• R(t) increasing smoothly over the selected ramp interval.

• R(t) approaching 1 after the ramp.

Wave equation

The one-dimensional wave equation is:

∂²u/∂t² = c² ∂²u/∂x²

with:

c² = T/ρ

where:

• u(x,t) is transverse displacement.

• c is wave speed.

• T is the string tension or constant horizontal tension component.

• ρ is the linear mass density of the string.

String boundary conditions

For a string fixed at both ends:

u(0,t) = 0

u(L,t) = 0

Initial conditions

The general initial conditions are:

u(x,0) = f(x)

∂u/∂t (x,0) = g(x)

For the example in the lecture:

• f(x) was a triangular, piecewise-linear displacement profile.

• g(x) = 0, representing a string released from rest.

The separation-of-variables assumption was:

u(x,t) = F(x)G(t)

The separated solution for fixed ends consists of sinusoidal spatial modes and corresponding temporal oscillations. The full solution is represented as a Fourier sine series:

Midpoint conductivity evaluation
