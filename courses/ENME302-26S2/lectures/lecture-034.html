Consistency

The latter part of the lecture introduced numerical consistency using the heat equation.

A numerical scheme is consistent if its truncation error tends to zero as the spatial and temporal step sizes are reduced appropriately.

The lecture discussed the DuFort–Frankel scheme. The transcript indicates that the truncation error contains a term scaling with:

\[

\frac{\Delta t^2}{\Delta x^2}

\]

If \(\Delta t/\Delta x\) is held at a nonzero constant, this ratio does not vanish as the grid is refined. The resulting limiting equation therefore contains an additional term involving a second time derivative.

The lecturer’s conclusion was that under this refinement path the scheme may become consistent with a different PDE rather than with the heat equation itself. This illustrates why the relationship between \(\Delta t\) and \(\Delta x\) matters when assessing consistency.

Stability

A numerical scheme is stable when solution errors remain bounded over all time steps.

The lecture used a simple one-degree-of-freedom heat-equation problem to provide an intuitive stability analysis. The boundary temperatures were fixed at zero, with a single unknown temperature at the midpoint.

For the FTCS scheme, the update relation was reduced using:

\[

\lambda = \frac{\alpha\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}

\]

The coefficient multiplying the current midpoint temperature becomes:

\[

1-2\lambda

\]

To prevent the next temperature from becoming negative when the current temperature is positive, this coefficient must satisfy:

\[

1-2\lambda \geq 0

\]

Therefore:

\[

\lambda \leq \frac{1}{2}

\]

The lecture demonstrated three behaviours:

• For \(\lambda < 0.5\), the temperature decays towards zero as expected.

• For \(\lambda = 0.5\), the temperature collapses to zero immediately in the simplified example.

• For \(\lambda = 1\), the coefficient becomes \(-1\), causing the solution to alternate sign and grow in magnitude. This is an unstable oscillation.

The lecturer distinguished between stability and physically realistic behaviour. At \(\lambda=0.5\), the scheme is stable in the sense that errors are not amplified, although the simplified result is not a useful gradual decay.

Von Neumann stability analysis was mentioned but deferred to a later lecture.

Equations and definitions

Transient heat equation

The governing equation was described as the heat equation for a solid with density, specific heat capacity, and temperature-dependent thermal conductivity. In general form:

\[

\rho c_p\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}

=

\nabla\cdot\left(\kappa(T)\nabla T\right)

\]

where:

• \(T\) is temperature.

• \(t\) is time.

• \(\rho\) is mass density.

• \(c_p\) is specific heat capacity.

• \(\kappa(T)\) is thermal conductivity as a function of temperature.

If \(\kappa\) is constant, the equation reduces to the usual linear heat equation. If \(\kappa=\kappa(T)\), the equation is nonlinear.

Thermal diffusivity

The thermal diffusivity is commonly defined as:

\[

\alpha=\frac{\kappa}{\rho c_p}

\]

The transcript uses \(\alpha\) in the FTCS stability analysis. Because the assignment uses temperature-dependent conductivity, \(\alpha\) may also vary with temperature unless a constant approximation is used.

The bottom patty surface is initially assigned:

\[

T=210\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}

This is a Dirichlet boundary condition.

Convective Robin boundary condition

The exposed surfaces use a convection condition of the general form:

\[

-\kappa\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}

=

h(T_s-T_\infty)

\]

where:

• \(n\) is the outward normal direction.

• \(T_s\) is the surface temperature.

• \(T_\infty\) is the surrounding-air temperature.

• \(h\) is the convective heat-transfer coefficient.

The exact sign depends on the normal and heat-flux convention used in the implementation.

For this problem:

\[

h=25\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}

\]

\[

T_\infty=20\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}

\]

Symmetry boundary condition

At a symmetry plane, the normal temperature gradient is zero:

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}=0

\]

This is a Neumann boundary condition.

FTCS stability parameter

For the simplified 1D heat equation:

\[

\lambda=\frac{\alpha\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}

\]

The stability condition derived in the lecture was:

\[

\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}

\]

Equivalently:

\[

\Delta t\leq\frac{\Delta x^2}{2\alpha}

\]

The latter rearrangement is a direct consequence of the stated condition; the lecture explicitly emphasised the condition in terms of \(\lambda\).

Simplified FTCS update

For a 1D heat equation with uniform spacing, the standard FTCS update is:

\[

T_i^{n+1}

=

T_i^n

+

\lambda

\left(

T_{i+1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i-1}^n

\right)

\]

For the one-interior-node example with zero-temperature boundaries:

\[

T_2^{n+1}

=

(1-2\lambda)T_2^n

\]

This was the relation used to derive the intuitive stability restriction.
