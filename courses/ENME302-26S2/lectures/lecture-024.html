Detailed notes

Structured and unstructured meshes

A structured mesh is defined by an organised set of rows and columns. In two dimensions, the neighbours of node \((i,j)\) are identified through index changes:

• Right: \((i+1,j)\)

• Left: \((i-1,j)\)

• Above: \((i,j+1)\)

• Below: \((i,j-1)\)

Advantages of structured meshes:

• Straightforward generation when the geometry is compatible with the topology.

• Low storage requirements for neighbour connectivity because the topology is known by definition.

• Typically well-behaved algebraic systems.

• Often better convergence behaviour than comparable unstructured meshes.

Disadvantages:

• Difficult to fit efficiently around curved boundaries or highly irregular domains.

• A simple mapping of a rectangular structured grid onto a curved geometry can create highly skewed elements, especially near corners.

• Poorly shaped elements can reduce solution accuracy and cause convergence problems.

An unstructured mesh has no regular row-and-column arrangement. The elements may still have similar sizes and shapes, but the connectivity is not implicit. The neighbouring nodes or elements must therefore be stored in a data structure.

Advantages:

• Can be distributed through arbitrary geometries.

• More suitable when a domain cannot be decomposed into structured regions.

Disadvantages:

• More complicated connectivity and neighbour storage.

• Neighbour searches and data handling can be more expensive.

• The lecturer recommended using a structured grid when practical, with an unstructured grid used when a structured grid cannot be created effectively.

O–H grids and geometry decomposition

A circular or curved domain is difficult to represent with one simple structured grid. Directly mapping a rectangular grid to the circle can result in highly skewed elements near the mapped vertices.

An alternative is to partition the geometry into multiple regions:

• A central square or rectangular region can receive a structured mesh.

• The surrounding curved region can be divided into four additional regions.

• Each of the five regions can then be meshed as a mapped structured grid.

This produces an O–H grid:

• The central region has an H-type structured topology.

• The surrounding regions form the O-type outer topology.

The geometry can be created by sketching and partitioning the domain. Although the geometry appears more complicated, the individual regions become easier to mesh.

Local refinement and matching interfaces

Mesh spacing can be varied spatially to resolve regions with large solution gradients.

For example, in a fluid-flow problem:

• A no-slip wall produces a strong velocity gradient near the wall.

• Smaller element spacing in the wall-normal direction helps resolve the boundary layer.

• Inflation layers can be used near walls to provide the required refinement.

When adjacent structured regions are connected, their interface faces should match. If two regions have non-matching cell interfaces, interpolation is required to transfer information between the regions.

Partitioning and swept meshes

An irregular body, such as the snake-like example mentioned in the lecture, may be difficult to mesh directly with a structured method.

Possible strategies include:

• Partitioning the body into simpler regions while retaining it as one physical body.

• Splitting the geometry along its centreline.

• Creating a mesh on a cross-section and sweeping it through the domain.

Although a partitioned body may contain multiple mesh regions, it can still represent one physical domain when the governing physics are solved.

Mesh refinement and convergence

Increasing the number of elements generally improves the approximation, but it also increases computational cost.

In the wrench example, mesh resolution was increased progressively. A quantity such as peak stress or stress concentration was monitored. As the mesh was refined:

• The result changed between successive meshes.

• The changes became smaller.

• The solution approached mesh convergence.

The important quantity is generally the change between successive mesh resolutions, not necessarily the absolute value from one mesh. The converged numerical solution must still be validated against experimental or analytical results.

A practical convergence study should:

1. Select a quantity of interest.

2. Run the model on an initial mesh.

3. Refine the mesh systematically.

4. Record the quantity of interest for each mesh.

5. Compare the changes between successive meshes.

6. Continue until the change is below a problem-appropriate tolerance.

Possible quantities of interest include:

• Maximum displacement of a beam.

• Peak stress in a wrench.

• Temperature at a specified location.

• A force, flux, or other output relevant to the design question.

The mesh should be refined where large gradients, stress concentrations, boundary layers, or other important local behaviour are expected. There is little value in refining an irrelevant region while leaving the quantity of interest poorly resolved.

Adaptive mesh refinement can automate this process by refining regions based on estimated error or gradients. The lecturer noted that adaptive refinement was not covered in detail in this course and that manually refining a mesh may be sufficient for the smaller two-dimensional problems used in class.

Consequently, transient simulations can become particularly expensive because more spatial steps and more time steps may be required.

Total numerical error can be considered as the combination of:

• Discretisation error, which generally increases for coarser meshes.

• Round-off error, which can increase when the mesh is made extremely fine because many more operations are performed.

Therefore, the smallest element size is not automatically the mesh with the smallest total error. In normal engineering use with double-precision arithmetic, the round-off-dominated region may not be reached, but it remains an important numerical-analysis concept.

Algebraic grid generation

Structured-grid generation requires a mapping between:

• Physical coordinates, denoted approximately by \(X\) and \(Y\).

• Logical or computational coordinates, denoted in the transcript by terms that sound like \(\xi\) and \(\eta\).

The logical coordinates are discretised, typically over integer indices from 0 to \(N\) and 0 to \(M\). They represent an orderly computational rectangle, while the mapping sends those points into the physical geometry.

For algebraic grid generation:

1. The four boundaries of the physical domain are specified.

2. Nodes are distributed along each boundary.

3. Interior nodes are calculated from the boundary coordinates.

4. The resulting points form the grid.

The interior coordinates are obtained by interpolating between the four boundaries. This method is also referred to in the lecture as transfinite interpolation.

Advantages:

• Direct and deterministic.

• Easy to implement.

• Low computational cost.

• Interior points are calculated directly from boundary points.

Disadvantages:

• Not suitable for every geometry.

• Boundary irregularities can propagate through the entire domain.

• Interior elements may become skewed, particularly near corners or strongly curved boundaries.

Elliptic grid generation

Elliptic grid generation uses additional partial differential equations, described as Laplace-type equations, to distribute the interior nodes.

The process involves:

• Prescribing the boundary locations.

• Solving equations for the mapping between logical and physical coordinates.

• Applying the chain rule to express the equations in the required coordinate system.

• Iteratively solving the resulting equations.

Compared with algebraic grid generation, elliptic grid generation produces grids that are more diffuse, smoother, and better distributed through the domain.

Advantages:

• Smoother interior grids.

• Better distribution of grid lines.

• Less direct propagation of boundary irregularities.

Disadvantages:

• More computationally expensive.

• Requires the solution of additional PDEs.

• The governing equations are nonlinear according to the lecture and require an iterative solution process.

Unstructured-grid generation

Two unstructured-grid methods were introduced briefly:

• Advancing-front generation.

• Delaunay triangulation.

#### Advancing-front method

The advancing-front method begins with the boundary edges:

1. Boundary edges are identified, numbered, and stored in a front vector.

2. The nearest suitable interior node to a front edge is located.

3. A new triangular element is formed.

4. The used front edge is removed or replaced with new front edges.

5. The process is repeated until the domain is filled.

The method can create reasonably good meshes for arbitrary geometries. Its computational cost increases because the algorithm repeatedly searches for suitable nearby nodes or edges. This may become significant for large three-dimensional meshes.

The construction is inherently sequential in the form presented. For parallel computation, the lecturer suggested decomposing the domain and applying the method to subdomains before combining the results.

#### Delaunay triangulation

Delaunay triangulation uses a geometric criterion based on circumcircles in two dimensions or circumspheres in three dimensions.

For a triangle:

• A unique circumcircle can be defined through its three vertices.

• The Delaunay condition requires that the circumcircle contain no other mesh vertex in its interior.

• If another vertex lies inside the circumcircle, the triangulation does not satisfy the Delaunay criterion.

The method helps produce a well-distributed mesh and controls changes in element size between neighbouring elements.

Finite-difference formulation

The lecture then introduced finite differences as an alternative to analytical solutions of PDEs.

The computational domain is discretised into a grid of nodes. The dependent variable is assigned a value at each node, such as:

\[

T_{i,j}

\]

for temperature at node \((i,j)\).

Neighbour indexing follows the convention:

• Right: \(i+1\)

• Left: \(i-1\)

• Up: \(j+1\)

• Down: \(j-1\)

Derivatives in the governing PDE are approximated using differences between neighbouring nodal values. The result is an algebraic system containing the unknown values at the grid nodes.

The discrete solution must satisfy:

• The governing PDE at the interior grid points.

• The specified boundary conditions at the boundary grid points.

For a linear PDE, the resulting algebraic equations are linear. The course focuses on linear PDEs, including elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic classifications, and does not develop nonlinear PDEs in detail.
