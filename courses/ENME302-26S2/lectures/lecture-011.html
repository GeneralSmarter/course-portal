Overview

This lecture introduces the purpose and scope of finite element analysis (FEA) for ENME302. The central idea is to extend familiar mechanics and materials methods into a computational framework capable of analysing structures that are statically indeterminate, geometrically complex, or difficult to solve analytically.

The lecture:

• Connects ENME302 to prior mechanics and materials study, particularly ENME202 or an equivalent course.

• Contrasts statically determinate and statically indeterminate structures.

• Explains why relative stiffness is needed to solve overconstrained structures.

• Introduces the finite element method as a way of dividing a complex structure into simpler elements.

• Emphasises one-dimensional elements as the starting point for the first four weeks.

• Previews axial truss elements for Weeks 1–2 and flexural beam elements for Weeks 3–4.

• Introduces nodes, degrees of freedom, boundary conditions, loading, and shape functions.

• Demonstrates the link between classical beam analysis and finite element modelling.

• Places FEA within a broader engineering context, including earthquake engineering, biomechanics, buckling, and PDE-based analysis.

The lecturer also described research applications involving structural damping, rocking walls, earthquake shake-table testing, hip-replacement implant sensing, and finite element buckling analysis.

Key concepts

• Finite element analysis: A computational method that represents a complex continuous structure using a collection of simpler finite elements.

• Element: A simplified component of a structure that can be modelled using known mechanics.

• Node: A discrete point where elements meet or where degrees of freedom and loads are defined.

• Shape function: A mathematical function used to interpolate displacement, strain, or other quantities between nodal values.

• Statically determinate structure: A structure whose reactions and internal forces can be found using equilibrium equations alone.

• Statically indeterminate structure: A structure with more unknown reactions or internal forces than can be determined from equilibrium alone.

• Relative stiffness: The stiffness distribution among members that determines how load is shared in a statically indeterminate structure.

• Axial truss element: A one-dimensional element carrying only axial tension or compression.

• Flexural beam element: An element capable of representing bending, shear, and associated rotations.

• Boundary condition: A constraint specifying permitted or prevented movement at a location.

• Degree of freedom: An independent displacement or rotation variable used to describe the state of a node.

• Global structure: The complete system assembled from individual elements.

• Local element model: The simplified mathematical description of one element before assembly into the complete structure.

• Mesh: The collection of elements used to discretise a continuous structure.

• Eigenvalue buckling analysis: An analysis used to estimate critical buckling loads and corresponding buckling modes.

• Research–teaching nexus: The use of research experience and findings to inform engineering teaching.

Statically determinate structures

The lecturer began with a simple pin-connected truss made from aluminium tubes. The stated tube dimensions were:

• Outside diameter: 42 mm.

• Wall thickness: 3.5 mm.

For a pin-connected truss:

• Loads are applied at nodal points.

• Members carry axial forces only.

• Members do not carry shear forces or bending moments.

• Reactions and member forces can be found using equilibrium.

• Normal stress follows from the axial member force divided by cross-sectional area.

The original truss was described as statically determinate. This means it has the minimum amount of constraint required for stability. Removing an essential support would make the structure unstable.

For such a structure, the internal member forces can be found from statics alone. The stiffness and material properties of the individual members are not required to determine the equilibrium forces, although they are needed later to determine deformation and stress-related responses.

Statically indeterminate structures

Adding an extra member or constraint makes the truss statically indeterminate. The structure becomes overconstrained, meaning that it contains more constraints than the minimum required for stability.

The additional restraint may improve safety and redundancy, but it also creates additional unknown forces. Equilibrium equations alone are insufficient to determine how the loads are distributed.

The load carried by each member depends on relative stiffness. Stiffness is affected by factors such as:

• Young’s modulus, \(E\).

• Cross-sectional area, \(A\).

• Element length, \(L\).

• Orientation.

• Boundary conditions.

• For bending, the second moment of area, \(I\).

The lecturer noted that statically indeterminate problems can become difficult to solve manually when members are inclined, when many elements are present, or when the connections are rigid.

Rigidly connected structures

If elements are welded rather than pin-connected:

• Connections can transmit moments.

• Elements can carry axial force, shear force, and bending moment.

• Rotations at rigid joints are constrained to be compatible.

• Distributed loads may act along members.

• The structure becomes substantially more difficult to analyse manually.

For a rigidly connected structure, the overall deflection may include:

• Axial deformation.

• Bending deformation.

• Rotation of an internal node.

• Bending of connected members.

• Interaction between the deformation of multiple elements.

This is a typical situation where a finite element framework is useful.

One-dimensional elements as the starting point

Although FEA can use three-dimensional solid elements, the course begins with one-dimensional elements.

A one-dimensional element:

• Is represented along its length.

• Initially models stretching or compression.

• Can be assembled in different orientations.

• Can form two-dimensional or three-dimensional structures when multiple elements are connected.

• Provides the mathematical foundation for more advanced element types.

The lecturer described this as both:

1. A stepping stone toward two-dimensional and three-dimensional FEA.

2. A powerful modelling approach in its own right.

One-dimensional structural models are often sufficiently accurate for high-level structural analysis and are much less computationally expensive than modelling an entire structure using three-dimensional solid elements.

Discretisation and shape functions

A continuous structure contains infinitely many possible points at which displacement, strain, temperature, or stress could be evaluated. FEA replaces this continuous problem with a finite number of discrete nodes and elements.

The mathematical model must ensure that the discrete nodal values provide a useful representation of the continuous system.

Shape functions are used to interpolate a field quantity between nodes. For example, if nodal displacements are known, shape functions can estimate the displacement at an interior location. Differentiating the interpolated displacement field can then provide strain.

Shape functions are a core topic for the following four weeks. They allow the finite element formulation to:

• Represent displacement within an element.

• Relate internal behaviour to nodal degrees of freedom.

• Construct element equations.

• Assemble individual element equations into a global system.

• Estimate quantities between nodes.

Motivation for the finite element framework

A simple cantilever can be solved analytically. A structure made from several rigidly connected elements is much more difficult because the solution must account for:

• Multiple element orientations.

• Axial and bending stiffness.

• Joint compatibility.

• Reaction forces.

• Internal moments and shear forces.

• Coupled nodal translations and rotations.

• Different boundary conditions.

The finite element method addresses this by:

1. Dividing the structure into simpler elements.

2. Developing a mathematical model for each element.

3. Defining the degrees of freedom at the nodes.

4. Relating element forces to nodal displacements.

5. Transforming element quantities where necessary according to orientation.

6. Assembling all element equations into a global system.

7. Applying loads and boundary conditions.

8. Solving for unknown nodal displacements and reactions.

9. Recovering element forces, strains, stresses, and other quantities.

The lecture did not derive the complete matrix formulation, but it established the need for this procedure.

Variable cross-sections and buckling

The lecturer described a damping-device shaft with a varying cross-section and spherical-plane bearings at its ends.

The boundary conditions could reasonably be modelled as pinned in both directions, but the changing cross-section prevents the use of a simple closed-form buckling equation based on a constant cross-section.

A solid finite element model can be:

• Created from the geometry.

• Divided into a coarse or fine mesh.

• Analysed using linear eigenvalue buckling methods.

• Used to estimate the load level at which buckling occurs.

• Used to identify the corresponding buckling mode.

The example demonstrates that traditional analytical equations remain useful but do not cover every realistic geometry or boundary condition. FEA can fill this gap when the geometry is too complicated for a simple analytical solution.

Equations and definitions

Axial normal stress

For a member carrying an axial force \(N\):

\[

\sigma = \frac{N}{A}

\]

where:

• \(\sigma\) is normal stress.

• \(N\) is axial force.

• \(A\) is cross-sectional area.

For a circular hollow tube with outside diameter \(D_o\) and wall thickness \(t\):

\[

D_i = D_o - 2t

\]

\[

A = \frac{\pi}{4}\left(D_o^2-D_i^2\right)

\]

Cantilever bending moment

For a cantilever of length \(L\), with a transverse tip load \(P\), and \(x\) measured from the fixed support:

\[

M(x)=-P(L-x)

\]

Equivalent form:

\[

M(x)=-PL+Px

\]

At the fixed end:

\[

M(0)=-PL

\]

At the free tip:

\[

M(L)=0

\]

Beam curvature relation

The classical Euler–Bernoulli beam relationship is:

\[

EI\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}=M(x)

\]

The exact sign depends on the adopted displacement and moment conventions.

where:

• \(E\) is Young’s modulus.

• \(I\) is the second moment of area.

• \(v(x)\) is transverse deflection.

• \(M(x)\) is bending moment.

Tip-loaded cantilever results

For a constant-\(EI\) cantilever with a transverse tip load \(P\), the standard magnitudes are:

\[

\delta_{\text{tip}}=\frac{PL^3}{3EI}

\]

\[

\theta_{\text{tip}}=\frac{PL^2}{2EI}

\]

Stiffness concepts

For an axial member, the characteristic axial stiffness is:

\[

k=\frac{EA}{L}

\]

This expression was not explicitly stated in the transcript, but it represents the relative-stiffness concept discussed for statically indeterminate structures.

For flexural behaviour, stiffness depends on \(EI\), where \(I\) is the second moment of area.

Definitions

• Statically determinate: All reactions and internal forces can be obtained from equilibrium equations alone.

• Statically indeterminate: Additional compatibility and constitutive relationships are required because equilibrium alone does not provide enough equations.

• Nodal point: A discrete location at which elements connect and where loads, displacements, or other degrees of freedom may be defined.

• Degree of freedom: An independent nodal displacement or rotation.

• Boundary condition: A specified constraint or prescribed value applied to a degree of freedom.

• Shape function: A function that interpolates a field variable between nodal values.

• Mesh: A discretised representation of a continuous domain using finite elements.

• Axial element: An element carrying only axial tension or compression.

• Flexural element: An element that represents bending and may also include shear and rotational behaviour.

• Eigenvalue buckling analysis: A linearised analysis that estimates critical buckling loads and buckling modes.
