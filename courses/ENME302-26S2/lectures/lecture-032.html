The central numerical method introduced was Crank–Nicolson: a midpoint-in-time method that averages the spatial finite-difference expression at time levels \(n\) and \(n+1\). It is second-order accurate in time and requires the solution of a simultaneous system of equations at every time step.

Key concepts

• The one-dimensional heat equation is

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}

=

\alpha \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2},

\]

where \(\alpha\) is the thermal diffusivity.

• The explicit forward-in-time, central-in-space scheme uses only temperatures from the previous time level. It is simple to implement but conditionally stable.

• The implicit backward-in-time, central-in-space scheme uses temperatures at the new time level. It requires a simultaneous system of equations but was described as unconditionally stable.

• Crank–Nicolson averages the central spatial-difference expression between time levels \(n\) and \(n+1\).

• The explicit and implicit schemes discussed were first-order accurate in time.

• Crank–Nicolson is second-order accurate in time and second-order accurate in space.

• The dimensionless diffusion parameter is commonly defined as

\[

\lambda=\frac{\alpha \Delta t}{\Delta x^2}.

\]

• For a mass-diffusion problem, the corresponding dimensionless parameter is

\[

\lambda=\frac{D\Delta t}{\Delta x^2},

\]

where \(D\) is the mass-diffusion coefficient.

• For constant \(\Delta t\), \(\Delta x\), and diffusivity, the coefficient matrix in an implicit or Crank–Nicolson solution does not change between time steps. Only the right-hand-side vector is updated.

• Dirichlet boundary conditions prescribe the field value directly, such as a fixed temperature or concentration.

• Heat diffusion and mass diffusion have the same mathematical form. Temperature is replaced by concentration, and thermal diffusivity \(\alpha\) is replaced by mass diffusivity \(D\).

• Non-dimensionalisation can remove dimensional parameters from the governing diffusion equation, allowing one dimensionless solution to represent a family of dimensional problems after re-dimensionalisation.

Detailed notes

Review of explicit and implicit schemes

The explicit method evaluates the temperature profile using values from the previous time level. Each new temperature can therefore be calculated directly without solving a coupled system.

The implicit method evaluates the spatial derivatives using values at the next time level. The unknown temperatures are coupled, so the equations must be solved simultaneously. This makes the method more complex, but it avoids the conditional stability restriction associated with the explicit scheme.

The lecturer summarised the comparison as follows:

• Explicit forward-time, central-space:

• Easy to implement.

• No simultaneous matrix solve is required.

• Conditionally stable.

• Requires sufficiently small time steps relative to the spatial grid size and diffusivity.

• Implicit backward-time, central-space:

• Requires a simultaneous system of equations.

• More computationally involved.

• Unconditionally stable according to the lecture.

• Can still be inaccurate when the time step is large.

• Crank–Nicolson:

• Combines the explicit and implicit spatial evaluations.

• Uses the midpoint between two time levels.

• Requires a simultaneous system of equations.

• Second-order accurate in time.

• Was shown to converge more rapidly in time-step refinement than the implicit first-order method.

Crank–Nicolson discretisation

The spatial second derivative is evaluated at the midpoint time level \(t_{n+1/2}\). The midpoint value is approximated by averaging the spatial central-difference expressions at time levels \(n\) and \(n+1\):

\[

\left.\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}\right|_{i,n+1/2}

\approx

\frac{1}{2}

\left[

\frac{T_{i-1}^{n}-2T_i^{n}+T_{i+1}^{n}}{\Delta x^2}

+

\frac{T_{i-1}^{n+1}-2T_i^{n+1}+T_{i+1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x^2}

\right].

\]

The time derivative is approximated using the values at the beginning and end of the time step:

\[

\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}\right|_{i,n+1/2}

\approx

\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}.

\]

Although this is a forward difference over one time interval, it represents the change between the beginning and end of the interval and is associated with the midpoint formulation.

Substitution into the heat equation produces six discrete points in the local space-time stencil:

• Three spatial nodes at time level \(n\).

• The same three spatial nodes at time level \(n+1\).

The three spatial nodes are \(i-1\), \(i\), and \(i+1\).

Rearrangement into matrix form

After substitution, all unknown values at time \(n+1\) are placed on the left-hand side. Known values from time level \(n\) are placed on the right-hand side.

Using

\[

\lambda=\frac{\alpha\Delta t}{\Delta x^2},

\]

the Crank–Nicolson equation for an interior node can be written as

\[

-\lambda T_{i-1}^{n+1}

+

2(1+\lambda)T_i^{n+1}

-

\lambda T_{i+1}^{n+1}

=

\lambda T_{i-1}^{n}

+

2(1-\lambda)T_i^{n}

+

\lambda T_{i+1}^{n}.

\]

The exact coefficient convention depends on whether the equation has been multiplied by a factor of two. The lecture used the coefficient form \(2(1+\lambda)\) on the new-time-level diagonal and \(2(1-\lambda)\) on the old-time-level central term.

For a finite domain, the equations for all interior nodes form a matrix system:

\[

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}^{n+1}=\mathbf{B}^{n}.

\]

Here:

• \(\mathbf{A}\) is the coefficient matrix containing the unknown temperatures at time \(n+1\).

• \(\mathbf{T}^{n+1}\) is the vector of unknown temperatures at the new time level.

• \(\mathbf{B}^{n}\) is the right-hand-side vector containing known temperatures from time level \(n\) and contributions from boundary conditions.

For fixed grid spacing, time step, and diffusivity, \(\mathbf{A}\) remains unchanged between time steps. The right-hand side changes because it depends on the current temperature field.

The lecturer noted that the system can be solved using a direct linear solve or a matrix factorisation approach. Forming an explicit inverse matrix at every time step is unnecessary and computationally inefficient, especially for larger systems.

\]

This has the same mathematical structure as the heat equation. The substitutions are:

\[

T \rightarrow C,

\]

The concentration field varies with both position and time.

Non-dimensionalisation

The lecturer introduced dimensionless variables using the characteristic length \(L\), the diffusion time scale \(L^2/D\), and the concentration range from \(C_0\) to \(C_s\).

The dimensionless variables can be defined as

\[

\tilde{x}=\frac{x}{L},

\]

\[

\tilde{t}=\frac{Dt}{L^2},

\]

and

\[

\tilde{C}=\frac{C-C_0}{C_s-C_0}.

\]

Rearranging the concentration definition gives

\[

C=C_0+(C_s-C_0)\tilde{C}.

\]

Applying the chain rule to the time derivative gives

\[

\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}

=

(C_s-C_0)\frac{D}{L^2}

\frac{\partial \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{t}}.

\]

For the spatial derivatives,

\[

\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}

=

\frac{C_s-C_0}{L}

\frac{\partial \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{x}},

\]

and

\[

\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}

=

\frac{C_s-C_0}{L^2}

\frac{\partial^2 \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{x}^2}.

\]

Substitution into the diffusion equation gives

\[

(C_s-C_0)\frac{D}{L^2}

\frac{\partial \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{t}}

=

D

\frac{C_s-C_0}{L^2}

\frac{\partial^2 \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{x}^2}.

\]

The common dimensional factors cancel, leaving

\[

\frac{\partial \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{t}}

=

\frac{\partial^2 \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{x}^2}.

\]

The dimensionless diffusion coefficient \(D\) no longer appears explicitly. A single solution of this dimensionless equation can therefore be applied to systems with different physical diffusivities by changing the dimensional time scale during re-dimensionalisation.

Non-dimensional boundary and initial conditions

With \(x=0\) corresponding to the surface maintained at \(C_s\),

\[

C(0,t)=C_s.

\]

Using the dimensionless concentration definition,

\[

\tilde{C}(0,\tilde{t})

=

\frac{C_s-C_0}{C_s-C_0}

=

1.

\]

At \(x=L\), where the concentration is maintained at \(C_0\),

\[

\]

so

\[

\tilde{x}=1,

\qquad

\tilde{C}(1,\tilde{t})=0.

\]

The initial concentration is uniform at \(C_0\):

\[

C(x,0)=C_0.

\]

Therefore,

\[

\tilde{C}(\tilde{x},0)=0.

\]

The dimensionless concentration varies from \(0\) to \(1\) across the domain.

Discretisation of the dimensionless diffusion equation

The next exercise used the backward-in-time, central-in-space scheme, which is the implicit method.

The time derivative at fixed spatial index \(i\) is approximated by

\[

\frac{\partial \tilde{C}}{\partial \tilde{t}}

\approx

\frac{\tilde{C}_i^{n+1}-\tilde{C}_i^n}{\Delta \tilde{t}}.

\]

The second spatial derivative is approximated using the central-difference stencil

\[

\frac{\partial^2\tilde{C}}{\partial\tilde{x}^2}

\approx

\frac{

\tilde{C}_{i-1}^{n+1}

-2\tilde{C}_{i}^{n+1}

+\tilde{C}_{i+1}^{n+1}

}{

\Delta\tilde{x}^2

}.

\]

The spatial derivative is evaluated at time level \(n+1\), which makes the scheme implicit.

Defining

\[

\lambda

=

\frac{\Delta\tilde{t}}{\Delta\tilde{x}^2},

\]

the rearranged equation is

\[

-\lambda\tilde{C}_{i-1}^{n+1}

+

(1+2\lambda)\tilde{C}_{i}^{n+1}

-

\lambda\tilde{C}_{i+1}^{n+1}

=

\tilde{C}_{i}^{n}.

\]

This equation applies to interior nodes. Boundary values are inserted into the equations adjacent to the boundaries.

The resulting coefficient matrix is tridiagonal and diagonally dominant in the standard formulation. The unknown values at time \(n+1\) are placed on the left-hand side, while the known values at time \(n\) and boundary contributions are placed on the right-hand side.
