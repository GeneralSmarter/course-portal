Overview

Lecture 8 consolidated the finite-element method for two-dimensional bar/truss structures and extended the workflow to a three-element structure.

The main focus was on:

• Reviewing the finite-element solution pipeline.

• Distinguishing local and global coordinates, displacements, and forces.

• Using shape functions to determine internal displacement within an element.

• Assigning structural degrees of freedom and constructing element-to-global connectivity.

• Interpreting assembly matrices rather than treating them as black boxes.

• Solving for nodal displacements, element forces, strains, stresses, and support reactions.

• Checking results against physical expectations and equilibrium.

• Demonstrating that different element orientations and numbering choices can produce different intermediate vectors and matrices while giving the same physical solution, provided the process is internally consistent.

• Previewing the extension from bar elements to elements involving bending moments and shear.

The lecture repeatedly emphasised that the simple hand-worked examples can underestimate the complexity of larger systems. Once additional elements are introduced, the computational finite-element workflow becomes substantially more useful.

Key concepts

• Finite-element solution pipeline:

1. Define element geometry, material properties, and cross-sectional properties.

2. Establish local and global coordinate systems.

3. Form each element’s local stiffness matrix.

4. Transform local stiffness information into global coordinates.

5. Assemble the global stiffness matrix.

6. Apply loads and boundary conditions.

7. Solve for the unknown global nodal displacements.

8. Recover element displacements and forces.

9. Calculate strains and stresses.

10. Calculate support reactions.

11. Check equilibrium and physical plausibility.

• Local versus global quantities:

• Lower-case quantities such as local element displacements and forces are aligned with the element’s local axis.

• Upper-case quantities such as global nodal forces and displacements are aligned with the global \(X_G\) and \(Y_G\) axes.

• The exact notation should be checked against the course notes because the ASR transcript frequently confused upper- and lower-case symbols.

• Coordinate systems:

• A local element coordinate system is chosen along the element.

• The orientation angle \(\alpha\) relates the local \(x\)-axis to the global \(X_G\)-axis.

• The angle must be used consistently in the transformation matrix and in the free-body diagram.

• An element can be oriented in either direction. Reversing its orientation changes the local degree-of-freedom order and signs of local quantities, but should not change the final physical response.

• Degrees of freedom:

• A pin support constrains both global translational directions.

• A roller support constrains one direction while allowing motion in the perpendicular direction.

• Constrained degrees of freedom have prescribed zero displacement and are not unknowns in the reduced system.

• For the three-element example, the pin constrained both \(X_G\) and \(Y_G\); the roller constrained \(Y_G\) but permitted \(X_G\) motion. The transcript’s node labels and exact diagram geometry should be checked against the lecture slides.

• Free-body diagrams:

• Free-body diagrams record the assumed element orientations, node numbering, force directions, and sign conventions.

• They are essential for interpreting element force and displacement vectors.

• They also provide a basis for calculating support reactions.

• Skipping them makes it easy to extract or interpret the wrong vector component.

• Assembly matrices:

• Assembly matrices map local element degrees of freedom into global structural degrees of freedom.

• A zero column indicates a local degree of freedom that is constrained and therefore has no corresponding unknown global degree of freedom.

• A zero row indicates that the element has no direct connection to the corresponding global structural degree of freedom.

• The position of the non-zero entries encodes element connectivity.

• Global stiffness:

• The global stiffness matrix is formed by adding the contributions of all elements after mapping them into the global degree-of-freedom system.

• A larger structure does not fundamentally change the method; it increases the matrix size and the amount of bookkeeping.

• Global and local force representations:

• Global components are preferable when combining forces from elements with different orientations, such as calculating a total support reaction.

• Local axial forces are preferable when calculating axial stress or sizing an element’s cross-sectional area.

• Displacement plotting:

• The computed displacements are usually small relative to the structure’s dimensions.

• A single magnification factor should be applied consistently to every node and direction when plotting the deformed shape.

• Applying different scale factors to different nodes or directions distorts the structural shape and can mislead interpretation.

• The lecture corrected a notation or code issue involving “radian”; the intended unit was stated to be radians, including an np-related expression. The exact code correction should be checked on page 55 of the notes.

• Internal displacement:

• Nodal displacements can be interpolated to find the displacement at a point inside an element.

• For the bar-element shape functions discussed, the displacement varies linearly along the element.

• The interpolation depends on assumptions including constant cross-sectional area, constant elastic modulus, and constant axial stiffness properties along the element.

• Verification:

• At a loaded node, the internal element forces should sum consistently with the applied external load.

• Support reactions should satisfy overall equilibrium.

• A non-zero reaction in a direction that the support cannot resist is a major warning sign.

• Results should be checked against the physical behaviour expected from the structure.

Assembly matrices

The assembly matrices encode the mapping between element-level and global structural degrees of freedom.

Important interpretation rules:

• An all-zero column means that the associated local degree of freedom is constrained and has a known zero displacement.

• An all-zero row means that the element has no direct connection to the corresponding global degree of freedom.

• Non-zero entries indicate which global degree of freedom receives a contribution from a local component.

• Different element orientations or numbering choices change the appearance of the assembly matrices, but not the physical solution when used consistently.

The lecturer specifically warned that assembly matrices should not be treated as unexplained computational objects. Their zero rows, zero columns, and non-zero locations contain direct information about supports and connectivity.

Equilibrium checks

At the loaded node, the internal forces contributed by the connected elements should sum to the applied external load.

The lecturer described this as a direct check:

Deflected shape, strain, and stress

The computed nodal displacements can be used to plot the deformed structure. Because the true displacement is usually much smaller than the structural dimensions, the plotted displacement is exaggerated by a common scale factor.

The lecture distinguished:

• True deformed position.

• Exaggerated plotted position.

• Local element displacement, which describes extension or compression along the element.

• Global nodal movement, which may include rigid translation caused by connected elements.

An inclined element can have non-zero displacements at both ends because its nodes move as part of the overall structure. The relative component of displacement along the element determines axial extension or compression.

The axial strain is calculated from the change in element length divided by the original length. Stress can then be found using the elastic modulus.

Equations and definitions

Local bar-element displacement interpolation

For a two-node bar element:

\[

u(x) =

\left(1-\frac{x}{L}\right)d_1

+

\left(\frac{x}{L}\right)d_2

\]

where \(u(x)\) is the axial displacement at position \(x\), \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) are the nodal axial displacements, and \(L\) is the element length.

The corresponding linear shape functions are:

\[

N_1(x)=1-\frac{x}{L}

\]

\[

N_2(x)=\frac{x}{L}

\]

so that:

\[

u(x)=N_1(x)d_1+N_2(x)d_2

\]

Midpoint displacement

At \(x=L/2\):

\[

u\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)=\frac{d_1+d_2}{2}

\]

This is valid for the linear bar-element interpolation discussed in the lecture.

Axial strain

The lecture defined axial strain as change in length divided by original length:

\[

\varepsilon =

\frac{\Delta L}{L}

\]

For the one-dimensional bar interpolation:

\[

\varepsilon =

\frac{du}{dx}

=

\frac{d_2-d_1}{L}

\]

Sign convention:

• \(\varepsilon>0\): tensile extension.

• \(\varepsilon<0\): compressive shortening.

Normal stress

For a linearly elastic bar:

\[

\sigma=E\varepsilon

\]

where:

• \(\sigma\) is axial normal stress.

• \(E\) is Young’s modulus.

• \(\varepsilon\) is axial strain.

The exact material assumptions and numerical modulus used in the three-element example should be checked against the course notes.

Local bar stiffness matrix

The standard two-node local bar stiffness matrix is:

\[

\mathbf k^{(e)}_{\text{local}}

=

\frac{EA}{L}

\begin{bmatrix}

1 & -1\\

-1 & 1

\end{bmatrix}

\]

Global stiffness contribution

For an element transformed from local to global coordinates:

\[

\mathbf k^{(e)}_{\text{global}}

=

\mathbf T_e^{T}

\mathbf k^{(e)}_{\text{local}}

\mathbf T_e

\]

where \(\mathbf T_e\) is the element transformation matrix.

The exact matrix dimensions and notation used in the course should be checked against the lecture notes.

Global stiffness assembly

The overall structural stiffness matrix is the sum of the element contributions:

\[

\mathbf K_G

=

\sum_e \mathbf K_G^{(e)}

\]

where each element contribution is placed into the appropriate global degree-of-freedom locations using its assembly/connectivity matrix.

Global equilibrium equation

The solved structural system has the general form:

\[

\mathbf K_G\mathbf Q=\mathbf F

\]

where:

• \(\mathbf K_G\) is the assembled global stiffness matrix.

• \(\mathbf Q\) is the vector of unknown global nodal displacements.

• \(\mathbf F\) is the applied global load vector.

The transcript uses \(Q\), \(F\), \(f\), \(D\), and \(d\) in ways that are not always distinguishable in the ASR. The exact course notation must be confirmed.

Support reactions

Support reactions are obtained after solving the unknown displacements by recovering the force components associated with constrained degrees of freedom.

At a support connected to multiple elements, the reaction is the sum of the relevant element force contributions expressed in global coordinates:

\[

\mathbf R_{\text{support}}

=

\sum_e \mathbf F^{(e)}_{\text{support, global}}

\]

Hollow circular cross-sectional area

For a hollow circular tube:

\[

A=\frac{\pi}{4}\left(D_o^2-D_i^2\right)

\]

with:

\[

D_i=D_o-2t

\]

where \(D_o\) is the outside diameter, \(D_i\) is the inside diameter, and \(t\) is the wall thickness.
