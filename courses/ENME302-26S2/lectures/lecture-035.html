1. Von Neumann stability analysis

The lecture considered the FTCS discretisation of the heat equation. The discretised equation was presented in the form

\[

\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}

-\alpha

\frac{T_{i+1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i-1}^n}{\Delta x^2}

=0

\]

where:

• \(T_i^n\) is the computed temperature at spatial index \(i\) and time level \(n\).

• \(\alpha\) is the thermal diffusivity.

• \(\Delta t\) is the time-step size.

• \(\Delta x\) is the spatial grid spacing.

The lecture defined the solution error as the difference between the computed and exact discrete solutions:

\[

\varepsilon_i^n = \hat{T}_i^n - T_i^n

\]

where the hat denotes the computed solution. Equivalently,

\[

\hat{T}_i^n = T_i^n+\varepsilon_i^n

\]

Substitution of the computed solution into the finite-difference equation gives an equation containing both the exact solution terms and the error terms. Since the exact solution satisfies the discretised equation apart from truncation-error effects, the error terms can be considered separately. The error therefore satisfies an equation of the form

\[

\frac{\varepsilon_i^{n+1}-\varepsilon_i^n}{\Delta t}

-\alpha

\frac{\varepsilon_{i+1}^n-2\varepsilon_i^n+\varepsilon_{i-1}^n}

{\Delta x^2}

=0

\]

This expresses how the solution error propagates through time and space.

2. Fourier representation of the error

The error was represented as a Fourier mode. The lecture first referred to the complete Fourier series, containing both cosine and sine components, and then used an exponential representation.

A single error mode can be written as

\[

\varepsilon_i^n = E^n e^{\mathrm{i}k i\Delta x}

\]

where:

• \(E^n\) is the amplitude at time level \(n\).

• \(\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}\) is the imaginary unit.

• \(k\) is a wave number.

• \(i\) is the spatial grid index.

• \(\Delta x\) is the spatial spacing.

The lowercase spatial index \(i\) must be distinguished from the imaginary unit \(\mathrm{i}\).

Because the heat equation and the finite-difference scheme are linear, Fourier modes are uncoupled. Consequently, it is sufficient to examine the propagation of one Fourier mode. The behaviour of the full error can then be inferred from the behaviour of its individual modes.

The neighbouring spatial terms become

\[

\varepsilon_{i+1}^n

=

E^n e^{\mathrm{i}k(i+1)\Delta x}

\]

and

\[

\varepsilon_{i-1}^n

=

E^n e^{\mathrm{i}k(i-1)\Delta x}

\]

The exponential terms can be factored using

\[

e^{\mathrm{i}k(i+1)\Delta x}

=

e^{\mathrm{i}ki\Delta x}e^{\mathrm{i}k\Delta x}

\]

and

\[

e^{\mathrm{i}k(i-1)\Delta x}

=

e^{\mathrm{i}ki\Delta x}e^{-\mathrm{i}k\Delta x}

\]

The identity

\[

e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}

=

2\cos(\theta)

\]

is used to simplify the neighbouring-mode terms.

3. Amplification factor

The dimensionless parameter

\[

\lambda = \frac{\alpha\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}

\]

was introduced.

After substituting the Fourier mode into the error equation and rearranging, the lecture obtained the relationship

\[

\varepsilon_i^{n+1}

=

\gamma \varepsilon_i^n

\]

where \(\gamma\) is the amplification factor.

For the FTCS heat-equation scheme,

\[

\gamma

=

1+2\lambda\left[\cos(k\Delta x)-1\right]

\]

Using the identity

\[

\cos(\theta)-1=-2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)

\]

this becomes

\[

\gamma

=

1-4\lambda\sin^2\left(\frac{k\Delta x}{2}\right)

\]

The amplification factor describes the change in the amplitude of a Fourier error mode:

• If \(|\gamma|>1\), the error grows and the scheme is unstable.

• If \(|\gamma|=1\), the error amplitude remains bounded at that step.

• If \(|\gamma|<1\), the error mode decays.

• Stability requires:

\[

|\gamma|\leq 1

\]

The criterion must hold for all relevant wave numbers \(k\).

4. Deriving the FTCS stability condition

Since

\[

0\leq \sin^2\left(\frac{k\Delta x}{2}\right)\leq 1

\]

the maximum value of the sine-squared term is 1.

The upper stability bound requires

\[

\gamma\leq 1

\]

For

\[

\gamma=1-4\lambda\sin^2\left(\frac{k\Delta x}{2}\right)

\]

this condition is satisfied provided \(\lambda\geq 0\). For a physical heat-diffusion problem, \(\alpha>0\), \(\Delta t>0\), and \(\Delta x>0\), so \(\lambda\geq0\) is automatically satisfied.

The lower stability bound requires

\[

\gamma\geq -1

\]

The most restrictive case occurs when

\[

\sin^2\left(\frac{k\Delta x}{2}\right)=1

\]

giving

\[

1-4\lambda\geq -1

\]

and therefore

\[

\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}

\]

Hence the FTCS stability condition is

\[

\boxed{\frac{\alpha\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}\leq\frac{1}{2}}

\]

or, equivalently,

\[

\boxed{\Delta t\leq\frac{\Delta x^2}{2\alpha}}

\]

This agrees with the earlier intuitive or physical interpretation of the stability condition.

5. Consistency, stability, and convergence

The lecture defined convergence as the property that the computed solution approaches the exact solution of the PDE as the temporal and spatial grid resolutions are refined:

\[

\Delta t\rightarrow0,

\qquad

\Delta x\rightarrow0

\]

In this limit, the discretisation error and solution error should vanish, and

\[

\hat{T}_i^n \rightarrow T(x_i,t_n)

\]

for the relevant spatial coordinates and time levels.

Consistency concerns the truncation error introduced when the derivatives in the PDE are replaced by finite-difference approximations. The truncation error arises from neglected higher-order terms in Taylor-series expansions.

Stability concerns whether errors remain bounded as the numerical method advances in time. For the FTCS method, von Neumann analysis examines whether Fourier components of the error are amplified.

The lecture stated the Lax equivalence theorem:

> For a well-posed linear initial-value problem and a consistent numerical scheme, the scheme is convergent if and only if it is stable.

Therefore, for the class of problems covered by the theorem:

\[

\text{consistency}+\text{stability}

\Longleftrightarrow

\text{convergence}

\]

The theorem applies to linear problems. The lecturer noted that nonlinear PDEs can sometimes be handled by linearisation, but the detailed treatment of nonlinear problems was outside the scope of the lecture.

6. Well-posedness and boundary conditions

A well-posed problem must be sufficiently constrained to have a meaningful solution. The lecturer used a structural-mechanics example involving a beam to illustrate the importance of boundary conditions.

If a beam is supported at only one end and a load is applied, it may rotate rather than reach a unique steady configuration. In that case, the boundary conditions do not adequately constrain the problem, so the problem is not well posed.

The practical point was that boundary conditions must be checked before assessing the numerical method. Stability and convergence analysis assumes an appropriately posed underlying mathematical problem.

9. Gradient and stationary points

For a multivariable objective function \(F(\mathbf{x})\), the gradient is the vector of first partial derivatives:

\[

\nabla F(\mathbf{x})

=

\begin{bmatrix}

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_1}\\

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_2}\\

\vdots\\

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_N}

\end{bmatrix}

\]

A stationary point satisfies

\[

\nabla F(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}

\]

For a one-variable function, this reduces to the familiar condition

\[

\frac{dF}{dx}=0

\]

A zero gradient identifies a stationary point, but does not by itself establish whether the point is a minimum, maximum, or saddle point.

For a one-variable function, a local minimum can be identified using the second derivative:

\[

\frac{d^2F}{dx^2}>0

\]

provided the function is sufficiently differentiable.

10. Hessian matrix

The Hessian is the matrix of second partial derivatives:

\[

\nabla^2F(\mathbf{x})

=

\begin{bmatrix}

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_1^2}

&

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_1\partial x_2}

&

\cdots

&

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_1\partial x_N}

\\

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_2\partial x_1}

&

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_2^2}

&

\cdots

&

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_2\partial x_N}

\\

\vdots

&

\vdots

&

\ddots

&

\vdots

\\

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_N\partial x_1}

&

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_N\partial x_2}

&

\cdots

&

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_N^2}

\end{bmatrix}

\]

For a twice-differentiable function, the mixed partial derivatives are generally equal under the usual smoothness assumptions, so the Hessian is symmetric:

\[

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}

=

\frac{\partial^2F}{\partial x_j\partial x_i}

\]

The Hessian describes second-order curvature and is related to the second-degree Taylor expansion. In one dimension it reduces to the scalar second derivative.

11. Convex and non-convex functions

A strictly convex objective function has a single minimiser, which is also the global minimum. The lecture illustrated this using the two-variable function

\[

F(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+x_2^2

\]

This function has a bowl-shaped surface with its minimum at the origin. An optimisation method can conceptually reach the minimum by moving downhill.

A multimodal function has multiple minima. These may include:

• One global minimum, which has the lowest objective-function value.

• Other local minima, which are lower than nearby points but not lower than the global minimum.

Local minima can act as traps for optimisation algorithms. Noise in measurement data can make the objective function more irregular and make optimisation more difficult.

12. Convex sets and convex hulls

A set \(\mathcal{X}\) is convex if, for any two points \(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2\in\mathcal{X}\), the entire line segment joining them also lies in \(\mathcal{X}\).

The line segment can be represented as

\[

\theta\mathbf{x}_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf{x}_2,

\qquad

0\leq\theta\leq1

\]

Thus, convexity requires

\[

\theta\mathbf{x}_1+(1-\theta)\mathbf{x}_2\in\mathcal{X}

\]

for all \(\theta\) in the interval \([0,1]\).

A circular region was given as an example of a convex set. A set with an indentation can be non-convex because a line segment between two points may pass outside the set.

The convex hull of a set is the set of all convex combinations of its points. Geometrically, it can be visualised as the smallest convex region containing the original points, similar to placing a stretched rubber band around the outermost points.

13. Epigraph and convexity of functions

The epigraph of a function is the region on or above its graph:

\[

\operatorname{epi}(F)

=

\{(\mathbf{x},y): y\geq F(\mathbf{x})\}

\]

A function is convex if its epigraph is a convex set.

The lecture used a function with bumps to illustrate that the region above the graph may fail the line-segment test. If two points in the epigraph can be joined by a line segment that leaves the epigraph, then the epigraph is non-convex and the function is not convex over that interval.
