Geometry modelling

The second part of the lecture introduced geometry modelling in Console. Geometry creation is part of the pre-processing stage of a simulation.

A geometric model should capture:

• The physical shape relevant to the problem.

• Material regions or properties where relevant.

• Loads and constraints through the appropriate boundaries.

• The computational domain required by the solver.

The geometry is subsequently used to generate the mesh. It can also be parameterised for studies involving variables such as angle of attack or aerofoil length.

CAD models intended for analysis do not need to contain every detail included in a manufacturing model. Small labels, markings, and other features that do not significantly affect structural integrity or the governing physics can usually be removed before meshing.

However, simplification must not remove physically important features. An external fillet may have little effect in a low-stress region and can potentially be removed. Removing an internal fillet at a loaded corner can create a sharp point, causing an artificial stress concentration and a numerical result that is not representative of the real component.

The model should therefore be simplified enough to reduce unnecessary computational effort, but not so much that it changes the relevant physics.

Mesh generation

Mesh generation subdivides the computational geometry into small discrete elements. The resulting mesh is used to approximate the governing equations and calculate the solution.

Mesh properties influence:

• Convergence rate.

• Whether a solution converges at all.

• Solution accuracy.

• Resolution of local gradients.

• Computational time.

• Memory requirements.

A finer mesh generally provides a more resolved and potentially more accurate solution, but increases the number of elements, degrees of freedom, equations, and CPU time.

A coarse mesh may solve quickly but fail to capture important physical behaviour. Simply selecting an automatic mesh setting is not sufficient justification for mesh quality; the result should be checked and refined where necessary.

Mesh quality and refinement

Important mesh considerations include:

• Grid or element density.

• Smooth variation in element size.

• Cell-length-to-volume ratio.

• Element skewness.

• Boundary-layer resolution.

• Local refinement in regions with steep gradients.

Adjacent elements should transition smoothly in size. Abrupt changes can reduce continuity between neighbouring elements and degrade the numerical solution.

Highly skewed or distorted elements should be avoided because they can reduce accuracy and impair convergence.

In CFD, a no-slip wall boundary condition causes velocity to decrease from the bulk-flow value to zero at the wall. At high Reynolds numbers, this change occurs over a small distance. Inflation layers place smaller elements near the wall to resolve the velocity gradient.

Adaptive mesh refinement can increase resolution only where it is needed. In the tsunami example mentioned in the lecture, the mesh was refined locally where the ocean thickness or wave solution changed rapidly. Console also supports adaptive mesh refinement.

Dimensional reduction

Thin or small features can sometimes be represented using lower-dimensional models:

• A thin electrical trace may be approximated as a two-dimensional surface, with current assumed constant through its thickness.

• A thin coil may be approximated as a one-dimensional line, with temperature or current treated as uniform through the small cross-section.

• A three-dimensional beam may be reduced to a two-dimensional plane-stress or plane-strain model.

Dimensional reduction is an approximation and is not physically identical to the full three-dimensional object. The assumptions must be assessed after solving to determine whether they are valid for the intended purpose.

Mesh terminology and element types

The lecture introduced the following terminology:

• Cell: a finite-volume or control-volume region.

• Node: a grid point.

• Cell centre: the centre of a cell, commonly used in finite-volume methods.

• Edge: the edge of an element or face.

• Face: the boundary of a cell.

• Zone: a group of cells or regions.

• Domain: a group of nodes and cells forming a computational region.

Common element types include:

• One-dimensional line elements.

• Two-dimensional triangular elements.

• Two-dimensional quadrilateral elements.

• Three-dimensional tetrahedral elements.

• Hexahedral elements.

• Prisms.

• Pyramids.

Elements may contain midpoint nodes. Midpoint nodes allow higher-order interpolation, such as quadratic shape functions, for dependent variables including displacement, temperature, or velocity.

Meshes may be:

• Structured.

• Unstructured.

• A combination of different element types.

A structured mesh has a regular arrangement of nodes, often corresponding to rows and columns. Neighbouring nodes can be indexed using directions such as north, south, east, and west, or with IJK indexing in three dimensions.

Advantages of structured meshes include:

• Easy generation.

• Low storage requirements.

• Simple neighbour identification.

• Well-behaved algebraic systems.

The main disadvantage is difficulty fitting a regular grid to complex geometries. Unstructured meshes are more flexible for complicated shapes but require more connectivity information and may be more difficult to control.
