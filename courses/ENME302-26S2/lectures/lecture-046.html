Overview

• Contextualised analytical and numerical PDE methods.

• Reviewed finite difference and finite element methods.

• Introduced computational fluid dynamics (CFD), including its benefits, limitations, and common modelling pitfalls.

• Explained the motivation, advantages, and limitations of BEM.

• Described research applications involving hydrofoils, cavitation, and normal flow control.

The central idea is that, for suitable PDEs, the field throughout a domain can be reconstructed from quantities defined on the boundary. This allows the numerical discretisation to be reduced by one spatial dimension and avoids meshing the domain interior.

Detailed notes

1. PDE solution approaches

Most physical processes can be modelled using PDEs. Engineering analysis therefore requires either solving PDEs exactly or approximating their solutions.

Two broad approaches were distinguished:

1. Analytical solution

• Separation of variables was given as an example.

• PDE classification is needed because different PDE types have different general solutions.

• Boundary conditions are then used to determine the constants or functions in the general solution.

• Analytical solutions are often difficult to obtain and limited in scope.

2. Numerical solution

• The continuous equations are discretised at a finite set of points or elements.

• The resulting equations can be represented using linear algebra.

• Computers are generally much better suited to solving large systems of algebraic equations than directly solving continuous PDEs.

• The lecture stated that most numerical methods follow this general pattern.

The course teaches finite difference and finite element methods to establish an understanding of numerical PDE workflows. Commercial numerical solvers are often used in practice because they are flexible and practical for engineering applications.

2. Finite difference and finite element methods

#### Finite difference method

The finite difference method approximates derivatives in the governing equation using finite changes over small distances.

The approximation is constructed using values at nearby grid points. Taylor-series expansions can be used to derive forward, backward, or central difference representations with different accuracy properties.

The conceptual process is:

1. Place a grid over the domain.

2. Approximate derivatives using neighbouring grid values.

3. Substitute those approximations into the governing PDE.

4. Solve the resulting algebraic system.

#### Finite element method

The domain is divided into a collection of finite elements. Within each element, the solution is approximated using polynomial shape functions.

The coefficients of the approximation are selected so that the approximation satisfies the governing equations in an appropriate approximate sense, typically by minimising or controlling the residual error over the elements.

The lecturer explicitly noted that this was only a brief and informal description and referred students to earlier lectures for a proper treatment.

3. Computational fluid dynamics

CFD was defined as the prediction of fluid behaviour using numerical methods.

A fluid was described as a material that deforms continuously under shear stress. The transcript rendered “shear” as “sheer” in this portion.

Fluids are important because:

• They are present in the surrounding environment.

• Engineering components interact with fluids or must manage fluid interactions.

• Fluid behaviour is difficult to predict because the governing equations are mathematically challenging.

For a basic fluid-flow problem, the momentum equation contains a material derivative and terms representing forces acting on the fluid. The lecturer compared this broadly with Newton's second law, describing it as an “F equals MA” relationship in a more complicated form.

The momentum equation is coupled with the continuity equation. Consequently, the equations must generally be solved together. The problem becomes more complicated for cases involving effects such as:

• Compressibility.

• Magnetic fields.

• Turbulence.

• Other coupled physical phenomena.

4. Why numerical fluid simulations are used

The lecture identified three general approaches to understanding fluid behaviour:

• Experimentation.

• Analytical theory.

• Numerical simulation.

Numerical methods are useful because experiments may be expensive, slow, difficult to instrument, or physically impossible at the relevant scale. Numerical simulations can provide:

• Relatively rapid setup and execution compared with some experiments.

• Low marginal cost per additional simulation, unless substantial computing resources are required.

• High densities of virtual probe points.

• Access to internal flow information that may be difficult or impossible to measure experimentally.

• The ability to investigate extreme conditions without building a physical test facility.

An example was given involving a space shuttle re-entering at approximately Mach 25. Constructing a wind tunnel capable of reproducing the complete physical situation would be extremely difficult, whereas a numerical model can represent such conditions without the same physical constraints.

5. CFD limitations and failure modes

The main drawback of numerical simulation is that a simulation can produce a plausible-looking result even when it is wrong.

Important requirements include:

• The computational geometry must represent the real geometry.

• The mesh must resolve the important dynamics of the domain.

• Boundary conditions must be physically appropriate and applied in the correct locations.

• Boundary conditions must be mutually consistent.

• All relevant physics must be included.

• The solver and numerical settings must be suitable for the problem.

Turbulence was highlighted as a major issue. Treating a genuinely turbulent flow as laminar can produce results that are unrelated to the real behaviour.

Other practical limitations include:

• The need for appropriate software and expertise.

• Potentially expensive software licences.

• Long runtimes, especially for complex turbulence simulations.

• The possibility that simulations may require very small time steps.

• The need for substantial computational resources.

The lecturer warned that commercial CFD software will always produce a colourful flow diagram or other visual output. The user must determine whether the result is physically valid rather than assuming that a converged or attractive plot is correct.

A practical warning was given that a fluid problem with more than one significant complicating factor may represent at least a PhD-scale research problem. Examples included:

• Complex turbulence.

• Non-Newtonian rheology.

• Complicated boundary conditions.

• Complicated geometry.

• Coupled multiphysics.

This was presented as a rule of thumb rather than a formal classification.

6. Motivation for BEM

Finite element and finite volume simulations are versatile and can provide high fidelity for many flows and PDEs. However, they are not always the most efficient approach.

For some specific PDEs and flows, a lower-fidelity method can provide sufficient accuracy at much lower computational cost.

BEM was presented as:

• Limited in scope.

• Very fast when applicable.

• Useful for optimisation because many simulations can be performed quickly.

• Easier to discretise because only the boundary is discretised.

• Capable of evaluating field values inside the domain after solving for the boundary behaviour.

• Suitable for certain elliptic PDEs, particularly problems related to Laplace's equation.

• Well suited to potential flow and Stokes flow in the context presented.

The lecturer stated that one example solved a flow field in approximately 5 milliseconds on a laboratory computer. This value is an illustrative lecture example, not a general performance guarantee.

7. BEM limitations

BEM is not a general replacement for CFD or finite element methods.

Its main limitations are:

• It applies only to certain governing equations and physical assumptions.

• It may not be possible to include all relevant real-world physics.

• The initial solution is obtained on the boundary; evaluating interior points requires additional computation.

• It is generally lower fidelity when the real problem contains physics that cannot be represented by the selected PDE.

The lecturer associated BEM primarily with elliptic PDEs such as Laplace's equation. The transcript stated that potential or Stokes flows have Reynolds numbers of “0 or infinity and nothing in the middle”; this is an informal and potentially oversimplified statement. The precise applicability depends on the governing assumptions and formulation.

8. Sources, dipoles, and Green's functions

Before developing the BEM formulation, the lecture introduced three ideas.

#### Point source

A point source can be viewed as imposing the direct value of a field at a point, analogous to a Dirichlet condition concentrated at a point.

Examples include specifying:

• Temperature at a point.

• Voltage at a point.

• Species concentration at a point.

The water-draining from a point into a flat basin was given as an informal two-dimensional analogy for a point source.

#### Point dipole

A point dipole was described as the directional or normal derivative of a point source, analogous to a Neumann condition at a point.

Examples include specifying:

• Heat flux in a direction at a point.

• A concentration gradient in a direction at a point.

• Flow velocity in a direction at a point.

A point does not have a conventional boundary normal, so the lecturer explained that the derivative direction can be selected and treated as the relevant direction.

#### Green's function

A Green's function, written conceptually as \(G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0)\), describes the response of a PDE at a field location \(\mathbf{x}\) due to a point source located at \(\mathbf{x}_0\).

Green's functions depend on the governing PDE and its associated conditions.

The lecture used an example involving an infinite plate held at \(0\) K. If a point on the plate is held at a prescribed temperature, the Green's function describes the temperature response at another location as a function of distance from the source, with the temperature returning to zero at infinity.

For Laplace's equation, the Green's function satisfies a relation involving the Laplacian and the Dirac delta function:

\[

\nabla^2 G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0) + \delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)=0

\]

The exact sign convention can depend on the definition of the Green's function. The equation above follows the sign convention stated in the lecture.

9. Dirac delta function

The Dirac delta function was described informally as:

• Zero everywhere except at its singular point.

• Unbounded at the singular point.

• Having unit integral over any domain that contains the singular point.

Its defining integral property is:

\[

\int_{\Omega}\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)\,d\Omega =

\begin{cases}

1, & \mathbf{x}_0 \in \Omega \\

0, & \mathbf{x}_0 \notin \Omega

\end{cases}

\]

The delta function also has the sampling property:

\[

\int_{\Omega} f(\mathbf{x})\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)\,d\Omega

= f(\mathbf{x}_0)

\]

provided that \(\mathbf{x}_0\) lies within the domain and the relevant mathematical conditions are satisfied.

The infinite value and infinitesimal width should be understood in the distributional sense, not as an ordinary finite-valued function.

10. Green's second identity and the boundary representation

The lecture considered two functions \(F\) and \(G\), with \(G\) chosen as a Green's function. Green's second identity was written in vector-calculus form:

\[

G\nabla^2F-F\nabla^2G

=

\nabla\cdot\left(G\nabla F-F\nabla G\right)

\]

The equality follows from the product rule.

Integrating over a domain \(\Omega\) and applying the divergence theorem converts the volume integral into a boundary integral:

\[

\int_{\Omega}

\left(

G\nabla^2F-F\nabla^2G

\right)

\,d\Omega

=

\int_{\Gamma}

\left(

G\nabla F-F\nabla G

\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}\,d\Gamma

\]

For a harmonic field \(F\),

\[

\nabla^2F=0

\]

and for the Green's function under the lecture's sign convention,

\[

\nabla^2G=-\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0)

\]

The volume integral then uses the delta-function sampling property to recover the value \(F(\mathbf{x}_0)\). The result is a boundary representation of the form:

\[

F(\mathbf{x}_0)

=

\int_{\Gamma}

\left[

G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial n}(\mathbf{x})

-

F(\mathbf{x})

\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_0)

\right]

\,d\Gamma

\]

The exact sign and normal-direction convention depend on whether the normal is taken inward or outward. The lecture's diagram used an inward-facing normal at one point, while the standard divergence-theorem expression is commonly written using an outward normal. This convention should therefore be checked against the course notes or derivation slides.

The key result is that if the values of \(F\) and its normal derivative are known on the boundary, then \(F\) can be evaluated anywhere in the domain by selecting the desired location \(\mathbf{x}_0\). No interior grid is required for this representation.

11. BEM discretisation and linear system

To convert the boundary representation into a numerical method:

1. Divide the boundary into boundary elements.

2. Define a control point, or collocation point, on each element.

3. Place the source point \(\mathbf{x}_0\) at a control point.

4. Use the symmetry of the Green's function to switch its arguments where appropriate.

5. Interpret the formulation as a distribution of sources and dipoles located on the boundary.

6. Assume a suitable distribution, such as constant source or dipole strength over each element.

7. Enforce the known boundary conditions at every control point.

8. Sum the influence of all boundary elements at each control point.

9. Assemble the resulting equations into a matrix system.

10. Solve the system for the unknown source and dipole strengths.

11. Use the solved boundary representation to evaluate the field at interior or exterior points.

The source and dipole strengths were initially discussed as having unit strength when calculating influence coefficients. The actual strengths are then found by solving the assembled system.

The resulting structure can be represented abstractly as:

\[

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}

\]

where:

• \(\mathbf{A}\) contains the influence coefficients of the boundary elements.

• \(\mathbf{x}\) contains unknown source or dipole strengths, depending on the formulation.

• \(\mathbf{b}\) contains the imposed boundary-condition values.

The lecture emphasised that the method is not solving for an unknown field on a volume mesh. Instead, it determines the boundary distribution required to satisfy the boundary conditions and then reconstructs the field from that boundary distribution.

13. Application to potential flow

The Navier–Stokes equations are not generally Laplace equations. To use BEM for fluid-flow problems, the lecturer imposed simplifying assumptions:

• Inviscid flow.

• Irrotational flow.

• Incompressible flow.

• Low Mach number in the context described.

For an irrotational flow, the velocity field can be represented as the gradient of a velocity potential \(\Phi\):

\[

\mathbf{u}=\nabla\Phi

\]

For incompressible flow, the continuity equation gives:

\[

\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0

\]

Substituting \(\mathbf{u}=\nabla\Phi\) gives:

\[

\nabla^2\Phi=0

\]

Thus, the velocity potential satisfies Laplace's equation and can be treated using a BEM formulation.

The same general process applies:

• Represent the flow using source distributions and dipoles over a body surface.

• Assume constant source or dipole strength over each element.

• Evaluate the influence integrals between control points.

• Assemble and solve a linear system.

• Recover the velocity potential.

• Obtain the flow field from the gradient of the velocity potential, together with any imposed or freestream contribution.

The lecture mentioned additional mathematical complications for external flow, including circulation-related corrections. These were not derived in detail.

14. Cavitation and hydrofoil flow control

Cavitation occurs when the pressure in a liquid falls sufficiently for local vaporisation to occur. A vapour-filled cavity can form, often near a solid surface.

At approximately constant height, Bernoulli's equation implies that increasing speed is associated with decreasing static pressure. For a hydrofoil:

• The flow accelerates over the foil surfaces.

• The resulting pressure difference generates lift.

• The angle of attack and foil geometry influence the pressure distribution.

• At sufficiently high flow speed, the pressure on the suction side can fall enough to initiate cavitation.

Cavitation is undesirable because:

• Collapse of the cavity can damage the surface.

• The cavity changes the flow path.

• Additional drag is generated.

• The lift-to-drag ratio can deteriorate substantially.

The lecture stated that the lift may not change significantly when cavitation occurs, but the drag can become sufficiently large that the lift-to-drag ratio becomes unacceptable.

15. Flow control

Two broad forms of flow control were identified:

1. Geometric flow control

• Modify the shape of the body interacting with the flow.

• Streamlining was given as an example.

2. Fluidic flow control

• Directly influence the fluid behaviour.

• Possible methods include injecting or removing fluid, or inducing wave-like behaviour.

The research application discussed was normal flow control. This involves transpiration through a surface:

• Fluid is injected through or sucked through the body surface.

• The flow through the surface is normal to the boundary.

• The amplitude can vary along the surface.

The objective was to modify the pressure distribution over a hydrofoil so that cavitation could be avoided.

The presented results involved:

• A baseline foil at an angle of attack of \(10^\circ\).

• A modified profile with improved lift.

• Maintenance of the moment coefficient about the aerodynamic centre so that the modified profile would not immediately create an unstable or unacceptable pitching response.

• A calculated distribution of normal control required to recover a target pressure distribution.

• A nondimensional cavitation criterion based on the pressure coefficient.

The lecturer described a case with a cavitation number of approximately 2 and a pressure-coefficient limit of approximately \(-2\). If the pressure coefficient does not fall below the critical value, cavitation should not be induced under the assumptions used.

The final question-and-answer discussion clarified that flow control does not necessarily mean only forcing fluid through pores. It can include any method intended to impose a desired form of fluid-body interaction, including streamlining, acoustic excitation, injection, or suction.

Preventing cavitation may reduce the lift coefficient in some cases, but the lift-to-drag ratio can still improve because cavitation can cause a much larger increase in drag. The design objective is therefore not necessarily maximum lift alone, but sufficient lift with acceptable drag and no damaging cavitation.
