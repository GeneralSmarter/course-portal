Overview

This lecture introduces finite-difference methods for solving transient partial differential equations, using the one-dimensional heat equation as the main example.

The lecture develops:

• Spatial discretisation using a second-order central-difference approximation.

• Temporal discretisation using a first-order forward difference.

• The fully explicit forward-time central-space (FTCS) scheme.

• The implicit backward-time central-space (BTCS) scheme.

• Time marching over a spatial grid.

• Convergence and stability of numerical solutions.

• The explicit-scheme stability restriction.

• Implementation of Neumann boundary conditions using ghost nodes.

• Matrix formulation of the implicit scheme.

The explicit method is simple to implement but conditionally stable. The implicit method requires the simultaneous solution of an algebraic system but is described as unconditionally stable. Both methods are first-order accurate in time and second-order accurate in space.

Key concepts

• Transient PDEs contain derivatives with respect to both space and time.

• Space is discretised into nodes, while time is discretised into time levels.

• The spatial second derivative is approximated using a central-difference stencil involving the left neighbour, centre node, and right neighbour.

• The forward difference in time compares the current time level with the next time level.

• In the explicit scheme, the spatial diffusion term is evaluated using known values at the previous time level.

• In the implicit scheme, the spatial diffusion term is evaluated at the next time level, so multiple unknown temperatures occur in the same equation.

• Explicit calculations update each interior node directly and independently from the previous time level.

• Implicit calculations produce a coupled system of algebraic equations that must be solved simultaneously.

• FTCS is first-order accurate in time and second-order accurate in space.

• BTCS is also first-order accurate in time and second-order accurate in space.

• Convergence means that the numerical solution approaches the true solution as the time step and spatial spacing are reduced.

• Stability means that numerical errors are not amplified as the calculation progresses.

• For the explicit heat-equation scheme, the stability coefficient must satisfy:

\[

\lambda = \frac{\alpha \Delta t}{\Delta x^2} \leq \frac{1}{2}

\]

• Refining the spatial grid imposes a severe restriction on the allowable explicit time step because:

\[

\Delta t \leq \frac{\Delta x^2}{2\alpha}

\]

• Halving the spatial spacing requires reducing the time step by a factor of four to maintain the same stability limit.

• Dirichlet boundary conditions can be imposed directly by fixing the boundary-node temperatures.

• Neumann boundary conditions specify a temperature gradient and can be implemented using ghost nodes.

• In the implicit scheme, the coefficient matrix remains constant over time when the material properties and grid are constant, while the right-hand-side vector changes with the previous temperature distribution.

• The steady-state solution of the one-dimensional heat equation with constant boundary temperatures is linear in space.

Detailed notes

1. From analytical to numerical solutions

Chapter 5 derived and solved the heat equation analytically using separation of variables and similarity solutions. The present lecture begins the numerical treatment of the same type of PDE using finite differences.

The key difference from an elliptic PDE is the presence of the time derivative. The solution must therefore be advanced, or marched, through successive time levels.

A one-dimensional spatial domain is divided into discrete nodes. The temperature at spatial node \(i\) and time level \(n\) is represented as:

\[

T_i^n

\]

The superscript denotes the time level, and the subscript denotes the spatial node. Time levels are separated by \(\Delta t\):

\[

t^{n+1}=t^n+\Delta t

\]

The initial condition is at \(n=0\), after which the solution is advanced through \(n=1,2,\ldots\).

2. Spatial discretisation

The diffusion term is approximated using the second-order central-difference formula:

\[

\left.\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}\right|_i

\approx

\frac{T_{i-1}-2T_i+T_{i+1}}{\Delta x^2}

\]

The approximation is second-order accurate in space, with a truncation error that scales with \(\Delta x^2\).

The stencil uses:

• \(T_{i-1}\): node immediately to the left.

• \(T_i\): centre node.

• \(T_{i+1}\): node immediately to the right.

This expression is applied to the interior nodes. Boundary values are instead determined by the prescribed boundary conditions.

3. Temporal discretisation

The time derivative is approximated using a one-sided forward difference:

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}

\approx

\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}

\]

This approximation is first-order accurate in time, with a truncation error that scales with \(\Delta t\).

The spatial index \(i\) remains fixed while the time level changes from \(n\) to \(n+1\). This represents the change in temperature at a fixed spatial position over one time step.

4. Explicit FTCS scheme

The one-dimensional heat equation is taken in the form:

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}

=

\alpha \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}

\]

where \(\alpha\) is the thermal diffusivity.

For the explicit method:

• The time derivative uses \(T_i^{n+1}\) and \(T_i^n\).

• The diffusion term is evaluated entirely at time level \(n\).

• The only unknown in each update equation is \(T_i^{n+1}\).

• No coupled system of equations is required.

Substitution of the finite differences gives:

\[

\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}

\alpha

\frac{T_{i-1}^n-2T_i^n+T_{i+1}^n}{\Delta x^2}

T_i^n

7. Steady-state behaviour

The numerical solution begins with zero interior temperature and fixed boundary temperatures of \(100^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and \(50^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The rod heats over time.

At steady state:

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=0

\]

The heat equation then reduces to:

\[

\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}=0

\]

Integrating twice with respect to \(x\) gives a linear temperature distribution. Therefore, the long-time numerical solution should approach a straight line between the two fixed boundary temperatures.

8. Explicit-scheme stability

Increasing \(\Delta t\) reduces the number of time steps needed to reach a specified final time. However, the explicit method becomes unstable if the time step is too large.

The lecture describes an example with a large time step, reported as \(5\) seconds. The numerical solution develops extremely large temperatures, values outside the physical range of the boundary conditions, and negative temperatures. These are indicators of numerical instability rather than physical behaviour.

The explicit stability condition is:

\[

\lambda \leq \frac{1}{2}

\]

Since:

\[

\lambda=\frac{\alpha\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}

\]

the time-step restriction is:

\[

\Delta t\leq \frac{\Delta x^2}{2\alpha}

\]

The restriction becomes more severe as the mesh is refined. If \(\Delta x\) is halved, \(\Delta x^2\) decreases by a factor of four, so \(\Delta t\) must also be reduced by a factor of four.

The lecture gives the following interpretation of the expanded explicit update:

\[

T_i^{n+1}

=

\lambda T_{i-1}^n

+

(1-2\lambda)T_i^n

+

\lambda T_{i+1}^n

\]

For non-negative temperatures to remain non-negative under the update, the coefficients should not become negative. The potentially problematic coefficient is:

\[

1-2\lambda

\]

Requiring:

\[

1-2\lambda\geq 0

\]

gives:

\[

\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}

\]

The explicit method is therefore:

• Simple and computationally inexpensive per time step.

• Easy to implement using a few lines of code.

• Limited by a conditional stability requirement.

• Potentially expensive for fine meshes because fine spatial spacing demands very small time steps.

9. Neumann boundary conditions and ghost nodes

A Neumann boundary condition specifies a temperature gradient rather than a temperature value.

At the left boundary, the lecture represents the prescribed gradient as:

\[

\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}=\beta

\]

A central-difference approximation at the boundary node \(i=0\) uses a ghost node:

\[

\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right|_0

\approx

\frac{T_1-T_{-1}}{2\Delta x}

=

\beta

\]

Here, \(T_{-1}\) is not a physical node. It is introduced to retain the central-difference stencil at the boundary.

Rearranging:

\[

T_{-1}=T_1-2\beta\Delta x

\]

This expression is substituted into the explicit heat-equation update at the boundary. The ghost-node value is therefore eliminated from the final calculation.

The Neumann boundary condition is applied at every time level. Because the example is explicit, the temperature values used in the boundary discretisation are evaluated at the previous time level.

10. Implicit BTCS scheme

The implicit scheme uses the same forward difference for the time derivative:

\[

\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}

\]

However, the spatial diffusion term is evaluated at the next time level:

\[

\frac{T_i^{n+1}-T_i^n}{\Delta t}

=

\alpha

\frac{T_{i-1}^{n+1}-2T_i^{n+1}+T_{i+1}^{n+1}}{\Delta x^2}

\]

Using \(\lambda=\alpha\Delta t/\Delta x^2\), rearrangement gives:

\[

-\lambda T_{i-1}^{n+1}

+

(1+2\lambda)T_i^{n+1}

-

\lambda T_{i+1}^{n+1}

=

T_i^n

\]

The next-time-level temperatures at the left neighbour, centre node, and right neighbour are all unknown. Consequently, the equations are coupled and must be solved simultaneously.

For four interior nodes, there are four unknown temperatures at time level \(n+1\), producing four algebraic equations. These can be written in matrix form:

\[

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}^{n+1}=\mathbf{B}

\]

where:

• \(\mathbf{A}\) is the coefficient matrix.

• \(\mathbf{T}^{n+1}\) is the vector of unknown interior temperatures at the next time level.

• \(\mathbf{B}\) contains the previous-time-level temperatures and contributions from prescribed boundary values.

For a uniform grid and constant \(\alpha\), the coefficient matrix has the tridiagonal structure associated with the coefficients:

\[

-\lambda,\qquad 1+2\lambda,\qquad -\lambda

\]

The matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) remains constant over time, while the right-hand-side vector changes because it depends on the previous temperature distribution. The constant matrix can therefore be formed, and potentially factorised or inverted, outside the main time loop.

11. Explicit versus implicit methods

Explicit FTCS:

• First-order accurate in time.

• Second-order accurate in space.

• Direct update of each interior node.

• Simple to code.

• Does not require a coupled linear solve.

• Conditionally stable.

• Requires small time steps when the spatial mesh is fine.

Implicit BTCS:

• First-order accurate in time.

• Second-order accurate in space.

• Requires a simultaneous solution for all interior nodes at each time level.

• More computationally complex per step.

• Described in the lecture as unconditionally stable.

• Can use larger time steps without numerical blow-up, although large time steps can still reduce accuracy.

• Has a coefficient matrix that remains constant when the grid and material properties are unchanged.

The lecture previews the Crank–Nicolson method as a hybrid approach that uses a more central treatment in time and is second-order accurate in time.
