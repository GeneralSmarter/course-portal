Overview

Lecture 15 introduced the time value of money and discounted cash-flow techniques for evaluating engineering projects. The lecture focused primarily on:

• Cash inflows and outflows

• Compound interest and spending power

• Present value and future value

• Discount rates and discount factors

• Net present value (NPV)

• Discounted payback period

• Internal rate of return (IRR)

• Sensitivity studies

• Communicating financial decisions clearly

The central idea is that cash flows occurring at different times must be converted into equivalent present-day values before they can be compared fairly.

Key concepts

• Engineering projects commonly require substantial investment, often using other people's or an organisation's money.

• Financial analysis is part of engineering decision-making because engineers provide information used to assess costs, cash flows, risks, and project viability.

• Cash flow is different from accounting profit.

• Cash inflows include:

• Revenue from selling products or services

• Sale of old assets or equipment

• Interest received

• Other money received by the company

• Cash outflows include:

• Employee wages and superannuation

• Payments to suppliers

• Dividends to shareholders

• Rent

• Capital expenditure on major assets

• Corporate tax

• Depreciation is not a cash flow. The lecturer instructed students to exclude depreciation from NPV calculations by default, while noting that it can affect taxable profit through a tax shield.

• Money has a time value:

• Money available today can be invested and grow.

• Inflation and changing spending power mean that a fixed amount of money generally buys less in the future.

• Future cash flows should therefore be converted into present-value terms.

• The discount rate and discount factor are different:

• The discount rate is the rate, usually denoted by \(R\), used in the calculation.

• The discount factor is the multiplier \(1/(1+R)^n\).

• NPV is the primary project-evaluation technique presented in the lecture.

• A positive NPV indicates that, at the selected discount rate, the project generates value in present-day terms.

• A negative NPV indicates that the project does not recover the required return at the selected discount rate.

• Mutually exclusive projects cannot all be selected: accepting one means rejecting another.

• Comparisons between projects must use consistent assumptions, including the same discount rate where appropriate.

• Discounted payback period accounts for the time value of money; ordinary payback period does not necessarily do so.

• IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV equal to zero.

• IRR can be misleading when cash flows are irregular or “lumpy”, particularly when there are multiple changes in the direction of cash flow.

• IRR does not directly indicate the total dollar value created. NPV and IRR can rank projects differently.

• Sensitivity studies test how changes in assumptions affect the result.

• Important variables for sensitivity analysis include:

• Initial investment

• Discount rate

• Revenue

• Expenses

• Tax treatment

• Inflation

• A useful approach is to change one variable at a time, then combine changes to examine best-case and worst-case scenarios.

• Financial results must be communicated using the cash amounts required at the relevant future dates, not only discounted present values.

• Financial communication should clearly state:

• The amount of money required

• When the money is required

• The expected benefit

• The project's NPV

• Any relevant payback or IRR information

Why financial analysis matters in engineering

Possible causes of financial failure include:

• Engineers not fully understanding the value being provided

• Incorrect financial calculations

• Underestimating technical or financial uncertainty

• Poor decisions involving other people's money

A project may have substantial revenue and still be financially poor if its costs, timing, or required rate of return are not considered correctly.

How money moves through a business

A company receives money by selling products or services. This money is invested in:

• Current assets

• Productive assets

• Cash holdings

• Plant and equipment

• Software

• Other resources needed to operate

Money then leaves the company through operating costs and investment costs, including employee salaries, suppliers, taxes, rent, and capital expenditure. Any remaining amount may be treated as profit.

Profit can be:

• Reinvested into the business

• Paid to shareholders as dividends

The appropriate dividend amount depends on the company's plans and the type of shares issued. The lecturer noted that preference shareholders may have a guaranteed dividend, whereas ordinary shareholders are not necessarily guaranteed one.

Compound interest and spending power

Compound interest means that interest is added to the original amount and subsequent interest is earned on the accumulated total.

The lecture example used:

• Initial amount: $100

• Interest rate: 10% per year

• Amount after two years: $133.10

This follows:

\[

100(1.10)^2 = 133.10

\]

The lecturer connected compounding with spending power. A $10 note may purchase more today than it will in 20 years because prices and the value of money change over time. Present-value calculations allow future money to be expressed in today's dollars, making financial decisions easier to interpret.

Future value and present value

The future value of a present amount is found by compounding it forward:

\[

FV = PV(1+R)^n

\]

where:

• \(FV\) is future value

• \(PV\) is present value

• \(R\) is the rate per period

• \(n\) is the number of periods, described in the lecture as the number of years

Rearranging gives the present value of a future amount:

\[

PV = \frac{FV}{(1+R)^n}

\]

The discount factor is:

\[

DF_n = \frac{1}{(1+R)^n}

\]

Therefore:

\[

PV = FV \times DF_n

\]

The lecturer emphasised that the discount factor is not the discount rate. Confusing these terms was identified as a common examination error.

The lecture also noted that some textbooks use different symbols and nomenclature. The exact alternative notation in the transcript is unclear, but the underlying calculation is the same.

Discount factor tables

Discount factor tables list the factor for different combinations of:

1. Identify the relevant year.

2. Identify the relevant discount rate.

3. Read or calculate the discount factor.

Net present value

NPV was described as the present value of the cash inflows minus the present value of the cash outflows. Equivalently, signed net cash flows can be discounted and summed across all project periods.

The practical process is:

1. Start with year 0.

2. Identify the discount rate.

3. Determine the cash flows for every year.

4. Identify revenue and expenses.

5. Calculate profit where required for tax calculations.

6. Calculate tax payable.

7. Calculate the net cash flow for each year.

8. Calculate the discount factor for each year.

9. Multiply each year's net cash flow by its discount factor.

10. Add all present-value cash flows to obtain the NPV.

The lecturer strongly emphasised starting at year 0. An initial investment generally occurs at year 0 and must be included. Starting at year 1 would omit or misplace the initial investment.

Interpreting NPV

At the selected discount rate:

• Positive NPV: the project is financially favourable under the model.

• Negative NPV: the project is financially unfavourable under the model.

• NPV equal to zero: the project exactly meets the required return represented by the discount rate.

When comparing mutually exclusive alternatives, NPV is useful because it focuses on the value created. The same assumptions must be applied consistently across the projects.

Discounted payback period

The discounted payback period is the time required for accumulated discounted cash flows to reach zero or become positive.

To determine it:

1. Calculate the discounted cash flow for each period.

2. Accumulate the discounted cash flows over time.

3. Identify the period in which the cumulative value crosses zero.

4. If necessary, interpolate between periods for a more precise estimate.

In the example, the cumulative discounted cash flow became positive between years 5 and 6. The lecturer stated that payback periods of approximately two to three years are often viewed as favourable, although companies may set their own target payback periods.

Ordinary payback period may ignore discounting, whereas discounted payback period accounts for the time value of money.

Internal rate of return

IRR is the discount rate that produces an NPV of zero:

\[

NPV(IRR) = 0

\]

A practical iterative approach is:

1. Build the NPV calculation.

2. Select a trial discount rate.

3. Calculate the NPV.

4. Adjust the discount rate.

5. Continue until the NPV is approximately zero.

A higher IRR is generally attractive because it indicates a higher rate of return. The lecture gave an example in which a project had an IRR of 7.78%, while an alternative investment was returning 9%; under that comparison, the 9% option would be preferred, subject to comparable risk and assumptions.

IRR has limitations:

• It can give misleading results for non-conventional or lumpy cash flows.

• Multiple sign changes in cash flows can produce unusual or multiple IRR results.

• It does not directly show the total dollar value created.

• A project with a higher IRR may have a lower NPV than another project.

Sensitivity studies

Sensitivity analysis is a form of pressure testing for the financial model. It examines how strongly the result depends on uncertain assumptions.

A basic method is:

1. Duplicate the financial model.

2. Change one variable.

3. Recalculate the NPV or other metric.

4. Compare the result with the base case.

5. Repeat for other variables.

Variables identified in the lecture included:

• Initial investment

• Discount rate

• Revenue

• Expenses

• Tax shielding

• Inflation

After individual-variable tests, multiple variables can be changed together to construct best-case and worst-case scenarios.

Sensitivity analysis helps identify:

• The assumptions with the greatest influence

• The acceptable range of outcomes

• The boundaries within which the project remains financially viable

• The uncertain or risky parts of the model

Choosing and communicating financial measures

The lecturer stated that many businesses use NPV as the main decision measure because it focuses on maximising value and returns. However, NPV does not necessarily indicate the most efficient use of company funds over time.

Other measures may be presented alongside NPV:

• Discounted payback period

• IRR

• Sensitivity results

These measures are not merely calculation steps for NPV; they can also be separate decision criteria used to inform a balanced decision.

When communicating a funding request:

• State the actual cash required in each relevant year.

• Do not present discounted values as if they were the amounts that must be paid in the future.

• Avoid mixing present-value figures and future cash requirements without labelling them clearly.

• State the project's NPV.

• Use discounted analysis rather than a simple undiscounted total.

• Include IRR or payback period when relevant to the organisation.

• Explain where the major uncertainties lie.

Equations and definitions

Compound interest

\[

FV = PV(1+R)^n

\]

• \(FV\): future value

• \(PV\): present value

• \(R\): interest or discount rate per period, depending on context

• \(n\): number of periods

Example:

\[

FV = 100(1+0.10)^2 = \$133.10

\]

Present value

\[

PV = \frac{FV}{(1+R)^n}

\]

This converts a future cash flow into its equivalent value at the present time.

Discount factor

\[

DF_n = \frac{1}{(1+R)^n}

\]

The discount factor is the multiplier applied to a future cash flow to obtain its present value.

\[

PV = FV \times DF_n

\]

The discount factor is not the same as the discount rate.

Net present value

Using signed net cash flows:

\[

NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{N} \frac{CF_t}{(1+R)^t}

\]

where:

• \(CF_t\): net cash flow in period \(t\)

• \(R\): discount rate

• \(t\): time period

• \(N\): final project period

Alternatively:

\[

NPV = \text{PV of cash inflows} - \text{PV of cash outflows}

\]

Interpretation:

• \(NPV > 0\): value is created at the selected discount rate.

• \(NPV < 0\): value is destroyed relative to the selected discount rate.

• \(NPV = 0\): the project exactly meets the selected required return.

Tax calculation in the lecture example

The lecture example used:

\[

\text{Tax payable} = \text{profit} \times \text{tax rate}

\]

with a stated New Zealand corporate tax rate of 28%. The lecturer also stated that tax is payable only when the relevant calculation produces a profit; in the example, the year-0 loss resulted in no tax payable.

Internal rate of return

\[

0 = \sum_{t=0}^{N} \frac{CF_t}{(1+\text{IRR})^t}

\]

IRR is the discount rate that makes the project's NPV equal to zero.

Discounted payback period

The discounted payback period is the time at which:

\[

\sum_{t=0}^{k} \frac{CF_t}{(1+R)^t} \geq 0

\]

It is determined from cumulative discounted cash flows rather than undiscounted cash flows.
