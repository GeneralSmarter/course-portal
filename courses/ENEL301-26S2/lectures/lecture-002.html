Overview

Lecture 2 focused on collaboration and teamwork as essential engineering capabilities. Engineering is presented as a socio-technical discipline: technical work depends on communication, relationships, culture, trust, and effective processes.

The lecture challenged the university emphasis on individual performance, noting that professional engineers spend substantial time working with others. Effective collaboration requires:

• A clearly shared objective.

• Deliberate use of differences in people’s knowledge, experiences, and ways of thinking.

• Purposeful technical and social interaction.

• Strong relationships built on trust.

• Explicit processes for communication, accountability, conflict, and quality assurance.

The lecture also revisited professional codes of conduct, using examples involving fraudulent professional claims and fictitious academic references to illustrate due process, professional obligations, and the possible consequences of misconduct.

Key concepts

• Engineering is collaborative: Engineers depend on clients, colleagues, specialists, stakeholders, and people outside their own discipline.

• The hidden curriculum: University assessment often rewards individual achievement, even though professional engineering depends heavily on collaboration.

• Collaboration is learned: It can be difficult and is shaped by upbringing, experience, personality, culture, and context.

• Engineering is socio-technical: Informal conversations, relationships, social interactions, and technical knowledge all contribute to engineering outcomes.

• Shared purpose: Team members must agree on the “same thing” they are trying to achieve.

• Productive difference: Effective teams identify and positively use differences in strengths, experiences, perspectives, cultures, and ways of thinking.

• Explicit and tacit knowledge: Written or formally communicated knowledge must be combined with knowledge gained through experience and social interaction.

• Trust and rapport: Relationships are necessary for engineering work, and relationships depend on trust.

• Communication medium matters: Calls and face-to-face conversations can carry contextual nuance that email and text omit.

• Task–relationship–process triangle: Collaboration can fail if the task is unclear, relationships are weak, or working processes are inadequate.

• Input–process–output model: Team outcomes depend on team and task inputs, collaboration processes, and emergent ways of working.

• Control versus influence: Some team conditions can be controlled, others only influenced, and some individual traits cannot realistically be changed.

• Outcome versus output: Engineering projects should be justified by the outcome or benefit produced, not merely by completion of a deliverable.

• Professional accountability: Codes of conduct are context-dependent and may be enforced through complaints, appeals, sanctions, or legal action.

• Due process: Suspected misconduct should be addressed through an established process, with evidence and an opportunity for the accused person to respond.

• Social licence to operate: Trust and cultural understanding contribute to whether society accepts an engineering activity or organisation.
