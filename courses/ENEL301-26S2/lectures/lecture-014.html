Detailed notes

The lecture began by positioning economic and social assessment within the three pillars of sustainability. Economic and social assessment are required parts of the course assignment, following earlier work on LCA.

Economic assessment

The lecturer defined the economy in terms of markets, purchasing, production, and the distribution of goods and services. This was distinguished from finance, which was described as being concerned primarily with cash and decisions about spending money.

An economy is fundamentally a market of buyers and sellers. Economic sectors are connected to one another, and understanding an economy requires examining these connections. The movement of money through economic sectors can lead to economic, social, and environmental outcomes. For example, spending in one sector can create income for another sector, which can then spend that income elsewhere.

The lecturer introduced the relationship between cost, price, and perceived value. A business needs to sell a product or service for more than it costs to produce. A customer considers a purchase good value when the perceived value is greater than the price paid.

GDP and value added

The lecture revisited GDP using a bread-production example:

• A farmer plants seeds and produces wheat.

• The farmer sells the wheat to a mill.

• The difference between the farmer’s production cost and the price received represents value added.

• GDP is calculated, using the production approach, as the sum of value added across an economy.

• The costs of inputs used to produce other goods are referred to as intermediate consumption.

Production does not necessarily mean producing a physical object. Software creation was given as an example of production that does not involve a conventional physical product.

Three GDP approaches were described:

• Production-based GDP: measures value added generated through production.

• Expenditure-based GDP: measures what people, organisations, or other economic actors purchase.

• Income-based GDP: measures income earned within the economy.

In a closed economy, where no money enters or leaves the country, these approaches should produce identical GDP values. In practice, international trade means that exports and imports affect the relationship between the measures. The lecturer stated that commonly reported GDP growth figures generally use the production-based approach, partly because it is easier to measure.

GDP is used as an economic health check. If inflation is under control, increasing GDP may indicate that people are financially better off and may be associated with positive social outcomes. However, this relationship is not guaranteed. Inequality can mean that overall GDP increases without equivalent benefits being distributed across the population.

The lecturer referred to previously discussed relationships between GDP and life expectancy. Some relationships may be correlations, while studies have also identified possible causal links between GDP and positive social outcomes. However, the lecture noted that the relationship between GDP and social outcomes may be weakening or decoupling in some recent global circumstances. The lecturer stated that the reasons were not yet fully understood.

A decrease in GDP may occur because:

• Industry produces less, reducing income and household spending.

• Households spend less, reducing demand and causing lower production.

The lecturer suggested that reduced household spending is often the second pathway, particularly when inflation reduces people’s purchasing power. The effects may also occur with a delay.

Limitations of GDP

The lecturer identified several limitations of GDP:

• GDP does not account for the wear and tear or depreciation of capital equipment.

• Net domestic product provides an alternative by accounting for depreciation.

• GDP does not measure living standards or well-being directly.

• GDP does not adequately account for environmental damage.

• Environmental impacts generally do not receive a direct monetary value in GDP, although mechanisms such as carbon pricing may introduce some monetary accounting.

Alternative or complementary indicators mentioned in the lecture included:

• Purchasing power parity (PPP): compares the spending power of people in different economies.

• The Big Mac Index: an accessible example related to purchasing power, based on how many Big Macs a person could purchase with their salary.

• The Human Development Index (HDI): includes measures related to health, life expectancy, and income.

• Gross national income (GNI) was also mentioned as another economic measure.

National input-output tables

The flow of money through an economy can be represented using a system of national accounts. New Zealand has national account input-output tables showing flows of money between economic sectors.

These tables show:

• Relationships between industries.

• Goods and services produced by industries.

• Industries that use those goods and services.

• The possible effects of policies on prices, wages, imports, and exports.

• How spending in one sector may flow through other sectors.

The lecture used a supermarket purchase as an example. A $10 purchase at Pak’nSave can be traced through the economy to examine where the money goes and what consequences the spending may have.

New Zealand supply and use tables were discussed:

• Use tables show what different sectors purchase or use.

Economic assessment process

A general economic-assessment process is:

1. Define the scope.

2. State what is included and excluded.

3. Identify the relevant economic sector.

4. Identify the amount of money to be spent.

5. Trace where the money goes.

6. Estimate economic impacts and benefits.

7. Communicate the findings.

Economic assessment can be used to estimate effects such as the number of jobs associated with an investment.

The lecture introduced a New Zealand economic-impact calculator called the Matatahi or Matatihi Impact Calculator. The calculator requires users to identify:

• The amount of money invested.

It can then estimate effects such as:

• Direct value added.

• Supply-chain impacts.

Social assessment

Social assessment was presented as having less standardised guidance than economic assessment. The lecturer recommended completing the economic assessment and LCA first because they can help reveal possible social impacts.

The purpose of social assessment is to forecast or predict possible social outcomes. The lecturer suggested an approach similar to risk assessment.

A social-assessment process may include:

1. Define what is included and excluded.

2. Establish a system boundary.

3. Expand the boundary if further investigation identifies additional relevant impacts.

4. Identify and map stakeholders.

5. Identify possible social outcomes for each stakeholder.

6. Gather evidence through stakeholder engagement and literature.

7. Use systems-thinking methods such as causal-loop diagrams.

8. Link outcomes to relevant indicators and SDG targets.

9. Analyse benefits and risks where required.

10. Consider mitigation methods where appropriate.

A system-boundary diagram can help identify all relevant parts of the system and the stakeholders connected to it. The deeper the investigation goes, the more indirect impacts and stakeholders may be identified.

Risk, uncertainty, and mitigation

The lecture described social-risk analysis in terms of likelihood and consequence, although the lecturer stated that students did not need to complete this analysis or identify mitigation techniques for the assignment.

Social-LCA databases can identify possible risks in a supply chain, such as child-labour or exploitation risks associated with a country. However, the result is an indicator of potential risk rather than proof that a specific supplier is causing the impact. Actual supplier information and additional evidence are needed to establish whether the risk is present.

Possible mitigation methods may include selecting suppliers or materials with verified conflict-free credentials. The lecturer expressed uncertainty about how reliable some such claims are, indicating that certification or supplier claims should be scrutinised.

Social trade-offs and externalities

The lecture emphasised that LCA, economic, and social outcomes can involve trade-offs. An infrastructure project may provide benefits such as improved internet access while also creating social risks elsewhere in its supply chain.

A user may benefit directly from a data centre without being aware of upstream impacts associated with minerals, manufacturing, or labour conditions. The lecturer presented this as an example of an unintended social impact.

Some social impacts can be assigned a monetary value. Economists refer to this as pricing externalities. Putting a dollar value on social outcomes can allow them to be included in an economic calculation, but the lecture acknowledged that this can be ethically difficult, particularly when attempting to value serious harm or human life.
