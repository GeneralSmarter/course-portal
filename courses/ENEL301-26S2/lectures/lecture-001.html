Concise overview

The lecture introduced ENEL301 as preparation for professional engineering practice rather than a conventional technical-design paper. The course connects ethical conduct, collaboration, financial and project viability, risk management, sustainability, law, intellectual property, and stakeholder management. The lecturer then covered course logistics and assessment rules before beginning an ethics discussion built around professional consequences, engineering-failure cases, and three broad ethical approaches: rule-based/deontological, consequence-based/utilitarian, and virtue-based reasoning.

Core concepts

Professional engineering is broader than technical correctness

Engineers must be able to assess whether a project is viable, manage risk and stakeholders, work effectively with other people, and understand the social, environmental, financial, and ethical consequences of technical decisions. The lecture cited project failure and financial ruin as reasons these skills matter, including the claim that roughly one in six projects ends in financial disaster.

Ethics, law, and professional responsibility

Legal compliance is a minimum boundary, not a complete ethical method. The lecture framed three useful orientations:

1. Deontological or rule-based reasoning: follow duties, laws, and professional codes.

2. Consequentialist or utilitarian reasoning: compare likely outcomes and seek the greatest overall benefit, while recognising that apparently beneficial outcomes can still impose serious harm on a minority.

3. Virtue ethics: ask what an honest, courageous, compassionate, and professionally responsible engineer would do.

No single framework automatically resolves every dilemma. A defensible decision should identify affected parties, test the decision against duties and codes, compare consequences, and examine the character and professional values expressed by the choice.

Failure cases as ethical evidence

• Therac-25: software/system failures led to radiation overdoses, deaths, and serious injuries. The case illustrates that software behaviour, interface design, testing, and safety controls can have direct physical consequences.

• Boeing 737 MAX: the lecture linked mechanical and software design failures to two fatal crashes. The broader lesson is that engineering decisions exist inside organisational cultures and commercial pressures.

These cases should be used to practise identifying technical failure modes, organisational contributors, affected stakeholders, and the professional duties that were neglected.

Collaboration and communication

The course treats collaboration as a major determinant of project success. Communication problems, poor culture, and weak stakeholder coordination can defeat technically sound work. Tutorials are intentionally interactive and are not recorded, so attendance is part of the learning design rather than merely administration.
