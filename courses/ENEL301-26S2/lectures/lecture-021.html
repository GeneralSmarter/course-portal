Detailed notes

1. Business processes and value creation

A business transforms inputs into products or services that customers are willing to buy.

Inputs may include:

• People

• Financial resources

• Money

• Data

• Information

These inputs are transformed through business processes into products and services. If the outputs create value, customers may pay for them.

Engineering opportunities commonly involve:

• Improving internal processes

• Improving productivity

• Reducing the cost of intermediate consumption

• Developing new products

• Improving services

• Identifying better customer groups or markets

Improving a process can reduce the cost of intermediate consumption. The lecture revisited the earlier wheat, flour, and farmer example to illustrate how process improvements can increase value by reducing input costs.

Strategy was described as a series of decisions, or a process, used to achieve organisational objectives. A basic strategic cycle is:

1. Analyse the situation.

2. Decide where the business wants to go.

3. Make a plan.

4. Implement the plan.

5. Monitor and review performance.

6. Continue improving.

Opportunity analysis supports this process by helping a business determine which possible ideas deserve further investment.

2. Meaning of an opportunity

An opportunity is more than an interesting idea. It requires favourable circumstances that create a need for a product, service, or business.

Businesses should avoid pursuing ideas merely because they appear novel or exciting. They should focus on ideas that add value for customers and satisfy the opportunity mix.

The five conditions in the opportunity mix are:

#### Customer value

The idea must create or add value for the customer. A product that is technically impressive but does not solve a meaningful customer problem may not represent a viable opportunity.

#### Market readiness

Customers must exist and be ready to pay. A product may be useful but fail because it is introduced at the wrong time or in the wrong location.

The lecture used early personal digital assistants as an example. The lecturer’s point was that the technology may have been useful, but the market was not ready. Later, the iPhone entered a market that was more receptive to a touchscreen, mobile computing device.

#### Strategic fit

The opportunity must fit the business’s capabilities, direction, and strategy. A business should consider whether the opportunity aligns with its existing resources, knowledge, customers, and objectives.

#### Profitability within a reasonable period

The expected rewards must justify the risk, and the opportunity should become profitable within a reasonable time. A company should not have to wait an excessive number of years before the investment generates returns.

#### Technical feasibility

The product or service must be technically achievable. It does not need to be perfect at the first attempt, but the business must have a credible path to producing and delivering it.

4. Customers, needs, and problem definition

A customer was defined using three conditions:

1. The person or organisation has a problem and wants it solved.

2. They have money available to address the problem.

3. They are willing or authorised to spend that money on a solution.

Not every person with a problem is therefore a customer. A potential customer must also have purchasing capacity and authority.

However, customers do not always know or articulate the need that a new technology could satisfy. Some products create or reveal an unknown need.

The iPhone was used as an example of a product that many people did not explicitly request before it existed but adopted once they experienced its capabilities.

When analysing a potential opportunity, ask:

• What problem is being solved?

• Who experiences the problem?

• How severe or frequent is it?

• What alternatives are currently used?

• Who pays for the solution?

• Who is authorised to purchase it?

• Is the proposed product solving the customer’s underlying problem or only copying an existing solution?

A market is a group of buyers considering a range of products or services that could solve their problems.

Examples of markets include:

• The medical market

• The legal market

• The fruit and vegetable market

A segment is a subsection of a market whose members share common traits, needs, or behaviours.

Examples include:

• Private hospitals

• Different types of law firms

• Households seeking home-delivered fruit and vegetables

• Cafés seeking commercial produce suppliers

Segments may need different products, pricing, distribution methods, or marketing approaches.

6. Technology adoption life cycle

Customers can be classified according to when they adopt a technology:

1. Innovators

2. Early adopters

3. Early majority

4. Late majority

5. Laggards

Innovators are willing to test new or incomplete products. In software, they may test beta versions.

Early adopters purchase new products before the wider market. They may have disposable income, strong interest in new technology, or a willingness to accept risk.

The early majority represents the beginning of broader market uptake. Evidence that early adopters are successfully using a technology can help a product cross the chasm into the early majority.

The late majority is more cautious and waits until a technology is well established.

Laggards adopt very slowly or may never adopt. The lecturer used people who refuse to buy a smartphone as an informal example.

The adoption life cycle can be converted into a cumulative adoption S-curve. The lecturer stated that adoption curves have become narrower over recent centuries, meaning that the interval between initial experimentation and broad adoption is shortening.

ChatGPT was mentioned as an example of an extremely rapid adoption curve. This should be treated as the lecturer’s illustrative claim rather than a quantitative conclusion established in the lecture.

A business may also price cheaply to cross the chasm, attract a larger user base, and increase prices later.

This creates a commercial risk: customers may eventually leave if price increases exceed the value they receive or if switching costs fall.

9. Porter’s five forces

Porter’s five forces analyse the factors shaping competition within an industry.

The five forces are:

1. Threat of new entrants

2. Bargaining power of buyers

3. Threat of substitutes

4. Bargaining power of suppliers

5. Rivalry among existing competitors

The lecturer used flat-pack furniture and the Warehouse Group as an example of how a new entrant can affect industry structure.

#### Threat of new entrants

A new entrant can increase competition and reduce the market share or margins of existing businesses.

The arrival of IKEA in New Zealand was used as an example. The Warehouse Group would need to consider the threat posed by a large, established international competitor entering the flat-pack furniture market.

#### Bargaining power of buyers

Buyers have greater bargaining power when they have more alternatives or when competing suppliers make it easy to compare prices and features.

If IKEA enters a market previously served by only a small number of retailers, buyers may receive more choice and better value for money. This increases buyer power.

#### Threat of substitutes

A substitute is a different product or service that solves a similar customer problem.

If IKEA can provide products that fulfil the same need as the Warehouse Group’s furniture, the two offerings are substitutable. A high threat of substitutes can limit pricing power.

#### Bargaining power of suppliers

Suppliers have greater bargaining power when a business depends heavily on a small number of suppliers or when alternative sources are difficult to obtain.

In the example, the lecturer suggested that IKEA may use different suppliers, so its entry might not immediately change the Warehouse Group’s supplier bargaining power.

#### Rivalry among existing competitors

Rivalry increases when competitors fight for the same customers through price, product range, service, marketing, or convenience.

Increased rivalry generally places downward pressure on profit margins. A business facing a major new competitor may need to act quickly to preserve its position.

10. Estimating market size

After analysing the environment and competition, a business must estimate how much it could sell.

Market-size estimation can use:

• Top-down estimates

• Bottom-up estimates

• Publicly available data

• Industry reports

• Census data

• Competitor numbers

• Forecast changes

• Local population data

• Comparable businesses

A top-down estimate begins with a broad market and progressively narrows it to the relevant geography, customer segment, product category, and likely market share.

The lecture used a hypothetical fruit-box delivery business in Rangiora:

• Broad market: Fruit and vegetables

• Potential customer segments: Households and cafés

• Delivery segment: Home delivery

• Excluded channels: Wholesale and a physical shopfront

• Possible data sources: IBISWorld, census data, and statistical information

• Further adjustment: Local competition and the number of existing providers

A market estimate should not be accepted without a reasonableness check. The lecturer suggested comparing the number of comparable businesses in Christchurch with the population they serve. If approximately 11 businesses operate in a population of approximately 412,000, this gives a rough benchmark of around 37,500 people per business. This does not mean each business actually serves exactly that number; it is only a sense check.

The estimate should account for:

• Existing competitors

• Customer purchasing behaviour

• Local geography

• Forecast population changes

• Price and inflation

• The proportion of the market that can realistically be captured

11. TAM, SAM, and SOM

The transcript renders this framework as “Tams Samson,” but the intended terms are clearly TAM, SAM, and SOM:

• TAM: Total available market. The entire market for the relevant product or service.

• SAM: Serviceable available market. The portion of TAM that could potentially be served by the business’s product, geography, business model, or capabilities.

• SOM: Serviceable obtainable market. The portion of SAM that the business can realistically capture.

The framework progressively narrows the estimate:

TAM → SAM → SOM

The resulting market size can be expressed as:

• Number of potential customers

• Annual units sold

• Annual revenue

• Other relevant commercial measures

SOM is the most useful figure for practical planning because it reflects the customers the business can realistically reach and win, not merely the size of the entire theoretical market.

12. SWOT analysis and strategic fit

SWOT analysis evaluates whether an opportunity fits the business strategically.

The four categories are:

• Strengths: Internal capabilities and advantages.

• Weaknesses: Internal limitations or areas where the business is not strong.

• Opportunities: External possibilities that the business could exploit.

• Threats: External factors that could harm the business.

Strengths and weaknesses are generally internal. Opportunities and threats are generally external.

Threats may be identified using:

• PESTLE analysis

• Porter’s five forces

• Other external-environment analysis

The lecturer described the strategic use of SWOT as:

• Use strengths to capitalise on opportunities.

• Use strengths to suppress or reduce threats.

• Identify weaknesses that could prevent the business from pursuing the opportunity.

• Determine whether weaknesses must be addressed before proceeding.

The business must also check whether it has enough resources, including:

• Money

• People

• Skills

• Capability

• Time

• Capacity to complete the work

Having people available is not sufficient. They must have both the capability to perform the work and the capacity or time to do it.

Because external conditions change, opportunity analysis must be repeated. Businesses should continually monitor and review PESTLE factors, competitive forces, customer behaviour, and resource constraints.
