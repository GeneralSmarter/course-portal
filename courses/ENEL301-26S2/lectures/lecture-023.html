1. Types of decisions

The lecture introduced a framework identified in the transcript as “Kernavan” or “Carnavan”. This is almost certainly a reference to the Cynefin framework, although the automated transcript does not capture the term reliably.

The framework divides decisions into four broad categories:

1. Simple or business-as-usual decisions

• Routine decisions.

• Low uncertainty.

• Usually based on experience.

• Made frequently to keep normal operations flowing.

• The relationship between cause and effect is generally understood.

2. Complicated decisions

• Often long-term decisions.

• Require analysis and expert knowledge.

• Usually involve greater risk and resource commitments than routine decisions.

• The relationship between cause and effect can be understood, but specialist expertise may be required.

3. Complex decisions

• Associated with “unknown unknowns”.

• Cause and effect can generally only be understood retrospectively.

• There may be no single objectively correct answer.

• Decision-makers need to learn and adapt as the situation develops.

4. Chaotic decisions

• There is no clear cause-and-effect relationship.

• The appropriate response is to act in order to stabilise the situation and manage outcomes.

• Traditional analysis may not be useful until some order has been restored.

Different quadrants require different decision-making methods. The lecture focused mainly on the complicated and strategically significant area rather than routine or chaotic decisions.

• Competing strategic objectives.

5. Enterprise risk management

Enterprise risk management (ERM) extends risk management from an individual engineering activity or project to the organisation as a whole.

ERM concerns how an organisation can:

• Identify and manage risks.

• Identify and seize opportunities.

• Support the achievement of strategic objectives.

• Protect business-as-usual operations where necessary.

The lecture described ERM as being similar to the ISO 31000 risk-management approach, but applied at an enterprise level.

A simplified ERM structure includes:

• Corporate strategy.

• Risk governance.

• Risk appetite statement.

• Risk frameworks and policies.

7. Bias and group decision-making

Biases are inclinations or prejudices that affect judgement. They may appear as:

• Conscious preferences.

• Stereotypes.

• Unconscious assumptions.

• Preferences for a desired outcome.

• Optimism about a project’s prospects.

Optimism bias can cause people to search for reasons why a project should proceed rather than seriously considering whether it should be rejected.

Group decision-making introduces additional effects:

• Individuals may behave differently within a group.

• The group can influence individual attitudes and behaviour.

• Individuals can also influence the group.

• Groups may default to a middle position rather than select the best option.

• Groupthink may discourage people from challenging a poor decision.

The lecturer referred to an example in which engineers reportedly believed that a product opportunity was not worth pursuing but proceeded anyway. The lecturer suggested that groupthink may have contributed, although this was presented as speculation rather than a confirmed diagnosis.

Good group processes and risk governance should help make it easier for people to:

• Raise concerns.

• Challenge assumptions.

• Present dissenting technical information.

• Avoid conformity-driven decisions.

• Separate evidence from group pressure.

8. Certainty and decision difficulty

Decision-making can also be viewed on a spectrum:

• At one end, alternatives and outcomes are well known.

• At the other end, alternatives and outcomes are uncertain.

• Moving toward uncertainty increases the difficulty of decision-making.

In engineering projects, decision-makers generally begin with uncertain information. As the project progresses:

• Information improves.

• Uncertainty decreases.

• Confidence in the decision increases.

• More resources are committed.

• The value at risk increases.

• Decision authority may move to different people or governance levels.

Projects commonly use multiple screening and decision points to progressively reduce uncertainty. These may be structured as gated processes or stage-gate reviews.

A key tension is that increased certainty does not necessarily mean reduced exposure. By the time a decision is well supported, substantial money and effort may already have been committed.

9. Risk appetite

Risk appetite was described as an organisation’s ability and willingness to take risk in pursuit of a strategy or growth.

Risk appetite balances two dimensions:

• Ability: whether the organisation has the financial and operational capacity to take the risk.

• Willingness: whether the organisation is prepared to tolerate the risk.

Ability may involve:

• Cost-benefit analysis.

• Forecast financial capacity.

• Available resources.

• The organisation’s ability to absorb losses.

Willingness may involve:

• Risk tolerance.

• Strategic priorities.

• Governance expectations.

• Ethical or legal limits.

• The organisation’s stated level of acceptable exposure.

A risk appetite statement specifies the levels and types of risk that an organisation:

• Is willing to accept.

• Is unwilling to accept.

• Must reject.

The lecture characterised the opportunity side as relating more to the organisation’s ability and the risk side as relating more to its willingness or tolerance. Risk appetite attempts to balance these factors.

11. Value at risk and Monte Carlo simulation

Value at risk (VaR) was presented as a probabilistic measure of financial exposure.

In the lecture’s terms, VaR asks:

• What loss in value is expected within a specified confidence level?

• Over what time period?

• Under normal market conditions?

• What is the maximum loss the organisation is willing to tolerate within that confidence limit?

Monte Carlo simulation can be used to estimate VaR.

A simplified process is:

1. Assign uncertainty distributions or ranges to uncertain inputs.

2. Select values for the inputs.

3. Calculate the project result, such as NPV.

4. Repeat the calculation many times using different sampled inputs.

5. Examine the resulting distribution of outcomes.

6. Identify the relevant loss threshold at the selected confidence level.

The “Monte Carlo” name comes from the casino analogy: uncertain variables are repeatedly “rolled” within their possible ranges, and the result is recalculated each time.

The lecture example used:

• A sensitivity-analysis range from −$15 million to +$25 million.

• A baseline NPV of $5 million.

• A Monte Carlo result in which 5% of simulations lost at least $10 million.

• A 30% probability of a negative NPV.

The VaR statement was expressed as a 5% VaR of −$10 million. In practical terms, the lecture interpreted this as approximately:

• A 1-in-20 chance of losing $10 million or more.

• Approximately a 1-in-3 chance that the project loses money.

The transcript contains an inconsistent phrase, “1 in 20% chance”. The intended interpretation is almost certainly “1 in 20”, corresponding to 5%.

Decision-makers must compare this exposure with the organisation’s risk appetite. They must decide whether the potential baseline return is worth accepting the probability and magnitude of loss.

13. Kepner-Tregoe decision process

The Kepner-Tregoe (KT) process was presented as a more nuanced version of a weighted decision matrix.

Regardless of the specific decision framework, two initial steps are critical:

1. Define the question.

2. Set the decision criteria.

The KT process then separates criteria into:

• Musts: mandatory requirements.

• Wants: desirable but non-mandatory requirements.

A typical process is:

1. Define exactly what decision is being made.

2. Identify the must-have criteria.

3. Eliminate any option that fails a must-have criterion.

4. Identify the wants or nice-to-have criteria.

5. Assign each want a weighting, commonly on a 1-to-10 scale.

6. Score each remaining option against each want.

7. Multiply scores by the relevant weights.

8. Add the weighted scores.

9. Compare the options.

10. Check that the result is consistent with stakeholder needs and strategic objectives.

Quantitative results, such as NPV or VaR, may be converted to a common 1-to-10 scale for comparison, provided that the scoring method is applied consistently.

Example criteria mentioned included:

• Strategic fit.

• Market readiness.

• Customer value.

• Risk.

• Financial performance.

• Sustainability.

• Resource requirements.

• Technical feasibility.

• Time.

• Stakeholder priorities.

The lecturer’s example included:

• A first criterion that operated as a go/no-go requirement.

• Options that passed or failed this requirement.

• Weighted scoring of the remaining options.

• Rejection of option C because it failed the mandatory criterion.

• Selection of option B as the highest-scoring remaining option.

Equations and definitions

Net present value

\[

NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}

\]

Where:

• \(CF_t\) is the cash flow in period \(t\).

• \(r\) is the discount rate.

• \(t\) is the time period.

• \(n\) is the final period.

Simplified decision rule stated in the lecture:

\[

NPV > 0 \Rightarrow \text{potentially accept the investment}

\]

This rule must still be considered alongside risk, strategy, constraints, and organisational risk appetite.

Internal rate of return

IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV equal to zero:

\[

0 = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+IRR)^t}

\]

The lecture stated that a high IRR is favourable, particularly when compared with a hurdle rate or WACC.

Payback period

Payback period is the time required for cumulative cash inflows to recover the initial investment.

A shorter payback period generally means lower exposure to timing risk, although payback does not capture all cash flows or the full profitability of a project.

Weighted average cost of capital

A commonly used WACC equation is:

\[

WACC =

\frac{E}{D+E}R_e+

\frac{D}{D+E}R_d(1-T)

\]

Where:

• \(E\) is the market value of equity.

• \(D\) is the market value of debt.

• \(R_e\) is the cost of equity.

• \(R_d\) is the cost of debt.

• \(T\) is the relevant tax rate.

The lecturer stated that students should know the WACC formula, its components, and how to apply it, but would not be required to calculate beta.

Value at risk

VaR describes a loss threshold associated with a specified confidence level and time horizon under stated conditions.

A result described as a 5% VaR of \(-\$10\) million means that, according to the model and its assumptions, approximately 5% of outcomes involve a loss of at least $10 million.

The exact sign convention can vary. The important elements are:

• Loss amount.

• Confidence level or tail probability.

• Time period.

• Assumptions, including normal market conditions.

Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo simulation repeatedly samples uncertain inputs and recalculates the output.

Conceptually:

\[

Y_i = f(X_{1,i}, X_{2,i}, \ldots, X_{k,i})

\]

Where:

• \(X_{j,i}\) is the sampled value of uncertain input \(j\) in simulation \(i\).

• \(Y_i\) is the resulting project outcome for simulation \(i\).

• \(f\) is the project or financial model.

The collection of \(Y_i\) values forms an estimated distribution of possible outcomes.

Weighted decision matrix

For an option \(j\), a weighted score can be represented as:

\[

S_j = \sum_{i=1}^{m} w_i s_{ij}

\]

Where:

• \(w_i\) is the weight assigned to criterion \(i\).

• \(s_{ij}\) is the score of option \(j\) against criterion \(i\).

• \(S_j\) is the total weighted score for option \(j\).

Mandatory “must” criteria are normally applied first. An option that fails a must-have requirement is rejected regardless of its weighted score on the remaining criteria.

Risk appetite

Risk appetite is the organisation’s stated ability and willingness to accept risk in pursuit of strategic objectives.

It should be distinguished from:

• Risk capacity: the maximum risk the organisation can absorb.

• Risk tolerance: the acceptable variation around objectives.

• Individual risk exposure: the risk associated with a particular project or decision.

The lecture primarily emphasised the organisation’s ability and willingness to take risk.

Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative that is forgone when a decision is made.

For example, declining a good opportunity may create an opportunity cost, while pursuing a poor opportunity may consume resources that could have been used elsewhere.
