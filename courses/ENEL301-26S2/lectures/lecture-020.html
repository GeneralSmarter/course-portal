Key concepts

• Risk arises from uncertainty and concerns anything with the potential to cause harm or jeopardise an achievement or objective.

• Risk cannot be completely eliminated. It can be reduced, controlled, shared, transferred, or accepted.

• Risk management is broader than risk assessment.

• Risk assessment consists of:

1. Risk identification.

2. Risk analysis.

3. Risk evaluation.

• Risk management also includes establishing context, treating risks, communicating and consulting, monitoring and reviewing, and documenting decisions.

• The risk-management method must be defined before individual risks are scored.

• Risk identification should distinguish between:

• The risk or event.

• Its source or cause.

• Its possible consequences.

• A consequence must not be confused with the event or cause that produces it.

• Likelihood may be estimated using:

Detailed notes

Purpose of risk management

Risk management helps engineers deal with incomplete information, uncertain outcomes, and situations where there is no single clearly correct design choice.

The lecturer linked risk management to:

• Sustainable use of resources.

• Managing uncertainty.

• Improving confidence in engineering decisions.

• Increasing the value delivered by a project or design.

The lecture contrasted the simplified view held by novice engineers with engineering practice. Novice engineers may assume that problems are well defined, that one solution exists, and that complete information is available. In practice, engineering projects often require estimates and approximations, and the available information may be incomplete or ambiguous.

Uncertainty is not limited to numerical probability. It can also arise from:

• How people interpret information.

• How people behave.

• Incomplete knowledge.

• Organisational decisions.

• External events.

• Changing project conditions.

Scope of risk

Risk may apply to many categories, including:

• Technical risks.

• Health and safety risks.

• Environmental risks.

• Financial risks.

• Reputational risks.

• Risks to people.

• Risks to property.

• Risks to project schedules and plans.

• Risks outside the organisation’s direct control.

The same general risk-management techniques can be applied across these categories, although the criteria, stakeholders, consequences, and treatment methods may differ.

Risk is always present. The lecturer used the possibility that the sun might not rise the following day as an extreme example of a risk with an extremely small likelihood. The practical point was that engineers should avoid claiming that risk has been eliminated entirely.

A plan is required rather than relying on hope. A statement such as “I hope this will work” does not demonstrate that risks have been identified or controlled. A defensible engineering approach should identify possible failures and define measures to mitigate or control them.

Establishing the context

The first step is to establish the context. This means defining:

• The background to the project.

• Why risk management is being performed.

• The objectives and scope of the assessment.

• The project history.

• Relevant relationships and stakeholders.

• The social, political, legal, economic, technical, and environmental issues associated with the project.

• Who is responsible for each part of the process.

• How the assessment will be undertaken.

• The risk criteria and acceptance thresholds.

Establishing context is often skipped. A common error is to move directly to a likelihood-consequence chart without first explaining:

• What is being assessed.

• Why it is being assessed.

• Which criteria will be used.

• Who will make decisions.

• How risks will be reviewed and controlled.

Risk criteria should be established before the risks are scored. If thresholds are chosen after seeing the scores, assessors may unconsciously alter the criteria to avoid taking action.

Risk management versus risk assessment

Risk assessment is a subset of risk management.

Risk assessment consists of:

1. Risk identification.

2. Risk analysis.

3. Risk evaluation.

Risk management additionally includes:

• Establishing context.

• Selecting and implementing treatments.

• Assigning responsibility.

• Communicating and consulting.

• Monitoring and reviewing.

• Recording and documenting the process.

Risk identification

For each risk, identify:

• The risk or undesirable event.

• The source or cause.

• The possible consequences.

• Whether the source is internal or external.

• Whether the organisation can control the source.

Categorising risks can make identification more systematic. For example, risks may be grouped under finance, health and safety, technical performance, environment, or other relevant categories.

The lecturer stressed the importance of defining the risk precisely. A vague statement such as “the system fails” is less useful than a description that identifies the relevant event, cause, and consequence.

A common failure in risk assessments is to describe a consequence as though it were the risk itself. For example:

• “A carriage catches fire” describes an event or consequence, but does not identify the causes of ignition.

• “A person slips on spilled oil” describes an accident outcome, but the assessment should also consider why the oil spill occurred.

The analysis should move further upstream and identify the conditions or failures that could produce the event.

Existing risk assessments may be available for commonly used components or materials, such as lithium batteries. These documents can be useful starting points, but they must be checked against the actual design. A different battery pack, operating environment, configuration, or use case may invalidate assumptions in the existing assessment.

Risk analysis: likelihood

Likelihood describes how probable or frequent an event is expected to be.

Two approaches discussed were:

• Forecasting: estimating the chance that the event will occur in the future.

• Backcasting or reflective analysis: examining how frequently the event occurred in the past.

Forecasting is difficult because the future cannot be predicted perfectly. Historical analysis can also be misleading. An event that has not occurred recently may still be possible, particularly if previous controls prevented it or if the event is rare.

The lecturer used rail-industry experience to illustrate this problem. If a train derailment has not occurred in ten years, that absence may reflect effective controls or good fortune rather than a genuinely negligible likelihood.

Potential ways to reduce bias include:

• Considering evidence from outside the organisation.

• Reviewing international experience.

• Examining whether similar events have occurred elsewhere.

• Using both forecasting and historical approaches where appropriate.

• Applying conservative judgement for high-consequence events.

Likelihood scales are commonly ordinal, such as 1 to 5 or 1 to 10. The exact scale and definitions must be established during context-setting. The numbers are not inherently meaningful unless the organisation defines what each level represents.

Risk analysis: consequence

Consequence describes what happens if the risk event occurs.

A consequence scale may range from:

• Minor annoyance or negligible loss.

• Small injury or limited disruption.

• Significant injury, property damage, or financial loss.

• Major disruption or severe injury.

• Catastrophic outcomes, potentially including multiple fatalities or financial ruin.

Organisations may attach quantitative thresholds to consequences, including financial values. The value assigned to a “large” financial loss depends on the size and circumstances of the organisation.

Consequence categories may include:

• Harm to people.

• Damage to property.

• Environmental damage.

• Financial loss.

• Reputational damage.

• Delay or failure to achieve project objectives.

Risk evaluation and risk matrices

After likelihood and consequence have been assessed, the risk is evaluated against pre-established criteria.

A common approach is to calculate a risk score and then classify it into categories such as:

• Low.

• Medium.

• High.

• Extreme.

A risk matrix may use colour coding to display these categories. The number of categories and thresholds depends on the organisation’s governance system and risk tolerance.

The lecturer gave the following general scoring approach:

Risk score = likelihood score × consequence score

The lecturer criticised an alternative approach used by a former organisation in which likelihood and consequence were added rather than multiplied. Addition may fail to distinguish adequately between combinations involving catastrophic consequences and combinations involving only moderate outcomes.

Risk thresholds determine the action required. Extreme risks may be escalated to senior management or the board. The responsible level depends on the organisation’s risk tolerance and governance structure.

Risk treatment and mitigation

Once risks have been evaluated, appropriate treatments are selected and implemented.

Possible treatment strategies include:

• Reducing the likelihood.

• Reducing the consequence.

• Sharing or transferring the risk.

• Accepting the risk when further treatment is not reasonably practicable or justified.

Stakeholders should be consulted when selecting a treatment. A mitigation measure may not work as intended or may introduce additional hazards.

The lecture gave personal protective equipment, guards, and other safety controls as examples that can themselves introduce risks if they obstruct movement, reduce visibility, complicate work, or create new failure modes.

The preferred order discussed was:

1. Reduce or eliminate the likelihood where practicable.

2. Reduce the consequences if the event still occurs.

3. Share or transfer the remaining consequences where appropriate.

4. Accept the remaining risk when no further reasonable treatment is available.

A train flyover was used as an example of reducing the likelihood of a collision by separating road and rail paths. The collision risk is not necessarily literally zero, but the particular interaction between road traffic and trains is greatly reduced.

Examples of methods for reducing likelihood include:

• Training and education.

• Preventive maintenance.

• Equipment maintenance.

• Auditing.

• Quality assurance.

• Process changes.

• Improved procedures.

• Design changes.

Examples of methods for reducing consequences include:

• Safety glasses.

• Physical barriers.

• Protective equipment.

• Emergency response measures.

• Recovery plans.

• Controls that limit the spread or severity of an incident.

The lecturer described sharing financial consequences through contracts as another treatment approach. A project contract may allocate financial risk between the contractor, client, and other parties. The Tauranga carpark example was mentioned as a case in which financial consequences associated with a technical problem were placed substantially on the client through the contract. The lecture presented this as an example of risk allocation, not necessarily as an ethically or commercially desirable arrangement.

Residual risk

After controls have been implemented, the risk must be reassessed.

Residual risk is the risk remaining after mitigation.

Residual risk score = likelihood after mitigation × consequence after mitigation

The residual risk is the basis for deciding whether further action is required. The lecturer gave an example in which a residual score greater than 10 required immediate action. This threshold was presented as an example from the material and should not be treated as universal.

Risk registers commonly record both:

• Initial or raw risk.

• Residual risk after treatment.

The lecturer stressed that action thresholds should be applied to the residual risk. A high initial score may be acceptable after effective treatment, while a moderate initial score may remain unacceptable if the proposed control does not reduce the risk sufficiently.

Risk management plans and registers

A risk management plan documents:

• The project context.

• The purpose and scope of the assessment.

• The assessment method.

• The likelihood and consequence scales.

• The risk matrix and thresholds.

• The responsibilities and governance arrangements.

• The risk-treatment approach.

• The calculation method.

• The communication, monitoring, and review process.

A risk register records individual risks. Typical fields include:

• Risk identification number.

• Risk description.

• Date reviewed.

• Cause or source.

• Consequence.

• Likelihood.

• Initial risk score.

• Proposed controls.

• Person responsible for the risk.

• Monitoring or verification method.

• Residual likelihood.

• Residual consequence.

• Residual risk score.

• Risk classification.

• Further actions.

Risk-management activities should be included in the project work breakdown structure. They are project work, not optional administrative activities to be performed only at the end.

Controls must also be verified. Training, procedures, certification, assessment, and auditing can be used to check whether a control has actually been implemented and remains effective.

Boeing 737 MAX case study

The Boeing 737 MAX case was used to illustrate that risk is not only a technical calculation. It involves design decisions, commercial pressure, communication, training, regulatory oversight, organisational culture, and governance.

The lecture’s account was:

• Airbus introduced the A320neo with a larger and more fuel-efficient engine.

• Boeing needed to compete while preserving the 737’s existing market position.

• The 737 was lower to the ground than the A320, creating less room for a larger engine beneath the wing.

• Boeing moved the 737 MAX engine position higher and further forward to accommodate the engine.

• This altered the aircraft’s behaviour at high thrust and high angle of attack, tending to pitch the nose upward.

• Boeing introduced the Manoeuvring Characteristics Augmentation System, or MCAS, to automatically command nose-down trim under certain conditions.

• The aircraft was marketed as sufficiently similar to the previous 737 that extensive additional pilot training was avoided.

• The lecture stated that training materials did not adequately explain MCAS and that many pilots received only limited additional training.

• Faulty or incorrect sensor data could cause MCAS to command nose-down movement.

• The lecture highlighted the absence of sufficient sensor redundancy as a critical technical weakness.

• Lion Air Flight 610 crashed in October 2018, killing 189 people.

• Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed in March 2019, killing 157 people.

• The lecture stated that pilots struggled with repeated nose-down commands and that the second crew was unable to recover in time.

• The aircraft fleet was subsequently grounded.

The lecturer identified several interacting causes:

• Commercial competition.

• Cost pressure.

• Pressure to preserve the appearance of continuity with the previous 737.

• Avoidance of additional pilot-training costs.

• Incomplete disclosure of design changes.

• Inadequate pilot preparation.

• Inadequate sensor redundancy.

• Potentially insufficient testing.

• Regulatory dependence on information supplied by Boeing.

• A relationship between the regulator and manufacturer that was described as too close.

• Organisational bias and overconfidence.

• A culture that prioritised commercial and certification objectives over engineering quality and independent oversight.

The case demonstrates that formal risk-management procedures are insufficient if:

• Relevant information is withheld.

• Risks are minimised to protect commercial objectives.

• Engineers’ concerns are ignored.

• Regulators rely excessively on the organisation being regulated.

• The organisation develops a false sense of security.

• Low estimated probability is used to dismiss high-consequence failure modes.

• Redundancy and fault tolerance are compromised.

The lecturer’s overall conclusion was that company culture and governance can determine whether risk management works in practice. An organisation may possess formal risk frameworks and still experience catastrophic outcomes if the framework is manipulated, ignored, or undermined by incentives.

Equations and definitions

Risk management:

• The coordinated activities used to direct and control an organisation with regard to risk.

Risk assessment:

• A subset of risk management consisting of risk identification, risk analysis, and risk evaluation.

Risk:

• Anything with the potential to cause harm or jeopardise the achievement of an objective. The lecture framed risk as being premised on uncertainty.

Risk identification:

• Determining the relevant risk or event, its source or cause, and its possible consequences.

Likelihood:

• An estimate of how probable or frequent an event is.

Consequence:

• The outcome or harm that results if a risk event occurs.

Initial or raw risk:

• The risk before the proposed mitigation or controls have been implemented.

Risk score:

Risk score = likelihood score × consequence score

Risk evaluation:

• Comparing the calculated risk against predefined risk criteria, thresholds, and tolerance levels.

Risk treatment:

• Selecting and implementing measures to modify risk.

Residual risk:

• The risk remaining after risk treatment has been implemented.

Residual risk score:

Residual risk score = post-treatment likelihood × post-treatment consequence

Risk owner:

• The person or role responsible for managing, monitoring, and reporting a particular risk.

Risk tolerance:

• The level or type of risk an organisation is prepared to accept. The exact tolerance depends on the organisation and must be defined as part of the context.

Risk matrix:

• A table or diagram that combines likelihood and consequence categories to classify risk, often using colour-coded levels such as low, medium, high, and extreme.

Fault-tree logic:

• AND gates represent combinations in which multiple events are required.

• OR gates represent alternatives in which any one of several events may be sufficient.

• The lecture indicated that Boolean algebra can be used to analyse the resulting logic.
