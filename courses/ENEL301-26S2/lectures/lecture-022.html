Overview

This lecture was delivered by Mike, a UC engineering alumnus and technology-industry leader, as a career-focused guest lecture. It used examples from product development, project management, product management, operations, startups, and executive leadership.

The central themes were:

• Engineering careers are often non-linear and can develop through successive changes in role and industry.

• Technical, product, commercial, operational, and management skills reinforce one another.

• Product-market fit should be understood before optimising cost or expanding scope.

• Technical risk must be managed systematically, including consideration of how designs can fail.

• Project priorities must be explicit because scope, cost, time, quality, and unit cost involve trade-offs.

• Customer understanding, stakeholder communication, team relationships, curiosity, and professional reputation are major contributors to project and career success.

• Engineering skills can be applied to major changes in electrification, demand management, artificial intelligence, data centres, and autonomous machines.

The lecture also included practical advice on leading teams, managing uncertainty, handling mergers and acquisitions, and taking accountability for project decisions.

Key concepts

• Product-market fit: The alignment between a product, the problem it solves, its target market, and the value customers perceive in it.

• Voice of the customer: Systematically learning what customers need and value, including through interviews and focus groups.

• Product vision: A clear description of the product, the problem it addresses, its customer, and its intended value.

• Lead customer: An initial customer whose requirements help define and validate the product.

• Minimum viable product (MVP): A limited product used to test an idea, but not necessarily a suitable final product for broad deployment.

• Scope creep: The gradual addition of features or requirements that increases project cost and duration and can obscure the original purpose.

• Iron triangle: The project trade-off between scope, cost, and time.

• Extended project trade-offs: For manufactured products, quality and unit cost also need to be considered alongside scope, cost, and time.

• Technical risk: The possibility that a technical design, component, process, or operating environment will prevent a product from meeting requirements.

• DFMEA: Design failure modes and effects analysis. A structured method for identifying how a design could fail, the effects and severity of failure, possible causes, likelihood, and suitable tests or mitigations.

• PFMEA: Process failure modes and effects analysis. The transcript identifies this as an analysis of risks in the manufacturing or production process.

• Design for manufacture, design for test, and design for service: Design approaches intended to make a product easier to manufacture, verify, maintain, and repair.

• Bill of materials (BOM): A list of the components and materials required to build a product.

• Porter's five forces: A framework for assessing industry competition and likely profitability through new entrants, buyer power, substitutes, supplier power, and competitive rivalry.

• Known knowns, known unknowns, and unknown unknowns: Categories of certainty and uncertainty that should inform contingency planning.

• Productisation: Moving from research and uncertain exploration toward a more predictable product-development process capable of meeting defined milestones.

• Stakeholder engagement: Keeping relevant people informed and aligned throughout a project, with a goal of avoiding surprises.

• Professional reputation: A career asset developed through integrity, authenticity, reliable performance, and relationships.

• Serendipity and timing: External conditions, such as Y2K, the emergence of smartphones, or location-based services, can create opportunities that cannot be fully planned.

Product vision, customer understanding, and scope

The speaker recommended asking:

• What is the product vision?

• What problem is being solved?

• Who is the customer?

• What is the customer value proposition?

• What is the competitive advantage?

He used the concept of an elevator pitch to illustrate the need to explain a company or product concisely. A useful pitch requires understanding both the customer and the organisation's competitive advantage.

He warned against prematurely turning a product into a platform or product family. Doing so may:

• Increase cost.

• Increase development time.

• Dilute the original product vision.

• Add complexity before the first product is validated.

Expanding to a platform may sometimes be correct, but the team should retain a clear lead customer and a specific problem to solve.

He also warned that an MVP is useful for testing an idea but should not automatically be launched to a large customer base. An MVP is inherently compromised or limited, so broad deployment can damage customer trust and reputation.

The most common project problem identified was scope creep. The recommended response was to return to the product vision and preserve focus on the most important objective.

Navman product example: successful execution but poor product-market fit

The speaker described a navigation product developed in Christchurch. The earlier product had taken approximately 18 months to design and sold approximately 50,000 units. The new product was intended to:

• Cost approximately one-third as much.

• Achieve approximately ten times the volume.

• Use a contract manufacturer.

• Be developed in approximately eight months.

The team executed effectively:

• It engaged contract manufacturers.

• It met the intended timeframe.

• Manufacturing reached approximately 3,000 units per day.

• Aircraft were chartered to move products from Singapore to Europe and the United States.

• The product sold approximately 580,000 units.

• The speaker stated that the product generated approximately $200 million.

However, the product used static maps and turn-based arrows instead of moving maps because the static-map licence was cheaper. The decision was made without sufficient customer testing. Customers disliked the absence of moving maps, and the product received poor reviews.

The map-data agreement was later renegotiated, allowing the product to be changed to moving maps. Although the product ultimately sold well, the initial decision caused reputational damage.

The main lessons were:

• Execution quality cannot rescue a product that does not meet customer expectations.

• Cost optimisation should not precede customer understanding.

• Teams must test important assumptions with customers.

• Team members should speak up when they believe a decision is poor.

• A commercially successful outcome can still contain serious product-management failures.

Project trade-offs and priorities

The traditional iron triangle consists of:

• Scope.

• Cost.

• Time.

For manufactured products, the speaker argued that the model should also account for:

• Quality.

• Unit cost.

These variables interact. Increasing scope may increase cost and time. Reducing unit cost may compromise quality or robustness. Reducing scope may make it possible to meet a schedule or price target, but it may also remove a feature that customers consider essential.

The project team must decide which constraints are most important. Possible priorities include:

• Lowest possible cost.

• Minimum delivery time.

• Required product scope.

• High quality and reliability.

• A specific customer or market opportunity.

The speaker stressed that everything cannot be a priority. If everything is treated as a priority, no priority exists in practice.

Technical risk and failure analysis

The speaker described two assumptions that caused problems in a navigation product:

1. A previously successful GPS module would perform adequately in the new product.

2. A battery-disconnection switch and its tool would be sufficiently robust for shipping and customer use.

The GPS module performed well in New Zealand testing but poorly in dense urban environments such as Chicago, New York, and London. Urban canyons and multipath effects created conditions not adequately represented by local testing.

The battery switch was also used in unexpected ways. Customers broke it while using the supplied tool, resulting in many warranty returns. The contract contained an “epidemic failure” threshold: if a failure mode exceeded 3%, the product would have to be recalled. The returns approached this threshold.

The product team had performed several engineering and process activities, including:

• Design for manufacture.

• Design for test.

• Design for service.

• Engineering verification testing.

• Design verification testing.

• Process verification testing.

• Process FMEA.

However, it had not performed a design FMEA. A systematic DFMEA could have prompted:

• More testing of GPS performance in dense urban environments.

• More customer testing of the switch and tool.

• Identification of the switch as a weak link.

• Consideration of the severity of failure if the switch broke.

• A design change before shipment.

The speaker distinguished testing from DFMEA:

• Testing asks whether the design works under selected conditions.

• DFMEA asks how the design could fail and what should be done about those failure modes.

The switch was later changed, and software improvements helped address some GPS issues. A subsequent product used a more complete technical-risk-management process and reportedly shipped approximately 800,000 units without the same problems.

Porter's five forces and strategic decision-making

The proposed smartphone-navigation project was assessed using Porter's five forces. The framework was described as a way to assess industry competition and likely industry attractiveness.

The five forces are:

• Threat of new entrants.

• Buyer power.

• Threat of substitutes.

• Supplier power.

• Competitive rivalry.

The analysis indicated that:

• Map data was controlled by external providers.

• Navigation engines were becoming commoditised.

• It was relatively easy for competitors to enter smartphone navigation.

• The company did not clearly own a defensible competitive advantage.

• Mobile-network operators had substantial buying power and could disrupt the business.

• The value was concentrated more strongly with map-data providers than with the proposed navigation product.

The project was therefore abandoned, despite the speaker's personal interest in putting navigation on smartphones.

Later market developments supported the decision:

• Google introduced free navigation for Android.

• Apple introduced turn-by-turn navigation for the iPhone.

• Competition against a free, platform-integrated product would have been difficult.

The lesson was that strategic analysis can identify an attractive-looking project that lacks sustainable competitive advantage. Not proceeding can be the correct engineering and business decision.

Leading projects and engineering teams

The speaker's practical advice included:

• Expect plans to be disrupted by unexpected events.

• Build strong relationships within the team.

• Invest in team cohesion.

• Keep the most important objective visible.

• Avoid excessive meetings and “meeting apocalypse”.

• Understand boundary conditions.

• Align the project with stakeholder priorities.

• Keep stakeholders engaged.

• Aim for no surprises.

• Communicate bad news early.

• Do not combine unpredictable research with a project that requires predictable productisation milestones.

• Maintain contingency for unknowns.

• Understand component lead times.

• Visit factories, customers, and engineering sites when possible.

• Use travel to learn context and build relationships.

• Respond to problems with resilience, judgement, quick thinking, and humour.

The speaker stated that approximately 24% of engineering time at Tokos was spent on non-project work, partly due to meetings. This figure was presented as an internal observation and not as a general benchmark.

Research versus productisation

Research is inherently iterative, uncertain, and difficult to schedule precisely. A productisation project, in contrast, should move toward predictable development and milestone completion.

The speaker advised against embedding major unresolved research questions inside a productisation project. If critical research remains unresolved, the project may not be able to meet its cost, schedule, or quality commitments.

Bills of materials and lead times

The speaker described the BOM as potentially containing thousands of lines. Every component may have its own lead time.

A single late component can prevent:

• Prototype construction.

• Manufacturing preparation.

• Production start.

• A milestone or customer delivery.

The practical recommendation was to understand the BOM and component lead times early. This issue was described as recurring even in experienced organisations.

Handling uncertainty and organisational change

The speaker used the categories of known knowns, known unknowns, and unknown unknowns to emphasise the need for contingency planning.

During mergers and acquisitions, he recommended:

• Being empathetic.

• Communicating openly and transparently.

• Explaining what is known and what remains uncertain.

• Avoiding promises that cannot be guaranteed.

• Keeping people informed as decisions develop.

• Providing employment and counselling support where possible.

• Continuing to meet existing product and delivery commitments.

He contrasted a difficult acquisition, where a Christchurch facility was eventually closed, with more positive acquisitions. In all cases, employees needed honest information and practical support.

Boards and CEO succession

The speaker explained that the board has two especially important responsibilities:

• Setting company strategy.

• Hiring or firing the CEO.

A company may replace a CEO because the organisation has entered a new phase and requires different capabilities. The speaker used Air New Zealand as an example in which different leaders were associated with different priorities, including brand development, operations, and asset or fleet management.

A CEO is therefore not necessarily replaced because the previous CEO failed. The board may instead decide that a different leadership profile is needed for the next stage of the organisation.

Project leadership and peer relationships

In the final discussion, a student asked about leading peers who had previously been equals.

The speaker recommended establishing role clarity at the beginning:

• Team members can remain friends and colleagues.

• The project leader is responsible for driving the project.

• The leader must make difficult decisions about time, direction, and priorities.

• Team input should be actively heard.

• Final accountability cannot be abandoned through endless consensus-seeking.

Seeking ideas is valuable, but prolonged consultation can slow progress. The project leader should periodically restate:

• What needs to be done.

• What is important.

• What the team is collectively trying to achieve.
