Overview

This lecture examined intellectual property (IP) rights in New Zealand, focusing on rights that may supplement or replace patent protection:

• Copyright

• Registered design rights

• Trademarks

• Domain names and related reputation-based protection

• Geographical indications

• Strategic IP planning, ownership, licensing, and enforcement

The central message was that IP protection is not one-size-fits-all. Different parts of an engineering or commercial project may require different forms of protection:

• Patents generally protect function or technical invention.

• Copyright protects the form of expression.

• Registered designs protect appearance or visual form.

• Trademarks identify and distinguish goods or services.

• Reputation-based actions can protect an established brand where registration is absent.

• Contracts determine or clarify ownership, licensing, and permitted reuse.

The lecture repeatedly emphasised:

1. Check other people’s rights before developing or launching a product.

2. Keep detailed records of development, design iterations, and ownership.

3. Put IP ownership and licensing arrangements in writing.

4. Consider the jurisdictions in which a product will be made, sold, advertised, or used.

5. Choose an IP strategy appropriate to the project rather than automatically protecting everything or nothing.

Key concepts

Copyright

• Copyright protects the form in which an idea is expressed, not the underlying idea itself.

• Protection attaches to specific copyright works.

• Independent creation does not infringe copyright because copyright infringement requires copying.

• There is no universally safe “10% change” rule.

• Infringement may occur if a qualitatively substantial part has been copied, even if that part is a small proportion of the total work.

• Objective similarity and a causal opportunity to copy are important considerations.

• Good records can help demonstrate independent creation or establish ownership and creation dates.

• In New Zealand, copyright protection is described as automatic and does not generally require registration.

• Copyright ownership assignments must be in writing and signed by the person assigning the copyright.

• Employment, commissioning, and consulting arrangements can affect who owns copyright.

• Copyright may apply to software, engineering drawings, diagrams, schematics, photographs, websites, and other technical or creative works.

Detailed notes

1. Copyright protects expression, not ideas

The lecturer used the example of asking several people to draw a tree. Everyone receives the same underlying idea, but each person produces a different expression. Each individual drawing can be a separate copyright work.

This illustrates the distinction:

• Unprotected idea: “draw a tree”

• Protected expression: a particular person’s specific drawing of a tree

Creating another work based on the same general idea is not automatically infringement.

2. Copyright works are separate even when related

A later drawing of a tree may create a second copyright work, even though it expresses the same general idea as an earlier drawing.

Similarly, a movie may contain multiple works with potentially separate ownership and rights. Commercial exploitation may therefore require attention to the rights in each component.

3. Copyright ownership and evidence

Copyright is automatic in New Zealand according to the lecture. The practical challenge is proving ownership and creation.

Useful evidence may include:

• Dated diaries

• Version histories

• Original CAD files

• Archived drawings

• Design notebooks

• Emails

• Source-control records

• Contracts

• Signed assignments

• Records of who worked on each version

• Evidence showing the development process

The lecturer described a client who lost the ability to pursue a copyright case after original files and blueprint records had been overwritten or discarded. The lesson was operational as much as legal: preserve originals and maintain controlled version histories.

4. The “10% rule” is a myth

The lecturer gave a definitive rejection of the idea that changing 10% of a work automatically makes reuse lawful.

The relevant questions include:

• Was a part of the work copied?

• Is there objective similarity?

• Is the copied portion qualitatively substantial?

• Is the similarity attributable to copying?

• Did the alleged copier have an opportunity to access the original?

“Qualitatively substantial” concerns the importance of the copied material, not simply the number of words, lines, pixels, or components copied.

5. Independent creation and indirect copying

Independent creation is a defence to copyright infringement because copying did not occur.

However, the lecturer warned that copying can be indirect. A person may not have directly viewed the original work but may receive specifications or requirements so specific that following them necessarily reproduces the original design.

The kiwifruit packing-tray example illustrated this problem:

• One company developed an efficient packing-tray design.

• A marketing or industry body wanted a standard tray but did not want to rely on one supplier.

• It issued specifications that were so specific that complying with them effectively reproduced the original design.

• The later designers may not have knowingly copied the original, but the information supplied to them created an indirect copying pathway.

6. Burden and sequence of proof in copyright disputes

The lecture described a general sequence, while noting that legal-onus details might be covered in the following week’s law lectures.

The rights holder may need to establish:

1. Ownership of copyright.

2. That the work qualifies for copyright protection.

3. Objective similarity.

4. Copying of a qualitatively substantial part.

The alleged infringer may then need to show that the similarity arose through independent creation. Exact burdens and procedures should be checked against current New Zealand law.

7. Copyright in technical material

Copyright may cover material that is not conventionally “artistic” in a cultural sense.

Examples included:

• Wiring diagrams

• Schematics

• Engineering drawings

• CAD files

• Computer code

• Technical diagrams

• Website content

The lecturer stressed that copyright protection does not depend on artistic merit. A technical schematic can qualify because of its form as a graphical representation.

The lecture presented New Zealand copyright in industrial designs as a potentially useful backup to patent protection, particularly where the appearance of a manufactured article is important.

The lecturer stated that New Zealand has protected three-dimensional articles derived from design drawings, while Australia uses a different approach. 9. Registered design protection must be considered early

• Consider overseas filings if international protection is needed.

• Do not assume that copyright will provide the same enforcement advantages.

The lecture stated that registered design protection in New Zealand may last up to 15 years, with renewal points at five and ten years. The lecturer noted that many designs are not renewed because fashion and product appearance may change quickly.

10. Trademarks as commercial identifiers

Trademarks are not limited to company names. They may include the visual, audio, sensory, or verbal elements by which customers identify a commercial source.

For a technology business, potentially relevant marks may include:

• Product name

• Company name

• App name

• App icon

• Logo

• Product shape

• Product colours

• Slogans

• Sounds

• Domain names

• Social media handles

• Hashtags

A product may require more than one trademark strategy.

11. Choose a distinctive mark before investing

The lecturer recommended checking for existing rights before spending heavily on branding.

A practical sequence is:

1. Search for similar marks, businesses, products, and domains.

2. Use available online trademark tools.

3. Consider an IP Office trademark search or AI-assisted check as an initial screen.

4. Obtain professional advice if the brand is commercially important.

5. Confirm ownership of the logo, slogan, and other creative components.

6. Register the mark in relevant jurisdictions.

7. Use and police the mark consistently.

The lecture gave the example of a company launching a brand and receiving a cease-and-desist letter shortly afterward. Early checking could have identified the problem before substantial investment.

12. Brand assets may themselves be copyrighted

A trademark strategy does not automatically transfer copyright in the artwork used to represent the mark.

For example:

• A logo is a copyright work.

• A slogan may be a copyright work.

• A graphic designer or marketing company may initially own the copyright.

• The client should confirm through an agreement that it owns the copyright or has sufficient rights to reproduce and modify the asset.

Without those rights, the business may infringe the designer’s copyright each time it reproduces the logo in marketing materials.

13. Trademarks and generic terms

A brand owner must use a trademark consistently as a brand rather than as the generic name of the product category.

The lecturer used escalator, Band-Aid, Hoover, and aspirin as examples of marks that became associated with the product itself. Once consumers use a term generically, maintaining exclusive trademark rights becomes more difficult.

14. Reputation is not a substitute for registration in every case

A business may rely on reputation-based protection, but this requires evidence that the public associates the mark with that business.

Registration provides:

• A certificate defining the registered rights

• National rights within the relevant scope

• A clearer enforcement position

• Less reliance on proving reputation from scratch

The trade-off is the upfront cost and the need to select appropriate goods, services, and jurisdictions.

15. International trademark risks

The lecturer emphasised that a New Zealand trademark does not automatically provide complete protection elsewhere.

Potential risks include:

• A foreign party registering the mark first.

• A local manufacturer registering the mark in the manufacturing country.

• A similar mark being registered in local characters.

• A transliteration sounding like the original brand.

• A translated version of the brand being used by another party.

• Social media activity targeting a country where protection has not been obtained.

• A trademark being cancelled for non-use.

For products manufactured overseas, the lecturer recommended considering trademark protection in the manufacturing country, especially where the mark will appear on goods made there.

16. Domain names and company names

The following are separate matters:

• Company-name registration

• Trademark registration

• Domain-name registration

• Social-media handle registration

Registering a company name does not necessarily prevent another party from owning or using a conflicting trademark.

A registered trademark may provide stronger rights than a company or domain registration. Domain-name dispute processes may assist where another party has registered a domain corresponding to the trademark.

17. IP strategy

The lecturer recommended asking:

• Is the valuable feature technical function?

• Is the valuable feature visual appearance?

• Is the valuable feature the brand or customer association?

• Is copyright automatically available?

• Should a patent be pursued?

• Should a design be registered?

• Which marks should be registered?

• Which jurisdictions matter?

• What rights are needed from contractors, employees, and suppliers?

• What should remain confidential?

• What should be licensed rather than assigned?

Different components of one project may use different rights simultaneously.

Equations and definitions

Copyright

Copyright: protection for the form of expression of an idea, rather than the underlying idea itself.

Copyright work: a specific work embodying protected expression, such as a drawing, diagram, photograph, film, written work, computer program, musical work, or sound recording.

Independent creation: creation of a similar work without copying the earlier work. According to the lecture, independent creation does not infringe copyright.

Objective similarity: similarity between the original work and the allegedly infringing work that can be assessed externally rather than merely asserted by one party.

Qualitatively substantial part: an important or significant part of a copyright work. Significance depends on quality or importance, not simply the percentage or physical amount copied.

Registered design

Registered design: registered protection for the appearance of a product, including features such as shape, configuration, pattern, or ornamentation.

Novelty: the requirement that the design is new when protection is sought. The lecture advised filing before disclosure or commercial sale.

Trademark

Trademark: a sign capable of distinguishing one company’s goods or services from another company’s goods or services.

Distinctiveness: the capacity of a mark to identify one commercial source and distinguish it from competitors.

Genericisation: the process by which a trademark becomes understood as the ordinary name for a product or service rather than as a brand.

TM: a symbol that may be used to indicate a claimed trademark.

®: the registered-trademark symbol. The lecture stated that it should only be used where the trademark is registered.

Passing off

Passing off: a reputation-based action concerning conduct that misleads the public into associating another party’s goods or services with the claimant’s established reputation.

Geographical indication

Geographical indication: a protection regime for names or indications associated with products from a particular geographic region, including examples such as Champagne, Scotch whisky, feta, Parma ham, and sherry.

Search costs

Search costs: the time, effort, and uncertainty consumers face when trying to identify and compare products. Trademarks can reduce search costs by signalling expected commercial source and quality.

Territoriality

Territoriality: the principle that IP rights generally operate within particular jurisdictions. Protection, infringement, registration, and enforcement may differ between New Zealand and overseas countries.

“Use it or lose it”

A trademark-registration principle described in the lecture under which a registered mark may be vulnerable to removal or cancellation if it is not genuinely used within the relevant jurisdiction and period. The transcript mentioned a typical period of approximately one to three years in many countries, but exact rules vary.
