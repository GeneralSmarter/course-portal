Engineering, social culture, and ethics

The lecturer reinforced the idea that engineering has achieved little without the help of other people. Engineering practice is shaped by social culture, and that culture consequently influences engineering ethics.

People do not all have the same ethical compass or direction. Ethical judgement is influenced by upbringing, culture, experiences, values, and social context.

There is debate in engineering ethics and education literature about whether someone can be taught to be a good person. One position is that morality cannot be taught. Another is that ethical reasoning develops over time and can be taught or strengthened. Regardless of the debate, engineers should make a serious effort to develop ethical awareness.

Heuristics and cognitive bias

Heuristics are mental rules of thumb. They develop through experience and help people process information and make decisions quickly. For example, an experienced sportsperson can rapidly decide how to react to a changing situation on the field.

Heuristics are useful because they simplify decision-making and increase speed. However, they can produce cognitive bias when they lead someone towards a decision that is not justified by the available evidence.

Cognitive bias can distort a person’s perception of reality. It affects:

• How people operate.

• How they make decisions.

• How they interpret information.

• How they interact with other people.

• How they make ethical judgements.

Everyone has cognitive biases. Awareness of bias does not automatically eliminate it, but it can help people identify when their judgement may be distorted.

The lecturer referred students to Project Implicit, an implicit-association testing project associated with Harvard University. The tests may reveal unexpected results, so students were advised to treat the results as an opportunity for self-awareness rather than as a definitive description of their character.

Explicit and implicit bias

The lecturer distinguished between:

• Explicit bias, where people openly express biased attitudes or beliefs.

• Implicit bias, where attitudes or associations are hidden, unconscious, or not directly observable.

The lecturer referred to a large systematic review or meta-analysis covering hundreds of studies. It reportedly found that explicit and implicit biases continue to exist across countries and can relate to characteristics such as skin colour, appearance, and sexual orientation.

The existence of implicit bias means that people can affect others unfairly without consciously intending to do so. Ethical decision-making therefore requires more than simply checking whether a person claims to be unbiased.

Examples of cognitive biases

#### Sunk-cost bias

A person or organisation may invest millions of dollars in a project, later recognise that the project is failing, and continue investing in the hope of recovering the money already spent.

The past investment is a sunk cost. It has already been spent and cannot be recovered by itself. Continuing a failing strategy solely because of previous investment is therefore poor reasoning.

The lecturer also used a sporting example: a team may continue using a strategy even while being decisively defeated because it has already invested time and money in that strategy.

#### Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias occurs when people notice or seek information that supports what they already believe or want to be true.

The lecturer used the example of someone expecting to buy a Volkswagen Golf GTI after graduation. Once they have formed that expectation, they may notice many “cool” people driving Golf GTIs, which appears to confirm the belief.

The relevant information may be selectively noticed because it supports an existing preference.

#### Guru bias

Guru bias occurs when a group assumes that the most experienced, senior, highly paid, or apparently successful person possesses the truth.

The lecturer used a business example involving Richard Pratt, described as a wealthy Australian business leader associated with the recycling company Visy [company name and details should be checked]. The lesson was that leaders should not assume they are the holders of truth. Expertise can be distributed throughout a team, including among people with less authority or lower pay.

Groupthink

Groupthink occurs when a group makes decisions that do not make sense because the desire for agreement suppresses critical evaluation.

The eight symptoms described were:

1. Illusions of invulnerability: Optimism creates an unjustified sense of safety and encourages risk-taking.

2. Collective rationalisation: The group discounts warnings or fails to challenge its own assumptions.

3. Belief in inherent morality: Members’ confidence in the group’s moral correctness causes them to ignore ethical consequences.

4. Stereotyped views of external groups: The group develops negative views of an opposing or external group and therefore dismisses its responses or evidence.

5. Pressure on dissenters: Members are pressured not to express arguments against the group’s view.

6. Self-censorship: Members suppress their own doubts or concerns.

7. Illusion of unanimity: The group assumes everyone agrees, even though this is not true.

8. Mindguards: Members protect the group or leader from problematic or contradictory information.

Cohesion, diversity, and performance

Group cohesion can improve performance. However, high cohesion can also increase groupthink if members value agreement more than critical evaluation.

Research does not provide a simple rule that diverse groups always perform better or homogeneous groups always perform better. The lecturer stated that both more diverse and more homogeneous groups can perform well in some circumstances.

The clearer conclusion is that collaboration processes are central to excellent performance. These processes require:

• Effective communication.

• Active listening.

• Deliberate opportunities for alternative views.

• Leaders who facilitate rather than dominate discussion.

• Procedures for identifying risks and challenging assumptions.

Group decisions are ultimately made by individuals. A company or organisation does not literally make a decision independently; people within it make and authorise decisions.

Reducing conformity and groupthink

Possible safeguards include:

• Use structured ethical decision-making frameworks.

• Refer to relevant ethical codes of conduct.

• Normalise dissent.

• Ensure that disagreement is treated as a legitimate part of the process.

• Provide anonymous ways for people to express concerns.

• Check whether supposedly anonymous systems reveal identifying information such as IP addresses or workplace network details.

• Include people who can provide diverse perspectives.

• Include external perspectives where appropriate.

• Ask individuals to speak up when they disagree.

• Ensure leaders actively draw out dissenting voices.

• Consider the consequences of both questioning the majority and remaining silent.

The lecturer acknowledged that repeatedly asking difficult questions may give someone a reputation as a troublesome critic. However, remaining silent can allow unsafe or unethical decisions to proceed.

Codes of conduct

Codes of conduct are written by people, usually with strong input from members of the relevant society or profession. Ethical experts may also contribute.

A code of conduct aims to set clear and collective standards for behaviour while incorporating diverse perspectives.

Reaching consensus is difficult because:

• People assign different importance to different issues.

• Wording must often be compromised.

• The final document cannot express every person’s view.

• Some concerns may be omitted.

• Averaging competing views can produce a centrist framework.

• Stronger or more distinctive clauses at the extremes may be removed.
