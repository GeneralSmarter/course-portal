Overview

This lecture introduced life-cycle assessment (LCA), with emphasis on:

• Why sustainability assessments must account for impacts across an entire life cycle rather than focusing only on a product’s operation.

• The importance of challenging assumptions, checking bias, and distinguishing average from marginal electricity impacts.

• The purpose, scope, and limitations of LCA.

• Functional units and their role in comparing products or services.

• System boundaries, exclusions, and the risk of obtaining misleading results through inappropriate boundary choices.

• Environmental indicators, including climate change, smog, ozone depletion, acidification, water pollution, toxicity, respiratory effects, and resource depletion.

• Life-cycle inventory data, including foreground and background data.

• Impact characterisation, normalisation, and subjective weighting.

• Practical applications of LCA in product development, process improvement, policy, marketing, and market access.

The lecture used the proposed “Data Grid” project as an example of how uncertain and changing assumptions can affect an LCA. The lecturer stressed that LCA results are model-based estimates, not direct measurements of reality.

Key concepts

• Life-cycle assessment (LCA): A tool for evaluating the potential environmental impacts of a product or service.

• Model of reality: An LCA represents reality using assumptions, data, databases, and mathematical models. It is not reality itself.

• Life-cycle thinking: Environmental impacts should be considered across resource extraction, processing, manufacture, packaging, transport, use, and end-of-life.

• Average versus marginal emission factors:

• An average electricity emission factor represents the average emissions intensity of the grid.

• A marginal emission factor represents the emissions associated with additional electricity demand.

• Additional demand may be supplied by higher-emission generation, especially during periods of high demand or cold weather.

• Functional unit: A quantified description of the primary function provided by a product or service. It provides the basis for comparing alternatives.

• System boundary: The set of processes included in the product system being modelled.

• Allocation problem: A product may provide multiple functions, making it difficult to divide its environmental impacts between those functions.

• Life-cycle inventory (LCI): The collection and modelling of inputs and outputs required to deliver the functional unit.

• Foreground data: Specific data collected for the system or process being studied.

• Background data: Existing data from databases describing supporting processes such as electricity, materials, transport, and fuel production.

• Characterisation: Conversion of different emissions or resource flows into a common impact category using characterisation factors.

• Characterisation factor: A factor representing the relative contribution of a flow to a particular environmental impact category.

• Normalisation: Comparison of an impact result with a reference or baseline, such as national impacts.

• Weighting: Assigning relative importance to different impact categories to produce a combined result. Weighting is inherently subjective.

• Trade-offs: Reducing one environmental impact may increase another. LCA therefore commonly involves comparing several indicators rather than seeking a single universally optimal answer.

• Greenwashing risk: LCA can be selectively designed or communicated to make a product appear environmentally superior.

• Critical evaluation: Results should be assessed by checking assumptions, data quality, functional units, system boundaries, indicators, time horizons, and whether comparisons are genuinely like-for-like.

Sustainability and life-cycle thinking

The lecture connected LCA with the commonly cited sustainability definition of meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs. The transcript attributes this definition to the “Bruland Commission”; this is probably a transcription error for the Brundtland Commission.

LCA generally concentrates on the environmental pillar of sustainability, but carrying out an LCA can reveal economic and social issues:

• Electricity emission factors require understanding how the electricity system operates and how additional demand affects generation.

• Supply-chain analysis can reveal social or societal issues associated with materials and production.

• Environmental assessments can expose dependencies and impacts that are not obvious when considering only the final product.

Bias and electricity emission factors

Students were encouraged to challenge their own biases about Data Grid. Although data centres may be viewed negatively, AI services used by students require data-centre infrastructure somewhere. Rejecting a particular site does not remove the underlying demand; it may relocate it.

The lecture distinguished between average and marginal grid emission factors:

• An average factor represents the overall emissions intensity of electricity supplied by the grid.

• If a new data centre increases electricity demand, the relevant additional generation may be represented better by a marginal emission factor.

• During periods such as cold weather, electricity demand increases and additional generation may come from gas rather than hydro or wind.

• Therefore, the marginal emission factor may be higher than the average grid factor.

• Electricity emissions can also depend on the time of day and operating conditions.

Using an average factor for all additional electricity demand may therefore underestimate the consequences of a new electricity-intensive facility.

Definition and purpose of LCA

LCA evaluates the potential environmental impacts of a product or service over its life cycle. The word “potential” is important because the result comes from a model based on assumptions and available data.

LCA is distinct from environmental impact assessment:

• LCA: Generally models environmental impacts associated with a product or service across its life cycle.

• Environmental impact assessment: Usually examines the effects of a particular project in a specific location, including terrain, waterways, land, and local environmental conditions.

LCA is governed by international standards. The lecturer described these standards as old and noted that there has been interest in changing them.

The lecture also warned against confusing:

• Life-cycle assessment: Environmental impacts.

• Life-cycle costing: Financial costs over the life cycle, including concepts such as net present value (NPV).

LCA and life-cycle costing can theoretically be performed together, but the lecturer did not recommend combining them directly because LCA software is not well suited to NPV calculations.

Why LCA conclusions depend on assumptions

The lecturer repeatedly emphasised that LCA results depend on the assumptions used. Examples included:

• Glass packaging may be environmentally favourable if containers are washed and returned for reuse.

• Glass placed in mixed recycling may break during collection and sorting, with only a fraction reaching recycling.

• Different glass colours may have different melting temperatures, reducing the usefulness of mixed glass.

• Reusable nappies may have different impacts depending on hot-water washing cycles and other assumptions.

• Electric vehicles may have lower operational emissions but higher production impacts due to batteries and mineral extraction.

• The environmental advantage of an EV depends partly on the electricity grid.

• Rooftop photovoltaic systems commonly have an environmental payback period of approximately 5–10 years according to the lecture, although this depends on the grid and the impacts included.

• PV systems still involve materials and components such as dopants, aluminium, copper, and inverters.

The appropriate conclusion is often “it depends,” followed by an examination of the assumptions that control the result.

Life-cycle stages and material flows

A typical product life cycle can include:

1. Extraction of materials from the ground.

2. Processing and transformation into useful materials.

3. Manufacturing.

4. Packaging.

5. Transport and distribution.

6. Use by the customer.

7. Disposal, recycling, reuse, or other end-of-life treatment.

At every stage, the model tracks:

• Material inputs.

• Energy inputs.

• Resource extraction.

• Emissions to air.

• Emissions to water.

• Emissions to soil.

• Waste and other outputs.

Each input may itself have a complete life cycle. For example, a steel paper clip requires steel, and steel requires iron ore, coking coal, oxygen, energy, and industrial equipment. The equipment used to extract iron ore may itself be made from steel. This creates an interconnected and recursive system of upstream processes.

LCA models are often represented by large matrices and databases. In practice, analysts commonly combine newly collected data with existing datasets rather than constructing every upstream process from the beginning. Building a complete model can nevertheless take months.

Functional units

The functional unit quantifies the primary reason a product or service exists. It allows alternatives to be compared according to the same delivered function rather than simply comparing physical products.

Examples from the lecture:

• Soft-drink packaging: 1 litre of liquid delivered to the consumer.

• Car: Passenger transport, potentially measured in passenger-kilometres:

• Number of people transported × distance travelled.

• Solar photovoltaic system: 1 kilowatt-hour delivered to a household.

• Data Grid: The assignment used the nominal functional unit of one year of Data Grid services.

The functional unit must make comparisons meaningful. For soft drinks, delivering 1 litre might involve several small cans or part of a larger bottle. Differences in serving size, utilisation, and packaging configuration must be considered.

A poor functional unit can make an apparently fair comparison misleading. Products should be compared on equivalent service provision, not merely per item, per kilogram, or per unit of packaging.

System boundaries

A system boundary defines which processes are included in the product system. The lecturer noted that the formal definition is not always practically helpful because upstream processes can continue indefinitely.

A rule of thumb mentioned in the lecture was to include approximately 95% of the environmental impacts. This was explicitly described as a rule of thumb rather than a requirement in the standard.

The difficulty is that an analyst may not know which processes are significant until they have been assessed. A process that initially appears negligible may still need to be included to demonstrate that its contribution is below the chosen cut-off.

The lecturer gave an example involving a Tetra Pak drink package. A peer reviewer required modelling the petrol used to transport the package from a supermarket to the customer’s home. The contribution was ultimately less than 5%, but it had to be modelled to demonstrate that it was below the threshold.

Previous studies, guidelines, and standards are useful for determining reasonable system boundaries. Being the first person to assess a novel system makes boundary selection considerably more difficult.

For the Data Grid project, possible boundary issues included:

• Whether to include lithium battery backup systems if they were not in the original brief but later became part of the project.

• Whether to include servers, PCs, racks, buildings, and other capital equipment.

• Whether to include maintenance.

• Whether to include electricity and water infrastructure.

• Whether to include transport of users or equipment.

• Whether to include human labour.

• Whether to include accidental events such as oil spills.

The lecture stated that many LCAs typically exclude:

• Capital equipment.

• Maintenance.

• Human labour.

• Accidents and unpredictable events.

However, the Data Grid assignment specifically included capital equipment and maintenance. Human labour was generally excluded, except that labour-intensive processes such as mud-brick production may require special consideration.

Misleading boundaries and comparison

The lecturer used an example attributed to McDonald’s (“Macca’s”) to illustrate how system boundaries can create favourable but misleading comparisons.

The company reportedly compared its restaurant environmental footprint with that of an average family restaurant and claimed lower energy use, water use, and waste. The comparison may have been affected by drawing the system boundary around only the physical restaurant.

A conventional restaurant may prepare ingredients in-house:

• Baking bread.

• Preparing patties.

• Chopping potatoes and vegetables.

• Performing other preparation activities on site.

McDonald’s was described as primarily a “heat and serve” operation, with more upstream preparation occurring outside the restaurant boundary. If the upstream preparation and transport are not treated equivalently for both systems, the comparison is not fair.

When reviewing an LCA, ask whether:

• Both alternatives use equivalent boundaries.

• Upstream processing is included consistently.

• Transport, preparation, energy, waste, and end-of-life are treated consistently.

• One system has shifted impacts outside the chosen boundary.

Environmental indicators

The study should identify environmental indicators that are relevant to the system and should not ignore known issues. The lecturer gave the example of discovering that a process produced cyanide, which required inclusion of toxicity indicators.

Indicators mentioned included:

#### Greenhouse gas emissions and climate change potential

The model may include carbon dioxide, methane, and other greenhouse gases. These flows are converted into a carbon-footprint or climate-change result using equivalence factors.

Climate-change results depend on:

• Which gases are included.

• How the gases are characterised.

• The selected time horizon.

• The data and modelling approach.

A 100-year time horizon is commonly used, but different time horizons can produce substantially different results. LCA studies should not be compared without checking their time horizons and characterisation methods.

#### Smog potential

Smog-related impacts can be represented using kilograms of ozone equivalent. The lecture associated this category with atmospheric pollution and smog formation.

#### Ozone depletion potential

Ozone depletion can be represented using CFC-11 equivalents. Different substances have different effects on stratospheric ozone, so the flows are converted using characterisation factors.

#### Acidification potential

Acid-forming substances can be released into the atmosphere, incorporated into clouds, and returned as acid rain. The lecturer described severe historical acidification impacts in industrial areas such as Pennsylvania, associated with pollution from steel and iron production.

#### Water pollution and eutrophication potential

The transcript calls this “neutrification potential,” but the intended term may be eutrophication potential. Nitrates and phosphates entering waterways can:

• Promote excessive algae growth.

• Reduce dissolved oxygen.

• Cause harm or death to aquatic organisms.

The result may be expressed using phosphate or nitrate equivalents.

#### Human toxicity

LCA can model the potential effects of substances on human health. The lecture described results in terms of potential damage to life years, but stressed that the uncertainty can be very high. A quoted scale of approximately \(10^{-14}\) life-years appears in the transcript, but the exact example and units are uncertain because this is an automated transcript.

#### Ecotoxicity

Ecotoxicity concerns effects on ecosystems and species. The lecturer associated this category particularly with heavy metals and described possible measures involving species damage or species loss.

#### Respiratory effects

Particulate matter can produce respiratory impacts. The lecture specifically highlighted particles smaller than approximately 2.5 micrometres, which can penetrate deeply into the lungs and contribute to serious health effects.

#### Fossil and mineral resource depletion

LCA can represent pressure on finite or increasingly difficult-to-extract resources. The lecturer described megajoule surplus as one possible measure:

• It represents the additional energy required to extract the next kilogram of a resource.

• Scarcer resources generally have a higher megajoule surplus.

• It is not the same as the embodied energy of the material.

• It represents extraction difficulty or resource scarcity.

The transcript also mentions financial investment required for extraction as another possible representation, but the precise method is not explained.

Inventory development and impact assessment

Inventory development identifies the inputs and outputs required to deliver the functional unit.

The general sequence described was:

1. Define the goal and scope.

2. Choose the functional unit.

3. Define the system boundary and exclusions.

4. Select relevant environmental indicators.

5. Collect foreground data.

6. Link the foreground data to background databases.

7. Build the life-cycle model.

8. Generate an inventory of resource inputs and emissions.

9. Characterise the inventory into impact categories.

10. Interpret and compare the results.

The inventory may contain a long list of:

• Resource inputs.

• Energy inputs.

• Emissions to air.

• Emissions to water.

• Emissions to soil.

• Waste outputs.

Weight is not necessarily a good proxy for environmental significance. A small mass of a highly toxic substance may be more important than a large mass of an inert material.

Characterisation, normalisation, and weighting

Characterisation converts different flows into a common unit within an impact category. For example, different greenhouse gases have different environmental intensities. Each flow is multiplied by an appropriate characterisation factor and the results are summed.

General form:

\[

\text{Impact category result}

=

\sum_i \left(\text{flow}_i \times \text{characterisation factor}_i\right)

\]

For climate change, the result may be expressed in kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent.

Characterisation produces separate results for different categories. It does not make all environmental impacts directly additive. For example:

• Kilograms of CO₂ equivalent cannot simply be added to kilograms of phosphate equivalent.

• They represent different environmental mechanisms and concerns.

Normalisation compares results with a baseline or reference. For example, a Data Grid carbon footprint could be compared with New Zealand’s total carbon footprint to express its relative contribution.

Weighting assigns relative importance to different impact categories in order to produce a single combined result. The lecturer described weighting as subjective. It may be informed by surveys or other methods, but the choice of relative importance is ultimately a value judgement.

Weighting in a peer-reviewed LCA should be checked to determine whether it changes the outcome. A conclusion that depends strongly on subjective weightings should be reported cautiously.

Because environmental indicators can conflict, LCA often becomes a trade-off problem. Improving one indicator may worsen another, analogous to “whack-a-mole.” The lecturer used electric vehicles as an example of a system in which reduced operational emissions may be accompanied by increased mineral, manufacturing, or toxicity impacts.

Interpreting LCA results

There may not be a universal threshold for whether an LCA result is “acceptable.” In practice, LCA is often comparative:

• Is the proposed system better than the existing baseline?

• Is it better than the competing product?

• Which assumptions determine the difference?

• Are the differences larger than the uncertainty?

• Does the preferred option perform better across the relevant indicators?

A final single number can conceal trade-offs, uncertainty, and subjective judgements. Results should therefore be interpreted alongside the assumptions, data quality, system boundary, and indicator selection.

Equations and definitions

Functional unit

The functional unit quantifies the primary function delivered by a product or service.

Examples:

\[

\text{Soft-drink functional unit} = 1\ \text{litre of liquid delivered to the consumer}

\]

\[

\text{Car functional unit} = \text{passengers transported} \times \text{distance travelled}

\]

The car example is commonly expressed as passenger-kilometres:

\[

\text{Passenger-kilometres} = N_{\text{passengers}} \times d

\]

\[

\text{Solar functional unit} = 1\ \text{kWh delivered to the household}

\]

Life-cycle inventory

The inventory contains the inputs and outputs associated with delivering the functional unit:

\[

\text{LCI} =

\{\text{resources and energy in},\ \text{emissions and waste out}\}

\]

Impact characterisation

For an impact category:

\[

I_k = \sum_i F_i C_{i,k}

\]

where:

• \(I_k\) = total result for impact category \(k\).

• \(F_i\) = quantity of flow \(i\).

• \(C_{i,k}\) = characterisation factor converting flow \(i\) into the units of impact category \(k\).

For climate change, the result may be expressed as:

\[

I_{\text{climate}} =

\sum_i

\left(

F_i \times \text{GWP}_i

\right)

\]

with units such as kg CO₂-equivalent. The precise global-warming-potential factors and time horizon must be stated because different choices produce different results.

Characterisation factor

A characterisation factor expresses the relative contribution of a flow to an environmental impact category.

Examples mentioned:

• Greenhouse gases → kg CO₂ equivalent.

• Smog formation → kg ozone equivalent.

• Ozone depletion → kg CFC-11 equivalent.

• Eutrophication or “neutrification” → phosphate or nitrate equivalent.

Normalisation

A normalised result compares an impact with a reference baseline:

\[

I_{\text{normalised}}

=

\frac{I_{\text{system}}}{I_{\text{reference}}}

\]

The lecture’s example was comparing Data Grid’s carbon footprint with New Zealand’s total carbon footprint. The exact reference year and dataset would need to be specified.

Megajoule surplus

Megajoule surplus was described as the additional energy required to extract the next kilogram of a resource. It indicates extraction difficulty or scarcity rather than the material’s embodied energy.
