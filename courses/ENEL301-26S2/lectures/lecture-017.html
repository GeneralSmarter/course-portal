Key concepts

• Liquidation priority: In general, liquidation costs are paid first, followed by secured creditors, other creditors, preference shareholders, unsecured creditors, and ordinary shareholders. The exact order can vary.

• Employment cost: The cost to an organisation is higher than an employee’s gross or take-home salary because it includes items such as KiwiSaver, ACC-related costs, and other benefits.

• Available working time: Annual leave and public holidays reduce the total number of days available for productive work.

• Utilisation: The proportion of an employee’s available time spent on billable work.

• Billable hours: Only a proportion of total working hours may generate revenue directly.

• Charge-out rate: The rate charged to a client must recover employment costs, non-billable time, overheads, and profit.

• Sunk cost: A cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered.

• Sunk-cost fallacy: Continuing to invest in an unsuccessful activity because of money already spent.

• Opportunity cost: The benefit forgone by choosing one alternative instead of the next-best alternative.

• Industry cost structure: The relative proportions of wages, purchases, and other operating expenses vary between industries.

• Cost-estimation bias: Estimates can be distorted by limited experience, human error, historical assumptions, or an externally imposed target price.

• Top-down estimate: A rapid estimate based on experience, analogy, historical data, or scaling metrics.

• Bottom-up estimate: A detailed estimate built from decomposed tasks, direct costs, indirect costs, contingency, and profit margin.

• Estimate maturity: Large projects may pass through several stages of increasingly detailed estimates before implementation.

• CAPEX: Capital expenditure on long-term or high-value assets such as buildings, machinery, and expensive computers.

• OPEX: Operating expenditure required for day-to-day operations.

• Overheads: Indirect operating costs that must be included when determining the true cost of work.

Liquidation and payment priority

A liquidator or liquidation firm is appointed to wind up the company and is paid first for carrying out the liquidation. KordaMentha was given as an example of an organisation involved in insolvency work.

The lecturer described the general order as:

1. Liquidator and liquidation costs.

2. Secured creditors, including banks and potentially bondholders.

3. Other creditors, including employees and the Inland Revenue Department.

4. Preference shareholders.

5. Unsecured creditors, including suppliers.

6. Ordinary shareholders.

The lecturer stressed that the order can vary and that lower-ranked claimants may receive little or nothing. Ordinary shareholders are generally last and commonly receive no payout. This material was explicitly described as background information and not assessed content.

Sunk costs

A sunk cost is money that has already been spent and cannot be recovered.

The example given was spending $50,000 on building something that produced no new sales. Once spent and unrecoverable, that $50,000 is a sunk cost.

Sunk costs can create a bias toward continuing an unsuccessful project. People may feel that further investment is justified because they want to recover money already spent. This reasoning is the sunk-cost fallacy.

When reassessing a project using methods such as net present value, costs already spent should not be included in the forward-looking analysis. Those costs are no longer recoverable and should not affect the decision about future expenditure.

Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the benefit lost when choosing one alternative over another.

It may be expressed as:

• Lost financial income.

• Lost time.

• Lost access to another opportunity.

• The benefit of the next-best alternative that was not selected.

The lecturer used postgraduate study as an example. If a person receives $35,000 per year while completing a PhD instead of taking a graduate role paying approximately $65,000 per year, the opportunity cost was described as approximately $30,000 per year, or approximately $100,000 over the relevant period.

The notes retain this uncertainty rather than treating the transcripted value as reliable.

Opportunity-cost comparisons should also consider potential benefits of the selected option. The lecturer noted that the example does not account for the possibility that a PhD could eventually lead to higher income. Such benefits are uncertain, especially if the person enters academia.

Why cost estimates are uncertain

Cost estimates are forecasts rather than known values. Preparing an estimate itself consumes time and money, and that estimation effort may not be recoverable.

For example, an engineering consultancy may spend three days preparing a proposal or quotation. If the proposal is unsuccessful, the labour cost of preparing it may be written off. This contributes to the organisation’s non-billable time and affects utilisation.

Sources of estimation difficulty include:

• Inexperience with similar work.

• Uncertainty about task duration.

• Human error.

• Incomplete organisational knowledge.

• Poor estimation tools or historical data.

• Unidentified tasks.

• Unexpected capital expenditure.

• Customer-imposed target prices.

• Changes between past and future projects.

A customer may state that they will not pay more than $20,000. This target can bias the estimator toward making the work fit the target rather than estimating the true cost independently. The project may then be accepted on the assumption that it will be profitable even when the underlying estimate does not support that conclusion.

Top-down costing

Top-down costing begins with a broad estimate and relies heavily on experience, historical data, and professional judgement.

An analogous estimate assumes that the current project is sufficiently similar to a previous project. The previous project’s cost is adapted to the new project based on similarities in:

• Scope.

• Duration.

• Type of work.

• Expected resource requirements.

Consultancies may use boilerplate templates for recurring job types. A previous project template can provide a rapid starting estimate for a similar job.

Top-down estimates can also be parametric. Historical costs are scaled using a measurable factor or metric. The lecturer gave software cost per line of code as an example of a possible scaling metric.

Advantages of top-down costing:

• Fast.

• Useful early in a project.

• Efficient when the project resembles previous work.

• Provides an initial estimate before detailed planning is available.

Limitations of top-down costing:

• Can be biased toward a target price.

• Assumes the past is representative of the future.

• May not be traceable or easy to defend.

• Does not capture variation between projects.

• May omit unusual or project-specific costs.

The lecturer described it as a useful first guess rather than a complete or definitive estimate.

Bottom-up costing

Bottom-up costing starts with a project-management plan and decomposes the project into tasks and activities.

The estimator then identifies and totals:

• Direct task costs.

• Labour.

• Materials and purchases.

• Equipment.

• Indirect costs.

• Company operating costs.

• Contingency.

• Profit margin.

Contingency provides a buffer for uncertainty and estimation error. Profit margin is added after the relevant costs and contingency have been considered.

Advantages of bottom-up costing:

• More detailed and traceable.

• Based on identifiable activities.

• Can account for project-specific requirements.

• Makes it easier to identify where costs originate.

Limitations of bottom-up costing:

• Time-consuming.

• May take weeks or months for a large project.

• Can still omit small but expensive tasks.

• Depends on the quality of the project decomposition.

• May fail to include unexpected capital expenditure or other major events.

The lecturer gave the example of a large item of capital expenditure failing partway through a project and unexpectedly having to be paid for from project funds.

Staged estimates for large projects

Large projects are often estimated several times before implementation. Early estimates are rough, while later estimates become more precise as the proposal develops and more information becomes available.

The lecturer referred to an example associated with Waka Kotahi involving six stages:

1. Programme business case estimate.

2. Indicative business case estimate.

3. Detailed business case estimate.

4. Pre-implementation Estimate 1.

5. Pre-implementation Estimate 2.

6. Implementation estimates.

The exact acronyms and classification names may differ between industries and organisations. The main point is that cost estimates become more detailed and reliable as project definition progresses.

Cost-accounting terminology recap

The lecturer’s concluding list of terminology included:

• CAPEX: Long-term, high-value capital items such as buildings, machinery, and expensive computers.

• OPEX: Day-to-day operating expenditure.

• Fixed costs: Costs that do not change directly with output over the relevant range.

• Variable costs: Costs that change with output or activity.

• Semi-variable costs: Costs containing both fixed and variable components.

• Marginal cost: The additional cost associated with producing one more unit or undertaking one more increment of activity.

• Labour: Employee-related cost, whose relative importance depends on the industry.

• Overheads: Indirect costs required to operate the organisation.

The lecturer emphasised that overheads must be included in project costing. Omitting them can result in work being priced below its true cost and cause the organisation to lose money.
