Key concepts

• Income statement: Reports revenue, expenses, depreciation, amortisation, interest, tax, and resulting profit or loss over a period.

• Revenue: Money received or earned from selling goods or services.

• Cost of goods sold (COGS): Direct costs associated with producing or delivering goods or services, including relevant labour and materials.

• Gross profit: Revenue minus cost of goods sold.

• Operating expenditure (OpEx): Other operating costs, such as administration, administrative salaries, rent, and utilities.

• EBITDA: Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation.

• EBIT: Earnings before interest and tax, after depreciation and amortisation.

• Net income or net profit: Profit remaining after interest and tax.

• Depreciation: Allocation of the cost of a physical asset over its useful life.

• Amortisation: Allocation of the cost of a non-physical or intangible asset over time, such as a software licence or intellectual-property creation costs.

• Tax shelter: A deduction or accounting treatment that reduces taxable earnings. In this lecture, depreciation and amortisation were described as reducing the earnings on which tax is calculated.

• Profit is not the same as cash. A company can be profitable but have little cash, or report a loss while still holding substantial cash.

• Cash-flow statement: Shows cash entering and leaving a business during a period.

• Operating cash flow: Cash associated with normal business operations.

• Investing cash flow: Cash associated with buying or selling assets and investments.

• Financing cash flow: Cash associated with borrowing, repaying loans, and paying dividends.

• Liquidity: The ability to meet short-term obligations using available current assets.

• Current ratio: A common liquidity ratio comparing current assets with current liabilities.

• Quick ratio: Another liquidity measure, generally more restrictive than the current ratio.

• Debt-to-equity ratio: Indicates the extent to which a business relies on debt relative to owners’ equity.

• Horizontal analysis: Analysis of financial statements or ratios across multiple periods to identify trends.

• Return on total assets (ROA): Indicates how effectively a company generates EBIT from its total assets.

• Return on equity (ROE): Indicates how effectively a company converts shareholders’ equity into profit after tax.

• Sustainability assessment: The assignment requires justified choices about impacts, system boundaries, data sources, stakeholders, assumptions, and conclusions rather than a supposedly perfect answer.

Detailed notes

Income statement

The income statement is used to determine whether a company is profitable. A loss does not automatically mean that the business is in serious trouble. Its significance depends partly on the amount of cash available and on the business’s wider strategy and circumstances.

The income statement generally includes:

1. Revenue.

2. Cost of goods sold.

3. Gross profit.

4. Operating expenditure.

5. EBITDA.

6. Depreciation and amortisation.

7. EBIT.

8. Interest.

9. Profit before tax.

10. Company tax.

11. Net income or net profit.

Revenue is the money generated from selling goods or services. COGS represents the direct costs of delivering those goods or services, such as the people doing the work and the materials used.

Gross profit shows the amount remaining after direct production or delivery costs. The business must then account for operating expenditure, including administrative costs, administrative salaries, rent, and utilities.

EBITDA, EBIT, and net profit

The relationships presented were:

• EBITDA is gross profit minus operating expenditure.

• EBIT is EBITDA minus depreciation and amortisation.

• Net profit is EBIT minus interest and tax.

EBITDA is intended to show the performance of the core operations before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation. EBIT includes the effect of depreciation and amortisation, but not interest or tax. Net profit is the amount remaining after interest and tax.

Depreciation and amortisation

Depreciation is calculated for physical assets. A company may maintain an asset register or spreadsheet containing:

• The assets owned by the organisation.

• Each asset’s useful life.

• Its depreciation schedule.

• The depreciation that can be claimed during a particular period.

• Whether an asset has failed and should be written off.

• Whether a fully depreciated asset is likely to be replaced.

Amortisation was described as the equivalent of depreciation for non-physical assets. Examples included:

• A large software licence.

• Expenditure associated with creating intellectual property.

The lecturer warned not to confuse this accounting use of amortisation with the amortisation of loans. These are different uses of the same term.

Tax calculation and tax sheltering

The tax calculation discussed in the lecture was based on earnings after accounting for depreciation and amortisation. The sequence described was:

1. Start with earnings before interest and tax.

2. Subtract depreciation and amortisation to obtain EBIT.

3. Subtract interest to obtain profit before tax.

4. Apply corporate tax to the resulting taxable profit.

5. The remaining amount is net profit after tax.

Depreciation and amortisation reduce the accounting earnings used in the tax calculation. They were therefore described as a form of tax sheltering.

The lecturer connected this idea with an earlier “WACC equation,” although the ASR rendered it as “whack equation.” The relevant feature was described as a term of the form 1 − T_c, associated with tax effects in debt financing. The exact notation and context should be checked against the course formula sheet.

A company generally does not pay income tax when it has no taxable earnings. The lecturer warned that students had previously made the mistake of applying tax to a company that was losing money.

Losses, cash, and business condition

A loss is not necessarily a problem:

• A loss with substantial cash reserves may be manageable.

• A loss combined with little or no cash may be dangerous.

• A company with insufficient cash may need to sell assets or obtain additional financing.

• If it has no assets left to sell and no cash, its ability to continue operating may be severely limited.

The wider context matters. A company may report a loss because of investment, depreciation, strategy, market conditions, or other factors while still having a strong share price or sufficient cash.

Profit versus cash

Profit reported in the income statement is not equivalent to cash generated during the period.

A company may use profit to:

• Pay dividends.

• Retain cash in the business.

• Use a combination of dividends and retained cash.

Consequently:

• A profitable company may have little cash available.

• A company reporting a loss may still have substantial cash.

• Cash-flow trends should be examined alongside profit and balance-sheet information.

A declining cash balance over several years may indicate that the business is funding its operations from existing cash reserves rather than generating enough cash from operations.

System boundaries

A system boundary defines which processes are included in the LCA model and which are excluded.

The boundary does not necessarily need to be divided by country or region. It may instead be organised by process, for example:

• Computer equipment.

• Mining and mineral processing.

• Infrastructure.

• Concrete and steel.

• Electricity used during operation.

• Excluded processes, such as human labour, shown outside the boundary.

Processes being modelled should be inside the boundary, while excluded processes should be clearly identified outside it.

Country-specific boundaries can be useful for complex international supply chains because they help explain where materials and activities originate. However, a generic process-based boundary is also acceptable if it is clearly defined and consistently applied.
