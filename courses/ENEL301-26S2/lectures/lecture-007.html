Sources and types of New Zealand law

#### Parliamentary law

Parliament’s primary lawmaking power is to enact Acts of Parliament, also called statutes.

The term “legislation” is broader than “statute”:

• Primary legislation consists of Acts or statutes enacted by Parliament.

• Secondary legislation, also called delegated legislation, consists of rules made by another person, agency, government department, or local authority under power delegated by Parliament.

Delegated legislation must remain within the limits set by the empowering statute. Parliament may specify:

• Which body is authorised to make the rules.

• The subject matter covered by the delegated power.

• The limits within which the rules may operate.

Examples include:

• Regulations and rules made by government departments or other government bodies.

• Bylaws made by local authorities or councils.

The lecture used road rules as an example. The Land Transport Act 1998 is an Act enacted by Parliament. It empowers the New Zealand Transport Agency to make rules and regulations concerning transport, including road use. The detailed road rules are therefore examples of delegated legislation rather than provisions all written directly into the Act.

The lecturer noted that a large number of delegated legislative instruments may exist under a single Act. The precise number mentioned for the Land Transport Act was approximately 480, but this figure was presented as something the lecturer had looked up and should be independently verified if relied upon.

#### Common law

The second major source of law is the courts, particularly through common law or judge-made law.

Courts have two relevant functions:

• Interpreting and applying statutory rules made by Parliament.

• Developing legal rules through their own decisions.

New Zealand’s legal system traces back to the English common law system. In a common law system, legal rules can develop when disputes are brought before courts. The court decides the dispute and may establish or clarify a legal principle that can apply to later cases.

Common law differs from a system in which all legal rules are intended to be written in advance in a comprehensive code. It develops incrementally in response to actual disputes.

Advantages identified in the lecture:

• It develops organically.

• It can respond to changes and unforeseen circumstances in society.

• It can address situations that legislators did not anticipate.

Disadvantages identified in the lecture:

• The law may be difficult to locate because it must be traced through cases.

• The relevant legal rule may have developed gradually over time.

• The result in unusual or marginal cases may not be obvious in advance.

Parliamentary statutes prevail over common law. If Parliament legislates in an area previously governed by common law, the statute replaces or modifies the common law to the extent of any inconsistency. The lecturer observed that statutes have increasingly occupied areas that were previously governed by common law, although common law remains significant. Contract law was given as an example of an area in which much of New Zealand law remains common law.

Parliamentary lawmaking process

The lecture presented a simplified process for enacting a statute:

1. A policy idea arises.

2. The idea may come from political parties, pressure groups, government departments, or other sources.

3. The Parliamentary Counsel Office drafts a bill.

4. The bill may go to Cabinet for approval.

5. The bill is introduced into Parliament.

6. The bill undergoes its parliamentary readings and committee stages.

7. If passed, it receives royal assent.

8. It becomes an Act of Parliament and comes into force according to its commencement provision.

The lecturer emphasised that this was a basic flow chart and omitted some steps and complexities.

The government of the day generally controls which bills are introduced. In the lecture, “government” referred to the group controlling the majority of members of Parliament, rather than simply the institution or office of the Prime Minister.

#### Private members’ bills

A private member’s bill is introduced by an MP who is not necessarily part of the ruling government.

There are allocated opportunities for private members’ bills. The lecture described a process involving bills being placed into an old biscuit tin, from which a bill is selected to be considered. This was presented informally as an explanation of the selection process.

Private members’ bills often fail because their supporters do not control a majority of Parliament. However, some succeed. The same-sex marriage legislation was given as an example of a successful private member’s bill, with the lecturer placing it approximately around 2012.

#### Stages of a bill

The lecture identified the following stages:

• Introduction and first reading.

• Select committee consideration, if the bill passes its first reading.

• Public submissions and hearings before the select committee.

• Second reading and debate.

• Committee of the whole House.

• Third reading and vote.

• Royal assent.

• Commencement of the resulting Act.

The select committee stage generally allows members of the public to give their views and make submissions. The government may treat a bill as urgent and bypass or shorten this process.

After a bill passes its third reading, it still requires royal assent. The head of state of New Zealand is the monarch, King Charles III, represented in New Zealand by the Governor-General. The Governor-General formally signs bills on behalf of the monarch.

The lecturer stated that, under constitutional convention, the Governor-General follows the advice of the government and would not normally refuse assent to a bill passed by Parliament. The Governor-General has theoretical residual powers, particularly in a constitutional crisis, but was described as having very little practical decision-making power in ordinary circumstances.

An Act may come into force:

• On the day after it is signed, if no different commencement rule applies.

• On another date specified in the Act.

Statutes, interpretation, and unusual cases

The lecture used the offence of driving while disqualified as an example. The relevant provision was identified as section 32 of the Land Transport Act, although the lecturer qualified this by saying it was believed to still be in force.

The provision was described as making it an offence for a person to drive a motor vehicle on a road while disqualified from holding or obtaining a driver licence.

Statutory wording may appear clear in ordinary cases but become difficult when applied to unusual facts. Legislators cannot anticipate every possible factual situation. Courts therefore have to determine how statutory words apply to marginal or unexpected cases.

Case example: R v Clayton

The lecture discussed a case referred to as “R and Clayton” or “R v Clayton,” from 1973. The transcript alternates between forms, so the exact formal citation should be checked separately.

The case concerned a person who was disqualified from driving. Mr Clayton was seated in the passenger seat, while his wife occupied the driver’s seat and operated the pedals. Mr Clayton controlled the steering.

The legal issue was whether Mr Clayton was “driving” for the purpose of the relevant statutory offence.

The High Court directed the jury that driving meant effective control of the steering. Mr Clayton was convicted.

The Court of Appeal held that driving meant taking an active part in the operation of a vehicle. This interpretation could allow more than one person to be regarded as driving a vehicle at the same time. The appeal was dismissed.

The case demonstrates that courts may have to determine the meaning of an ordinary word within a statutory context. It also demonstrates how a decision can establish a precedent for later cases.

#### Relevance to self-driving vehicles

A future court could ask whether a person in a self-driving vehicle is taking an “active part in the operation” of the vehicle. Possible considerations could include:

• Whether the person has effective control.

• Whether the person is supervising the vehicle.

• Whether entering a destination constitutes active participation.

• Whether the existing interpretation is sufficiently broad for autonomous vehicles.

A future court might apply the existing precedent or develop it further if the technology creates facts not adequately addressed by the earlier decision. The lecturer did not give a definitive answer on whether a person in a self-driving vehicle would be guilty.

Doctrine of precedent

Precedent is particularly important in common law systems because courts develop legal rules through their decisions.

If an earlier decision comes from a higher court in the hierarchy, a lower court is generally bound to follow it, even if the lower court judge disagrees with the reasoning.

A judge may:

• Follow the precedent while explaining disagreement.

• Distinguish the earlier decision by identifying materially different facts.

• Avoid applying the earlier case if it does not govern the facts of the current dispute.

A decision from a lower court is generally not binding on a higher court. The lecturer stated that a judge may generally disregard a lower court decision, although it may still be persuasive.

Contracts

A contract was defined as:

• An agreement or set of promises between two or more persons.

• Intended to be enforceable at law.

The definition has two central components:

1. There must be an agreement or mutual assent.

2. The parties must intend the agreement to have legal consequences.

A contract is the legal mechanism used by courts to deal with legally enforceable agreements. The basis for enforcing a contract is that the parties agreed to be bound by its terms.

1. Intention to create legal relations.

2. Offer and acceptance.

#### Intention to create legal relations

The parties must intend their agreement to be legally enforceable.

Not every agreement is a contract. For example, an agreement between friends to meet for dinner is generally not intended to create legal relations. If one person fails to attend, the other normally cannot sue for breach of contract.

Commercial arrangements generally carry an intention to create legal relations. This requirement is usually straightforward in commercial cases, subject to rare exceptions.

Social and domestic arrangements are more difficult. Agreements between friends or family members may or may not be intended to be legally binding. Relevant circumstances can include:

• The nature of the relationship.

• The subject matter of the arrangement.

• The seriousness of the obligations.

• Whether substantial money or financial commitments are involved.

• Whether the parties clearly expressed their intentions.

A family arrangement involving substantial financial obligations may be legally enforceable even though it is between family members.

#### Offer

An offer is an expression of willingness to contract made with the intention that it will become binding as soon as it is accepted by the person to whom it is addressed.

An offer therefore communicates a readiness to contract immediately upon acceptance.

#### Acceptance

Acceptance is a final and unqualified expression of assent to the terms of the offer.

Acceptance must correspond with the offer. A response that changes a term may not be acceptance; it may instead be a counteroffer.

#### Counteroffer

A counteroffer:

• Rejects or destroys the original offer.

• Substitutes a new offer on different terms.

• Means the original offer can no longer be accepted, unless it is renewed.

Example:

• Seller: “I will sell you my car for $10,000.”

• Buyer: “I will pay $9,000.”

• The buyer’s response is a counteroffer, not acceptance.

The lecturer noted that mixed communications can create uncertainty. For example:

• Seller: “I will sell you my car for $10,000, with settlement on 1 September.”

• Buyer: “I accept your offer and look forward to settlement on 1 October.”

This response appears to accept the offer but changes the settlement date. Its legal effect may therefore be uncertain and may depend on how the communication is interpreted.

A request framed as a question may have a different effect:

• “Would you consider settlement on 1 October?”

This may be a request to vary or renegotiate the offer rather than a counteroffer, and may not necessarily reject the original offer.

Invitation to treat

An invitation to treat is an invitation for another person to make an offer. It is preliminary and does not normally express a willingness to contract immediately upon acceptance.

Common examples include:

• Advertisements.

• Goods displayed on shop shelves.

• Prices listed online.

An advertisement for a product at a specified price is generally not an offer. If it were an offer, the business could potentially be bound to sell to everyone who attempted to accept it, even if the stock were limited.

The contract is generally formed later, when the customer makes an offer to buy and the business accepts it, often at the point of purchase.

Goods displayed on a shelf are similarly generally invitations to treat. The customer does not form a contract merely by placing an item in a trolley. The customer can change their mind and return the item to the shelf. The contract is generally formed at a later stage, such as when the transaction is accepted at the checkout.

The lecturer stated that online prices would normally be treated similarly, although the precise contractual process can be modified by a business’s terms and conditions.

E-commerce terms

Businesses may specify when and how a contract is formed in their online terms and conditions. For example, terms may state that no contract exists until:

• The business confirms the order.

• The goods are dispatched.

• Another specified event occurs.

The fact that a customer has paid does not necessarily determine the point of contract formation if the applicable terms clearly provide otherwise.

#### Browse-wrap

Browse-wrap terms are made available through links, often on web pages or at checkout.

Whether the terms are incorporated into the contract depends on factors such as:

• How prominent the link is.

• Whether it is clear that terms govern the transaction.

• Whether a reasonable user would have notice of the terms.

Terms hidden in an obscure or imperceptible location are less likely to be effective. The lecturer used white text on a white background as an example of terms that would generally not provide reasonable notice.

#### Click-wrap

Click-wrap requires the user to actively indicate agreement, commonly by:

• Viewing or accessing the terms.

• Selecting an “I agree” box.

• Sometimes scrolling through the terms before the box becomes available.

The lecturer described click-wrap as a more certain method of incorporating terms. Users will generally be bound where the terms are presented clearly and the user actively agrees to them.

Consumer protection
