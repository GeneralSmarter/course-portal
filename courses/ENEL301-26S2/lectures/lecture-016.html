Key concepts

• A discount rate is a forecast of the annual cost of obtaining and servicing the capital required to fund a project.

• The discount rate incorporates financing costs and some elements of risk, but it is not necessarily the same as an interest rate or a direct measure of risk.

• The discount rate can vary between projects, companies, industries, and over time.

• WACC combines the costs of the different sources used to finance a company.

• Company financing can be divided broadly into:

• Debt financing: loans and bonds.

• Equity financing: ordinary shares and preference shares.

• Debt and equity both have costs to the company.

• Debt financing may provide a tax shelter because interest-related debt costs can reduce tax liability.

• Ordinary shareholders generally receive uncertain returns through dividends and changes in share price.

• Preference shareholders receive a fixed dividend but generally do not have voting rights.

• Beta measures how sensitive a share’s return is to movements in the overall stock market.

• A beta greater than 1 indicates a more volatile, or aggressive, share.

• A beta less than 1 indicates a less volatile, or defensive, share.

• WACC is commonly used as the discount rate for NPV calculations.

• WACC changes when interest rates, bond-market conditions, dividends, risk-free returns, or other financing conditions change.

• Nominal cash flows include inflation and should be discounted using a nominal WACC.

• Real cash flows exclude inflation and should be discounted using a real WACC.

• The nominal/real treatment must be consistent. Mixing nominal cash flows with a real discount rate, or real cash flows with a nominal discount rate, gives an inappropriate NPV.

• Sensitivity studies test how changes in uncertain inputs, such as the discount rate, costs, and revenue, affect the NPV.

• Cash flow and the time value of money are more relevant to engineering investment decisions than accounting profit alone.

Detailed notes

1. Discount rates

The discount rate represents a forecast of how much it will cost to service the money needed to fund a project. It is described as the annual cost of capital and is used to value future cash flows.

The discount rate:

• Includes the cost of servicing debt.

• Includes the cost of creating value for shareholders.

• Incorporates elements of financial risk.

• Is used to discount future cash flows in an NPV calculation.

• Is not necessarily the same as the interest rate on a particular loan.

• Is not a direct or complete measure of project risk.

• Depends on the industry, company, and financing structure.

• May change as the project progresses.

Because the discount rate is a forecast and can change, a project may need to be re-evaluated later. Forecast expenses and revenue may also change as more information becomes available.

The lecture identified WACC as the main method used in ENEL301. Other financial models exist, including the capital asset pricing model, but they were not explored in detail.

2. Sources of company finance

A company can obtain funding through debt or equity.

Debt financing includes:

• Bank loans.

• Lines of credit.

• Overdraft facilities.

• Equipment loans.

• Bonds.

Equity financing includes:

• Cash or other capital supplied by owners.

• Ordinary shares.

• Preference shares.

• Private or publicly traded ownership.

The total capital value of a company was described as the combination of its total debt and total equity.

Both debt and equity have a cost:

• Debt costs the company through interest and related financing costs.

• Equity costs the company because shareholders expect a return through dividends, share-price growth, or both.

3. Weighted average cost of capital

WACC is a weighted average of the costs of the company’s different financing sources.

The weights represent the proportion of the company’s total financing supplied by each source. For example, the proportion financed by loans is the value of loans divided by the total value of loans, bonds, ordinary shares, and preference shares.

The transcript used the following symbols:

• DL: debt in loans.

• DB: debt in bonds.

• EOS: equity in ordinary shares.

• EPS: equity in preference shares.

• KL: cost of loan financing.

• KB: cost of bond financing.

• KOS: cost of ordinary shares.

• KPS: cost of preference shares.

• TC: corporate tax rate.

The denominator is the total financing:

DL + DB + EOS + EPS

The debt terms include the factor:

1 − TC

This reflects the tax shelter associated with debt financing. The lecture stated that having debt can reduce tax liability, reducing the effective cost of servicing debt.

4. Loan financing

Loan financing is represented by DL.

Examples of loan financing include:

• Bank loans.

• Lines of credit.

• Overdraft facilities.

• Equipment loans.

Loans may be secured by some form of security. Different loans have different interest rates, so a company with multiple types of loans may need to account for each separately. For ENEL301, the treatment is simplified.

The relevant loan cost is the annualised interest rate. Annualisation is important because some loans compound more frequently than once per year.

The lecture gave a broad indicative range of business lending rates of approximately 5.5% to 20%, depending on the loan type. This figure was presented as a contemporary example rather than a universal or permanent value.

5. Bond financing

A bond allows a company to borrow money from investors. The investors act like many small lenders:

1. The company issues bonds.

2. Investors provide money to the company.

3. The company repays the principal and provides an additional return.

4. Repayment occurs at the bond’s maturity.

Bonds can be traded between investors in bond markets. Maturities may range from approximately six months to 30 years.

The cost of bond financing is represented by KB. The lecture described KB as being similar to the internal rate of return for the bond. Students are not required to calculate KB in ENEL301; if needed in an examination question, the value will be provided.

The lecture stated that bond returns are typically higher than ordinary interest rates, although this is not always the case. Corporate tax sheltering also applies to bond financing.

6. Equity financing and shares

The lecture focused on shares rather than cash holdings as the relevant equity sources.

Shares represent ownership of a company. They may be:

• Private shares.

• Public shares listed on a stock exchange.

Shareholders expect a return for providing capital. The two main sources of return are:

• Dividends.

• Growth in the share price.

A company may distribute some of its profits to shareholders as dividends. Dividends may be:

• Interim dividends, paid during the financial year.

• Final dividends, paid at the end of the financial year.

• Special dividends, paid when an unusual event generates substantial available profit, such as the sale of a major asset.

A special dividend is distributed to shareholders according to their shareholding.

An initial public offering (IPO) is used to float a company on a public stock exchange and raise capital. After an IPO, the share price may remain stable, increase, or fall substantially.

7. Ordinary shares

Ordinary shares do not guarantee a fixed dividend or a fixed rate of return. The shareholder receives whatever return the company and market conditions provide.

Ordinary shareholders generally have voting rights and can participate in decisions affecting the company. The lecture used KiwiSaver as an example: a KiwiSaver investor may indirectly own shares, while portfolio managers make investment decisions on the investor’s behalf.

Ordinary shareholders benefit from limited liability. If the company loses money, shareholders are not normally required to use personal assets to cover the company’s losses.

The total return on an ordinary share over a period can include:

• Dividend income.

• Capital growth or loss from the change in share price.

The lecture example used:

• Initial share price: $10.

• Dividend received: $1.

• Final share price: $12.

The stated total return was $13 received for each $10 invested, corresponding to a 30% return over the year.

The lecture gave approximately 9% as an indicative average return for the largest NZX 50 companies, while emphasising that individual returns can vary widely. Some companies may have returns around −30%, while others may return approximately 40%. Returns tend to become smoother over longer periods.

8. Risk and beta

The volatility of an ordinary share can be compared with the average volatility of the stock market.

Beta measures the sensitivity of a share’s return to movements in the overall market:

• β = 1: the share moves in line with the market average in the theoretical model.

• β > 1: the share fluctuates more than the market average; it is more aggressive and risky.

• β < 1: the share fluctuates less than the market average; it is more defensive and stable.

A beta of exactly 1 was described as theoretical because a share does not generally move perfectly in line with the market in practice.

Beta can be estimated by plotting:

• Market-average stock-price change on the x-axis.

• The company’s stock-price change on the y-axis.

The slope of the resulting regression line is beta. The lecture referred to an example with β = 1.3, indicating that the company’s share price was more volatile than the market average.

9. Cost of ordinary shares

The cost of ordinary shares, KOS, is related to:

• The risk-free rate of return.

• The beta of the share.

• The expected return on the stock market.

The risk-free return was described as typically being based on the return from a US government bond. The reasoning presented was that if investors do not receive a sufficient return from ordinary shares, they could instead invest in bonds.

The lecture indicated that students need to understand what beta represents, but will not be required to calculate KOS from the full equation. The value will be provided if required.

10. Preference shares

Preference shares provide a fixed dividend. The lecture gave 3% or 4% as illustrative fixed dividend rates.

Compared with ordinary shares, preference shares generally provide:

• A more secure or predictable dividend.

• No voting rights.

• Preferential rights to distributions during a company wind-up, according to the lecture.

The lecturer was uncertain about the exact priority of preference shareholders relative to employees and other claimants during liquidation, and stated that this would be clarified later. The precise legal order of payment should therefore not be inferred from this lecture alone.

The cost of preference shares, KPS, is calculated from the dividend and the price of the share. Students will be given KPS when required rather than calculating it.

11. Worked WACC example

The lecture described a company with:

• Bank loan: $6 million at 5%.

• Bonds: $2 million, issued two years earlier, with an internal rate of return of 7%.

• Ordinary shares: $21 million.

• Preference shares: $6 million.

• KOS and KPS: values supplied in the example.

The total financing was:

$6 million + $2 million + $21 million + $6 million = $35 million

The resulting financing weights were stated as approximately:

• Loans: 17.1%.

• Bonds: 5.7%.

• Ordinary shares: 60%.

• Preference shares: 17.1%.

After applying the relevant financing costs and weights, the lecture stated that the WACC was 5.6%.

That 5.6% WACC would be used as the discount rate for NPV calculations for that company at that time.

The example demonstrates that the discount rate is not simply the interest rate on one loan. It represents the weighted cost of all relevant financing sources.

12. Changes in WACC and sensitivity studies

WACC changes over time because its inputs can change. Examples include:

• Loan interest rates.

• Bond-market conditions.

• Share prices.

• Dividends.

• Risk-free returns.

• Required returns on equity.

• Market risk and beta-related conditions.

A project may therefore need to be re-evaluated after it has started.

Sensitivity studies can be used to examine the effect of uncertainty. Relevant questions include:

• What happens to NPV if the discount rate is slightly wrong?

• What happens if project costs are higher than forecast?

• What happens if revenue is lower than forecast?

• How sensitive is the investment decision to the assumed inflation rate?

Risk can be incorporated by:

• Testing a range of cost and revenue forecasts.

• Applying a factor to costs or revenues, similar to a design safety factor.

• Increasing the discount rate to account for additional uncertainty.

The lecturer advised that, in an organisation, engineers should ask the finance manager or accountant which discount rate should be used. The ordinary interest rate should not automatically be substituted for the company’s discount rate.

13. Nominal and real approaches

Inflation creates two consistent ways to prepare an NPV analysis.

Nominal approach:

• Future cash flows include inflation.

• Future wages, prices, costs, and revenues are increased as appropriate.

• The discount rate is nominal.

• WACC is usually nominal because interest rates, bond-market prices, and related financial values generally incorporate inflation.

If nominal WACC is used, inflation must be included in the future cash flows unless the cash-flow estimates already include it.

Real approach:

• Future cash flows exclude inflation.

• The discount rate must be converted to a real rate.

• A nominal WACC must be reduced to remove the effect of inflation.

The terms “nominal” and “real” must be defined correctly in an NPV question. An inflation rate included in a question may be a distractor if the required approach is already expressed consistently.

The lecturer noted that WACC is usually nominal, but that a WACC may sometimes be reported as a real rate. The stated basis of the WACC must therefore be checked.

14. Inflation and specific growth rates

Future expenses can be forecast using compound growth:

• Present expense increased by a growth factor.

• The growth factor may represent inflation.

• Some texts represent the inflation rate using the symbol π.

• In this context, π means the inflation rate, not the mathematical constant approximately equal to 3.14.

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is a common measure of inflation. However, CPI is based on a broad basket of goods and services, such as food and rent. It may not accurately represent the inflation affecting a particular engineering project.

Where possible, use an inflation or growth rate specific to the relevant:

• Materials.

• Labour.

• Equipment.

• Energy.

• Services.

• Revenue stream.

Different parts of a project may experience different rates of price growth.

15. Fisher equation and real returns

The Fisher equation converts between nominal and real rates by accounting for inflation.

The lecture used an example involving:

• A savings account interest rate of 2.5%.

• Inflation of approximately 4.5%.

Because inflation exceeded the nominal interest rate, the real value of the savings declined. The lecturer stated that the real loss was approximately 1.9% per year, rather than simply subtracting 2.5% from 4.5%.

The same principle applies to wages. If a pay increase is below the inflation rate, the person receives a real pay cut even if the nominal dollar amount increases.

Equations and definitions

Total financing

V = DL + DB + EOS + EPS

Where:

• V = total company financing or capital value.

• DL = debt in loans.

• DB = debt in bonds.

• EOS = equity in ordinary shares.

• EPS = equity in preference shares.

Weighted average cost of capital

The WACC structure described in the lecture is:

WACC = [DL / V] KL(1 − TC) + [DB / V] KB(1 − TC) + [EOS / V] KOS + [EPS / V] KPS

Where:

• KL = cost of loan financing.

• KB = cost of bond financing.

• KOS = cost of ordinary shares.

• KPS = cost of preference shares.

• TC = corporate tax rate.

• The terms DL/V, DB/V, EOS/V, and EPS/V are the financing weights.

The transcript repeatedly called this “WAC”; the standard abbreviation for weighted average cost of capital is WACC.

Ordinary-share return

The ordinary-share return described in the lecture can be represented as:

Return = [D + (P1 − P0)] / P0

Where:

• D = dividend received during the period.

• P0 = initial share price.

• P1 = final share price.

The lecture example used a $1 dividend, an initial price of $10, and a final price of $12, producing the stated 30% return.

Cost of ordinary equity

The transcript described the cost of ordinary shares using the capital asset pricing model:

KOS = Rf + β(Rm − Rf)

Where:

• KOS = cost of ordinary shares.

• Rf = risk-free rate of return.

• β = beta, measuring sensitivity to market movements.

• Rm = expected market return.

• Rm − Rf = market risk premium.

Students were told that they need to understand beta but will not need to calculate KOS in the course assessment unless the required value is provided.

Cost of preference shares

KPS = Dps / Pps

Where:

• KPS = cost of preference shares.

• Dps = fixed dividend per preference share.

• Pps = price of the preference share.

The lecture stated that KPS would be provided when needed.

Inflation-adjusted future value

A future expense or revenue can be forecast using compound growth:

Future value = Present value × (1 + G)^n

Where:

• G = growth or inflation rate.

• n = number of periods.

The transcript noted that some texts use π for the inflation rate in this context. This should not be confused with the mathematical constant π.

Fisher equation

The Fisher relationship can be written as:

1 + nominal rate = (1 + real rate)(1 + inflation rate)

Therefore:

real rate = [(1 + nominal rate) / (1 + inflation rate)] − 1

The nominal and real rates must be paired consistently with the treatment of future cash flows.

Nominal and real NPV consistency

Nominal approach:

• Include inflation in future cash flows.

• Use a nominal WACC.

Real approach:

• Exclude inflation from future cash flows.

• Use a real WACC.

Definitions

• Discount rate: The rate used to convert future cash flows into present value; conceptually, it represents the cost of capital used to fund the project.

• WACC: Weighted average cost of capital; the weighted cost of a company’s debt and equity financing.

• Debt: Borrowed finance that must generally be serviced and repaid.

• Bond: A debt instrument through which investors lend money to a company in exchange for repayment and a return.

• Equity: Ownership capital supplied by shareholders.

• Ordinary share: An equity share with uncertain dividends and usually voting rights.

• Preference share: An equity share with a fixed dividend and generally no voting rights.

• Dividend: A distribution of company profit to shareholders.

• Beta: A measure of a share’s sensitivity to movements in the overall market.

• Risk-free rate: A benchmark return, described in the lecture as typically based on a US government bond.

• Nominal cash flow: A cash flow that includes inflation.

• Real cash flow: A cash flow expressed without inflation.

• CPI: Consumer Price Index; a broad measure of price changes across a basket of goods and services.

• NPV: Net present value; the present value of project cash inflows and outflows after discounting future cash flows.
