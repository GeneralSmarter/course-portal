Detailed notes

Why business knowledge matters to engineers

The lecturer presents engineering as a process that includes more than technical design. A simplified engineering process involves:

1. Identifying clients’ and society’s needs.

2. Conceiving possible future solutions.

3. Predicting performance.

4. Making an investment decision.

5. Delivering the solution:

• On time.

• Safely.

• Within predicted cost.

• With predicted performance.

• With minimal environmental impact.

Engineers often regard idea generation and performance prediction as “real engineering”, while treating business, investment, delivery, legal, and organisational activities as peripheral. The lecture argues that these “grey” areas form a large part of engineering practice.

Engineers need to understand the value they create because this can help them:

• Explain and sell the importance of their work.

• Improve their earning potential.

• Strengthen their professional reputation and job security.

• Focus work on meaningful outcomes.

• Assess whether a proposed process or project is genuinely useful.

A value-based question for evaluating a new process is whether it:

• Improves the experience of users or clients.

• Improves research or other organisational outcomes.

• Makes it easier for staff to perform valuable work.

• Provides a clear benefit that justifies its cost and complexity.

The lecture also emphasises collaboration with people who control or provide financial resources. An engineering idea cannot be implemented without access to money, investment, or other resources. Understanding cost estimation and charge-out rates is therefore important for delivering value.

An engineering degree can support both employment and entrepreneurship. Engineers may use these concepts to start and manage their own businesses.

Corporate governance

Corporate governance is described broadly as how a company is run.

Good corporate governance can lead to:

• Improved organisational performance.

• A wider and more objective view of the business.

• Accountability and oversight.

• Better management of corporate risk.

• A balance between short-term and long-term objectives.

• Better economic, social, and environmental outcomes.

The lecturer links sustainability activities to governance. Sustainability initiatives are not necessarily separate from governance; when they are integrated into responsible decision-making, they may contribute to overall organisational performance.

Poor governance can contribute to:

• Tax avoidance or other regulatory problems.

• Corruption.

• Excessive executive remuneration.

• Poorly challenged or unsupported decisions.

• Failure to recognise serious organisational problems.

• Reputational damage.

• Legal consequences.

The lecture uses the United Kingdom Post Office Horizon scandal as an example of poor governance. According to the lecture, franchisees were required to use a fundamentally flawed software system, while the Post Office became convinced that it was not responsible for the resulting problems. The lecturer presents the case as an example of how an organisation can fail to question its assumptions and properly oversee decisions.

Basic New Zealand company structure

The lecture refers to the New Zealand Companies Act 1993 as the legislation governing the basic company structure.

A company has:

• A legal name.

• One or more shares.

• One or more shareholders who own the shares.

• One or more directors.

When there is more than one director, the directors normally form a board.

A company may use a trading name that differs from its legal entity name. The lecture gives Two Degrees as an example:

• Legal entity: Two Degrees Mobile Limited.

• Trading name: Two Degrees.

A trading name is not a separate legal entity. The lecture states that:

• A trading name should be protected through a trademark.

• There is not a separate register for trading names; trademark registration is relevant to protecting the brand.

• A trading name should not be used as the legal contracting party by default.

• A contract can identify the legal entity first and then define the trading name for later reference.

For example, a contract may identify “Two Degrees Mobile Limited” and state that it will subsequently be referred to as “Two Degrees”.

Main business structures

#### Sole trader

A sole trader is an individual who operates a business on their own.

The individual is effectively:

• The owner.

• The decision-maker.

• The person responsible for the business.

• The person exposed to the business’s financial and legal obligations.

A local electrician is given as an example.

#### Partnership

A partnership involves two or more people operating a business together.

The partners generally:

• Own and operate the business together.

• Share responsibility for its activities.

• Have personal financial and legal responsibility for the business.

A small engineering consultancy is given as a possible example. The lecturer states that partnerships are relatively uncommon in New Zealand and that some businesses instead form private companies to limit the liability of individuals.

#### Limited liability company

A limited liability company is owned by shareholders and is a separate legal entity.

Shareholders may include:

• Individuals.

• Trusts.

• Governments.

• Other companies.

The company itself is responsible for its legal and financial obligations. Shareholders are generally liable only for the money they have invested in the company. Their personal assets are therefore generally protected from the company’s liabilities.

Limited liability does not mean that the company has no liability. It means that the liability is generally held by the company rather than transferred directly to shareholders.

The lecture uses the following examples:

• Two Degrees Mobile Limited is described as privately owned.

• Spark is described as publicly owned, with shares listed on the stock exchange.

• Meridian Energy is described as having a mixture of government ownership and publicly traded ownership.

The lecturer states that New Zealand company names do not necessarily distinguish between private and publicly listed limited companies. Whether a company is publicly listed depends on its status on a stock exchange, not simply on its legal name.

A company may list on a stock exchange to raise capital. The trade-off is that it sells part of its ownership to investors.

#### Cooperative

A cooperative is presented as a special type of limited liability company in which members own and control the organisation for their mutual benefit.

Characteristics include:

• Members are both users and owners.

• Members have a say in how the organisation is run.

• Benefits or profits are returned according to member participation rather than simply according to share ownership.

• Members may receive additional benefits, such as discounts or services.

Fonterra is used as the main example. Farmers participate by supplying milk to the cooperative, and payments are linked to the quantity of milk solids supplied.

The lecturer also mentions that some health-insurance providers can operate as cooperatives, with members owning and influencing the organisation.

The lecturer explicitly states that cooperatives are not expected to be a major assessment focus.

#### Franchise

A franchise involves purchasing the right to operate a business using another organisation’s brand, systems, products, or business model.

The franchisee may receive:

• Brand rights.

• Operating systems.

• Recipes or product specifications.

• Marketing support.

• Permission to trade under the franchisor’s brand.

The franchisee generally pays money back to the franchisor.

Examples mentioned include:

• Domino’s.

• McDonald’s.

• Subway.

• Jim’s Mowing.

• Mainfreight franchises.

The company operating a franchised store may not have the same legal name as the consumer-facing brand. Receipts and bank statements may therefore show a company name different from the visible brand.

Directors and their responsibilities

Directors are responsible for ensuring that the company is run properly.

The lecture states that directors must:

• Comply with the Companies Act.

• Comply with the company’s constitution.

• Act in good faith.

• Exercise their powers with due care.

• Keep good records.

• Make properly considered and justified decisions.

• Protect confidential company information.

Directors may delegate tasks or powers to individuals or committees, but delegation does not remove their ultimate responsibility.

Directors may be paid or unpaid, although they are commonly paid.

The lecture warns against directors:

• Acting in bad faith.

• Breaching the Companies Act or the company constitution.

• Knowingly making decisions that create serious loss or problems for the company.

• Making decisions without obtaining appropriate advice when advice is reasonably required.

• Disclosing confidential company information.

A decision that turns out badly is not automatically evidence of director fault. Business decisions involve uncertainty. The relevant issue may include whether:

• The decision was reasonable at the time.

• The director knowingly made a bad decision.

• Appropriate advice was considered.

• The decision was properly justified.

• Adequate records were kept.

• The decision was reasonably practicable in the circumstances.

Good records are important because they show the reasoning and evidence behind a decision, particularly if the outcome is later adverse.

Types of directors

#### Executive director

An executive director is also an employee of the company.

They:

• Participate in day-to-day management or operational work.

• Bring detailed knowledge of what is happening inside the company.

• Contribute practical knowledge to board decisions.

A managing director is given as an example.

#### Non-executive director

A non-executive director is not an employee of the company.

They are appointed to provide:

• External experience.

• A different perspective.

• Challenge to internal assumptions.

• Representation of significant shareholders in some cases.

A non-executive director may represent a large institutional investor, such as a KiwiSaver or superannuation organisation.

#### Independent director

An independent director is intended to have no significant relationship or financial interest that compromises their independence from the company.

Their purpose is to provide an independent perspective on the company’s activities and decisions.

Board structure and roles

#### Chair

The chair leads the board.

Responsibilities include:

• Setting the agenda.

• Running meetings.

• Ensuring different views are heard.

• Helping the board reach consensus.

• Ensuring decisions are clearly understood.

• Ensuring decisions are formally recorded.

#### Company secretary

The secretary supports the governance process.

Responsibilities include:

• Recording board decisions.

• Maintaining governance records.

• Helping ensure compliance with the company constitution and relevant legislation.

• Supporting the chair in running meetings.

Good minutes and records provide evidence of the process that led to a decision.

#### Committees

Committees may operate under the board and focus on specific organisational needs.

A committee may be chaired by a director with a relevant portfolio. Directors can delegate tasks to committees, but they remain responsible for the decisions and work delegated to them.

When refreshing or rebuilding a board, a company may identify areas where it lacks expertise and recruit directors accordingly. For example, if the board is weak in health and safety, it may seek a director with health-and-safety experience.

• Executive expertise.

A monopoly exists when a single company controls nearly all of the supply of a good or service.

Economic value and value added

Equations and definitions

Value added

Value added at a production stage can be expressed as:

Value added = value of output − value of intermediate inputs

For the simplified bread example:

• Farming value added = $1,200 − $700 = $500.

• Milling value added = $1,800 − $1,200 = $600, assuming the implied flour output value is $1,800.

• Bread production value added = $2,600 − $1,800 = $800, assuming the implied intermediate flour value is $1,800.

The $1,800 intermediate flour value is inferred from the stated value-added figures and final price; it is not explicitly stated in a complete sentence in the transcript.

GDP using the value-added method

GDP = sum of value added across all relevant production stages

For the simplified example:

GDP contribution = $500 + $600 + $800 = $1,900

This is the total value added across the stages represented in the example. It is not the same as adding all transaction prices, which would double-count intermediate goods.
