Overview

This lecture introduced intellectual property (IP), with primary emphasis on patents and their relevance to engineering, commercialisation, and technology strategy.

The lecture covered:

• IP as protection for products of human intellect, including inventions, designs, written material, branding, and technical improvements.

• The commercial importance of IP as a source of competitive advantage and business value.

• The main types of IP protection and the roles of government agencies, patent attorneys, and lawyers.

• Patents as time-limited, geographically defined exclusive rights granted in exchange for public disclosure.

• Patent strategy, including filing timing, secrecy, commercial markets, manufacturing locations, licensing, and patent “thicketing”.

• The distinction between patent protection for function and other IP rights that protect appearance, branding, or creative expression.

• New Zealand patent requirements: novelty, enablement, inventive step, and credible utility.

• Limits and uncertainties surrounding software patents, AI-generated inventions, Māori traditional knowledge, and indigenous plants and animals.

• The practical importance of preserving confidentiality before filing a patent application.

The lecture was delivered by Virginia Nichols, a patent attorney, and focused mainly on the New Zealand system while noting that patent law varies significantly between countries.

Key concepts

• Intellectual property: An umbrella term covering creations and outputs of human intellect, such as inventions, improvements, written or drawn works, designs, brands, and technical knowledge.

• Patent: An exclusive statutory monopoly granted by the government for a limited period in exchange for disclosure of the invention.

• Negative legal right: A patent gives the owner the right to stop others from carrying out specified acts. It does not automatically give the owner permission to use the invention.

• Patent term: In the New Zealand system described in the lecture, a patent lasts 20 years from the date of filing the complete patent specification. The lecturer stated that this term generally cannot be extended in New Zealand.

• Patent disclosure bargain: The inventor discloses how the invention works; in return, the government grants exclusive rights for a limited time. After the term ends, the disclosed technology becomes available for public use.

• Novelty: The invention must not already have been disclosed or done anywhere in the world.

• Prior art: Existing knowledge used to assess whether an invention is new and inventive. The lecture stated that the New Zealand prior-art base can include material from any country, language, and format, including written and oral disclosures.

• Enablement: The patent application must describe the invention with enough detail that a suitably skilled person can perform it without having to reinvent essential aspects.

• Inventive step: The invention must not be an obvious development to a notional skilled person working in the relevant field.

• Credible utility: The invention must have specific, substantial, and credible usefulness.

• Patent strategy: Decisions about whether, when, and where to seek protection, based on cost, markets, manufacturing, competitors, development timelines, funding, and commercial objectives.

• Patent thicketing: Building a series of patents around successive genuine improvements to a technology, potentially extending practical commercial control even though each patent retains its own term.

• Licensing: Giving another party permission to use IP under agreed conditions, usually in exchange for payment or another benefit.

• Cross-licensing: Two parties licensing relevant technologies or improvements to each other.

• Trade secret/confidential information: An alternative to patenting in which valuable technical information is kept secret rather than disclosed publicly.

• Function versus form: Patents generally protect how something works or is used; other IP rights may protect how it looks, is branded, or is expressed.

• Territorial protection: Patents are granted by individual jurisdictions. There is no single worldwide patent.

• Inventorship: The people who made an inventive contribution must be recognised as inventors on the patent document. Ownership and inventorship are distinct issues.

• Standard-essential patent: A patent covering technology that is necessary to comply with an industry standard. The lecture associated these with telecommunications and licensing on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory terms.

• Springboarding: Developing or testing technology during an existing patent term so that it can potentially be commercialised after the patent expires. The lecturer linked this to research and experimental-use exceptions, while noting uncertainty in the New Zealand case law.

• Compulsory licensing: A mechanism under which a court may grant a licence where there is a demonstrated need and the patent owner refuses reasonable commercial terms.

• Crown use: In an emergency, the Crown may use patented technology and resolve compensation or licensing issues afterward.

• Bioprospecting: Taking biological resources or traditional knowledge and exploiting or patenting them without appropriate engagement, recognition, or benefit for the communities connected to that knowledge or resource.

Detailed notes

Intellectual property and engineering

IP was presented as central to engineering because engineering work commonly produces:

• New products.

• New processes.

• Technical improvements.

• More efficient designs.

• Cost-saving methods.

• New electrical devices, circuits, chemicals, and mechanical systems.

• Solutions to practical problems.

Examples of engineering-related IP included chip design, parallel processing, GPUs, improved energy and water efficiency, jet boats, ski lifts, trampolines, washing-machine operation, and electrical circuits.

IP can create competitive advantage through:

• Better technical performance.

• Lower production cost.

• More efficient processes.

• Stronger or more popular branding.

• More attractive styling.

• Protection from competitors copying or entering a market.

The lecturer stated that IP may represent approximately 80% of business value in general discussions, and possibly closer to 90% for top-performing companies. This percentage was presented informally and should not be treated as a universal measured rule.

IP strategy is not identical for every company. Relevant factors include:

• The cost of obtaining and maintaining protection.

• Development pathways.

• Funding requirements.

• Commercialisation plans.

• Target markets.

• Manufacturing locations.

• Whether the goal is to generate income, block competitors, or support a broader public benefit.

• The risk that protection in one jurisdiction may not be useful in another.

Sources of IP assistance

The lecture distinguished between three groups:

• The government IP office: Administers relevant legislation and provides general information. The transcript rendered the name as “IPONS” or “Ions”; this is likely an automated-transcription error and should be checked against the official New Zealand agency name.

• Patent attorneys: Specialists qualified to assist with patent applications and other IP matters. The lecturer stated that patent attorneys are the only people permitted to charge for helping obtain a patent.

• Lawyers: Particularly useful for infringement disputes, company structures, shareholder agreements, licensing agreements, and other commercialisation arrangements.

The lecturer emphasised that ownership arrangements should be settled early when multiple people contribute money, technical knowledge, or other resources.

Main types of IP protection

The lecture described IP rights as overlapping imperfectly with different types of intellectual output:

• Patents: New products, processes, and technical improvements.

• Confidential information and trade secrets: Secret technical or commercial information.

• Copyright: Written, drawn, and other creative expression.

• Registered designs: The visual appearance or eye appeal of a product.

• Trademarks: Brand identifiers.

• Plant variety rights: Specific protection for plant varieties.

• Circuit layout designs: Protection for circuit layout configurations.

• Geographical indications: Signs associated with products from a particular geographical origin, such as wine or Scotch whisky.

The lecturer noted that legal categories can contain gaps and overlaps, so one creation may involve several types of protection.

What a patent provides

A patent was defined as an exclusive statutory monopoly. In the lecture’s simplified description, the owner has exclusive rights to:

• Manufacture.

• Sell.

• Use.

• Import.

• Market.

• Licence.

The patent is not granted for free. The required exchange is disclosure of:

• What the invention is.

• How the invention works.

• Enough information to allow the invention to be implemented.

The purpose of disclosure is that, after the patent term, the technology can become publicly available.

A patent is an asset that may be:

• Bought.

• Sold.

• Licensed.

• Transferred.

• Recorded as part of a company’s assets or commercial position.

The lecturer described the patent as a negative legal right: it allows the owner to stop certain acts by others, but does not necessarily mean that the owner can legally practise the invention. Other patents, regulations, safety laws, or third-party rights may still restrict use.

Patent duration and publication

The lecture stated that in New Zealand:

• The patent term is 20 years from the date of the complete patent specification.

• The term generally cannot be extended.

• Earlier New Zealand law had a 16-year term.

• The move to 20 years was associated with international harmonisation and the TRIPS agreement, although the lecturer was not certain of the precise policy reason.

• Some countries have had or still have mechanisms for extending terms for certain pharmaceuticals; the lecturer stated that New Zealand removed such mechanisms in the 1990s.

The lecturer described a filing and publication sequence in which:

1. An initial filing is confidential.

2. A further filing occurs one year later.

3. The application becomes public 18 months after that further stage.

The exact procedural description was informal and should be checked against current filing rules before being relied on. The important strategic point is that an application eventually becomes public even if a patent is not ultimately granted.

Once published, the disclosure may affect the ability to obtain protection elsewhere. Therefore, filing and publication timing must be considered before commercial disclosure.

Territorial nature of patents

There is no single worldwide patent. Each country or jurisdiction has its own patent system, and protection costs money in each jurisdiction.

A company should consider:

• Where its major markets are.

• Where it expects to sell products.

• Where it manufactures products.

• Where competitors manufacture.

• Where a monopoly would provide meaningful commercial value.

• Whether the cost of protection is justified in each country.

The ski-lift example illustrated this approach: protection was sought in countries with significant ski industries, but not necessarily in countries where the company was unlikely to sell ski lifts.

Improvements and patent thicketing

An improvement to an existing patented invention may itself be patentable if it satisfies the legal requirements.

The lecturer distinguished between:

• An improvement that is merely an obvious or non-inventive modification. This may receive some additional protection according to the lecture, but remains tied to the original 20-year term.

• A genuinely inventive improvement. This may qualify for its own patent and its own 20-year term.

The lecturer called the practice of obtaining successive patents for qualifying improvements “patent thicketing”. Examples included:

• Dyson vacuum cleaners, with successive cyclone and handheld improvements.

• Springfree trampolines, originally developed at the University of Canterbury, followed by an improved spring material for the spring pylons.

An improvement patent may depend on the original patent. A company with the original patent may need to license the improvement, while the improvement owner may need a licence to use the original technology. This can lead to:

• Licensing negotiations.

• Cross-licensing.

• Commercial cooperation between competitors.

An improvement to somebody else’s patented product may therefore be patentable, but patentability does not necessarily mean the improver can commercialise it without permission from the original patent owner.

Secrecy versus patenting

Patents are especially useful where an invention can be reverse engineered from a product or process. Without patent protection, competitors may be able to inspect the product and reproduce its function.

A trade secret may be preferable where:

• The technology cannot easily be reverse engineered.

• The secret can be kept confidential.

• Disclosure would give competitors too much information.

• The expected value of secrecy exceeds the value of a time-limited patent monopoly.

The trade-off is:

• Patent: disclose the invention and obtain exclusion rights for a limited period.

• Trade secret: retain secrecy indefinitely in principle, but risk losing protection if the information is independently discovered, disclosed, or reverse engineered.

Infringement and permitted activities

The lecturer stated that infringement occurs when a person carries out an act within the scope of the patent claims, even if the person:

• Did not know that the invention was patented.

• Developed the product independently.

• Did not deliberately copy the patent.

The practical question may be whether the patent owner discovers the conduct and chooses to act, but lack of knowledge does not necessarily prevent technical infringement.

The lecturer identified possible exclusions or exceptions for:

• Research.

• Experimental use.

• Some forms of in-house development.

• Certain commercial staple products.

• Use by an end user after a product has been sold, depending on the circumstances.

The boundaries of experimental use were described as uncertain because there is limited New Zealand case law.

The lecture also discussed exhaustion of rights and resale or repair of patented products. Ink-cartridge refilling was given as an Australian example involving questions about whether patent rights are exhausted after first sale. The lecturer did not recall the final Australian outcome.

The lecture stated that New Zealand’s right-to-repair position remained unsettled and had been the subject of proposed political action, but no clearly defined general rule was presented.

Public-interest uses and non-use

A patent owner may choose not to use the patented technology and may effectively “sit on” the patent during its term. This is consistent with the patent being a right to stop others, rather than a positive obligation or permission to use the technology.

New Zealand was described as having:

• A compulsory licensing mechanism where a demonstrated need exists and the owner refuses reasonable commercial terms.

• Crown-use provisions allowing government use during a state of emergency, with compensation or licensing addressed afterward.

The lecturer said compulsory licensing is rarely or never used in practice but can operate as a deterrent that encourages negotiation.

Patents and standards

The lecture introduced standard-essential patents in the context of telecommunications and interoperable technologies.

When a patented technology becomes necessary to comply with a standard:

• Other manufacturers may need access to the patented technology.

• The patent owner may be required to license it on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory terms.

• Governments or standards systems may impose a legal framework over the patent rights.

USB-C was used as an illustrative example of standardisation, although the exact legal and patent details of that example were not developed.

Patents for function rather than appearance

Patents protect function rather than merely appearance.

If two machines look different but carry out the same claimed sequence of operations to produce the same result, the second machine may infringe if it falls within the patent claims.

The scope of protection is defined primarily by the wording of the claims. Claim drafting must therefore:

• Capture the essential technical features.

• Avoid unnecessarily narrow language.

• Account for possible variations in implementation.

• Specify the required sequence where order is technically necessary.

Whether the order of steps matters depends on the wording of the claims and the technical logic of the process. If step B cannot happen before step A, the claim may need to reflect that dependency.

Software and computer-implemented inventions

The lecturer described New Zealand’s treatment of computer programmes as complex.

The lecture referred to section 11 of the Patents Act and summarised the structure as:

• A computer programme is not an invention.

• This exclusion applies to the extent that the invention relates to a computer programme “as such”.

• If the contribution is more than the fact that it is software, other technical effects may be considered.

The lecturer contrasted earlier New Zealand practice, where software could be patentable if it produced a commercially useful effect or artificial state of affairs, with the later statutory framework introduced in 2013.

The practical guidance was that software-related inventions are more likely to be patentable when tied to:

• Hardware.

• A physical process.

• A real-world effect.

• A technical operation outside the software itself.

The washing-machine example described a process for operating a washing machine to improve washing efficiency. This was associated in the lecture with Fisher & Paykel and was informally called the “Fisher and Paykel exception”.

The lecturer stressed that software patentability differs between New Zealand, Australia, Europe, and the United States. Overseas examples, especially US examples, should not automatically be treated as New Zealand law.

Artificial intelligence and inventorship

The lecture discussed cases involving attempts to name an AI system as an inventor.

The lecturer stated that courts had generally held that an inventor must currently be a human being. An AI system cannot presently be listed as the inventor merely because it generated an output.

The example involved Steven Thaler and an AI system referred to in the transcript as “DABUS”, although the transcript contains substantial recognition errors around the name and description. The applications were described as deliberately testing whether an AI system could be recognised as an inventor.

The lecture distinguished between:

• AI allegedly producing an invention without human inventive input.

• AI being used as a tool by a human inventor.

Where a human contributes the inventive concept, selection, direction, or development, the inventorship analysis may differ. The lecturer described this as an evolving area involving questions about what constitutes inventive input.

The issue also connects to copyright and whether prompting or directing an AI system amounts to sufficient human creative contribution.

Māori traditional knowledge and indigenous resources

New Zealand patent examination may involve specific considerations where an invention uses:

• Māori traditional knowledge.

• Indigenous flora or fauna.

• Taonga species.

• Knowledge associated with Māori communities.

The lecture stated that such applications may be considered by a Māori advisory committee associated with the New Zealand IP administration system.

Relevant questions include:

• Whether the invention derives from Māori knowledge.

• Whether it uses indigenous plants or animals.

• Who was involved in developing the invention.

• Whether the developer engaged with the relevant community or local rūnanga.

• Whether commercial exploitation would be contrary to Māori values.

• Whether the people with a kaitiaki relationship to the resource or knowledge have been properly consulted.

The purpose is partly to prevent bioprospecting: extracting and patenting traditional knowledge or biological resources without meaningful consultation, recognition, or benefit returning to the people connected with them.

The lecturer also referred to the Wai 262 claim and suggested that future government action might strengthen protection in this area. This was presented as an ongoing policy matter rather than a settled technical rule.

Requirements for obtaining a patent

The lecture summarised four central requirements:

1. Novelty

• The invention must be new.

• It must not have been done or described before.

• The prior-art base now includes worldwide disclosures in any language and format.

• Independent development does not restore novelty if the same invention was already publicly available.

2. Enablement

• The application must contain enough technical detail for a skilled person to perform the invention.

• The reader should not need to reinvent essential parts of the invention.

3. Inventive step

• The invention must not be obvious to a suitably skilled person in the field.

• Routine workshop development is unlikely to qualify.

• An “aha” moment or non-obvious technical contribution may support inventiveness.

• The assessment must avoid hindsight.

• Combining ideas from disparate technical fields may be inventive, whereas applying common general knowledge in an expected way may be obvious.

4. Utility

• The invention must have specific, substantial, and credible utility.

• A claim of “free energy” based on an ordinary-looking transformer was given as an example of an application rejected for lack of credible utility.

Grace periods and disclosure risk

The lecturer stated that New Zealand has a grace period associated with the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership for inventions developed after 2019.

However:

• Not all countries recognise the same grace period.

• Some Asian and European countries may not recognise it.

• Relying on a grace period can therefore destroy international filing options.

Potential novelty-destroying disclosures include:

• Publishing the invention.

• Non-confidential presentations.

• Commercial sales.

• Public use.

• Commercial use of a process.

The main practical rule was:

> If an invention may be patentable, keep it secret until an appropriate patent strategy has been assessed and filing has occurred.
