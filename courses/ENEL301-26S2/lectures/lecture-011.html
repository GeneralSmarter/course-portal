Key concepts

• Sustainability has no single universally agreed definition. It is complex, contested, and context-dependent.

• The commonly cited Brundtland definition describes sustainable development as development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

• Sustainable development involves balancing economic, social, and environmental outcomes. Cultural considerations may be treated as part of the social or societal pillar.

• Sustainability is relevant to corporate governance because it can help organisations identify and manage risk, make better decisions, improve environmental outcomes, engage stakeholders, and attract people whose values align with the organisation.

• Engineering decisions are made under constraints and usually require explicit trade-offs between competing factors.

• Good decision processes should:

• Involve people affected by the decision.

• Be as transparent as possible.

• Recognise and manage uncertainty.

• Use decision gates where appropriate.

• Attempt to minimise bias.

• Consider whether the decision creates meaningful value.

• Align engineering work with the organisation’s objectives.

• Systems thinking is used to analyse, understand, model, and communicate complex systems.

• A system is an organised assembly of connected components that performs some function or produces some behaviour.

• Complex systems can produce unintended outcomes because changes in one part of the system may propagate through interactions, feedback loops, delays, and other subsystems.

• A causal loop diagram represents qualitative cause-and-effect relationships between system variables.

• A reinforcing loop amplifies change. A balancing loop counteracts change and tends to move a system towards a target or equilibrium.

• Causal loop diagrams are qualitative: they show the direction and structure of relationships but do not quantify relationship strengths.

• System boundaries define what is included in the analysis and what is excluded. Boundary selection affects the conclusions that can be drawn.

• Sustainability assessments should be integrated rather than divided into isolated components because environmental, economic, social, and systems analyses influence one another.

Engineering decisions under constraints

Engineering judgments and decisions are always made under constraints, similar to engineering design.

A structured decision process is useful because it:

• Makes the decision method explicit.

• Clarifies how competing factors will be traded off.

• Helps reveal assumptions.

• Supports transparent communication.

• Provides a basis for revisiting the decision as new information becomes available.

Stakeholder engagement is critical because people affected by a decision should have an opportunity to contribute. Transparency helps prevent stakeholders from being surprised by the outcome.

Decisions can be made through gates:

1. A project may begin with substantial uncertainty.

2. An initial decision may be made using the information available at that stage.

3. As the project progresses, uncertainty may reduce or change.

4. Further decisions can then be made at defined points.

The proposed second bridge for Auckland was used as an example of a large, uncertain project progressing through decision gates. The lecturer noted that such gates can require considerable expenditure before a final decision about whether, where, how, and when a project should proceed.

Engineers should also:

• Actively attempt to avoid bias.

• Consider whether their work adds value.

• Think about the organisation’s objectives.

• Ensure engineering activity is aligned with what the employing company is trying to achieve.

Causal loop diagrams

Causal loop diagrams describe relationships between elements in a system and help unpack causes and outcomes.

They are useful for identifying feedback structures.

#### Homeostasis example

Homeostasis is the ability of a living organism to maintain a relatively consistent internal condition.

Examples discussed:

• Maintaining body temperature.

• Maintaining blood-sugar levels through insulin release.

• Sweating when body temperature is too high.

• Shivering when body temperature is too low.

A simplified temperature-regulation sequence is:

1. Define an ideal body temperature, approximately 37 °C for the example.

2. Compare actual body temperature with the ideal temperature.

3. Identify a temperature gap.

4. If actual temperature is lower than ideal, shivering increases.

5. Shivering raises actual body temperature towards the ideal state.

The lecturer noted that actual ideal body temperature varies between people and can vary with time of day and other factors.

This is an example of a balancing feedback loop because the response counteracts the deviation from the desired condition.

#### Pharmacy dispensing example

The pharmacy example illustrated both reinforcing and balancing feedback loops.

Reinforcing loop:

1. Dispensing errors increase rework.

2. More rework increases the pharmacist’s workload.

3. Increased workload puts pressure on scheduling.

4. Scheduling pressure increases the likelihood of further dispensing errors.

5. Further errors create more rework.

This loop amplifies the original problem.

Balancing loop:

1. Increased pressure may increase a pharmacist’s productivity.

2. Increased productivity allows more work to be handled.

3. The amount of outstanding work is reduced.

4. Reduced outstanding work counteracts the pressure.

The lecturer noted that the productivity response is not necessarily guaranteed; it was presented as an argument or possible relationship.

#### CLD notation and construction

When creating causal loop diagrams:

• Use nouns when naming variables or nodes rather than verbs.

• Draw causal links in the correct direction.

• Indicate whether a relationship changes in the same or opposite direction.

• Use a plus sign for a same-direction relationship.

• Use a minus sign for an opposite-direction relationship.

• Identify loops as reinforcing or balancing.

• Check the direction and interpretation of every link.

The transcript’s wording for the plus/minus notation is somewhat compressed, but the intended distinction is between same-direction and opposite-direction causal effects.

CLDs are qualitative. They do not quantify the strength of relationships, but they provide a useful representation of how a system may behave.
