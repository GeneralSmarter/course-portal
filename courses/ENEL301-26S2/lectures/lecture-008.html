Key concepts

• Consideration: Something of legal value exchanged between the parties; it is sometimes described as the “price of the promise.”

• Sufficient consideration: Consideration must be real and legally recognised.

• Adequate consideration: The value exchanged does not have to be economically equivalent or fair.

• Nominal consideration: A very small amount, such as $1 or a peppercorn, may satisfy the requirement for consideration.

• Deed: A formal legal instrument that can make an arrangement enforceable without consideration.

• Legal person: A person or entity recognised by law as having legal rights and obligations.

• Natural person: A human being.

• Artificial person: An organisation recognised as a separate legal person, such as a company or body corporate.

• Minor: For the New Zealand rule discussed, a person under 18.

• Capacity: The legal ability to enter into an enforceable contract.

• Voidable contract: A contract that remains effective unless the entitled party chooses to avoid it.

• Duress: Entering a contract because of threats or coercion, including threats of physical harm or property damage.

• Undue influence: Unfair domination or influence arising from a relationship of trust and confidence.

• Unconscionability: Taking deliberate advantage of another party’s serious disadvantage.

• Misrepresentation: A false statement made before a contract is formed that may induce the other party to contract.

• Mistake: An error by one or both parties concerning a matter connected with the contract.

• Illegality: A contract or contractual purpose that is contrary to legislation, criminal law, public policy, or the administration of justice.

• Exclusion clause: A contractual term attempting to remove liability entirely.

• Limitation clause: A contractual term restricting liability to a specified amount or extent.

• Tort: A civil or private wrong independent of contract.

• Negligence: Liability for harm caused by a failure to take reasonable care in relation to foreseeable risks.

• ACC: New Zealand’s Accident Compensation scheme, which generally prevents lawsuits for personal injury caused by accidents.

• Neighbour principle: The obligation to take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to persons closely and directly affected by one’s acts or omissions.

Detailed notes

Contract formation and consideration

The lecturer stated that the lecture began with the third basic requirement for contract formation: consideration. Consideration is not being used in its ordinary everyday sense. It is an old legal doctrine requiring an exchange between the parties.

Each party must give something and receive something in return. This may take the form of:

• Reciprocal obligations.

• An exchange of promises.

• Goods exchanged for money.

• Services exchanged for payment.

• Labour or other legally recognised acts or promises.

A promise to make a purely gratuitous gift is generally not enforceable as a contract because nothing has been given in exchange. The example used was a promise to transfer shares worth $1 million where the recipient gives nothing in return. The promise would not generally be enforceable as a contract.

The consideration must be sufficient, meaning that it must be real and valuable in the eyes of the law. Examples of recognised consideration include:

• Payment or a promise to pay money.

• Transfer of goods.

• Transfer of land.

• Provision of services.

• Provision of labour.

The amount of consideration does not need to be adequate. The law does not generally require the exchange to be economically balanced or fair. It is enough that each party provides something legally recognised as consideration.

The lecturer used the example of shares worth $1 million being transferred in exchange for $1. The dollar is sufficient consideration even though it is not adequate compensation for the value of the shares. The purpose is not to balance the transaction economically; it is to establish that there is an exchange rather than a purely one-sided gift.

The same principle explains transactions in which businesses or assets are sold for $1. The lecturer also described peppercorn leases, where land or buildings are leased in exchange for a single peppercorn. The peppercorn is nominal but can function as consideration.

Under the English-based contract-law approach discussed in the lecture:

• Gifts are not contracts.

• One-sided promises are generally not enforceable contractually.

• Consideration is required unless an alternative legal form, such as a deed, is used.

Deeds and contracts under seal

A deed is a formal legal method of recording an arrangement. The lecturer stated that the requirement for consideration does not apply to contracts recorded by deed.

Historically, deeds were described as contracts “under seal.” A document was signed, sealed with sealing wax, and delivered to the other party. This is the origin of the phrase “signed, sealed, and delivered.”

The lecturer noted that consideration is a feature of legal systems derived from English contract law and that other legal systems may not have the same requirement.

Capacity to contract

The lecturer distinguished between requirements for contract formation and other factors that may create defects or enforceability problems. Capacity was introduced as one of those later issues.

Generally, legal persons have capacity to enter contracts. The two categories identified were:

• Natural persons: Human beings.

• Artificial persons: Organisations recognised by law as separate legal persons, such as companies or bodies corporate.

A company is legally separate from the natural persons involved in operating or owning it. When someone contracts with a business operated through a company, the contracting party is the company itself, not necessarily its directors, shareholders, or other individuals.

Minors’ contracts in New Zealand

A minor was described as a person under 18. The lecturer stated that, in New Zealand, contracts are generally unenforceable against minors unless a court finds that enforcement would be fair and reasonable.

This means that a party contracting with a person under 18 generally cannot simply enforce the contract against that minor. The party would first need to go to court and establish that enforcement is fair and reasonable. The lecturer stated that this is expensive and has reportedly happened only rarely since the relevant rule was introduced by statute in 1969.

Minors enter into everyday consumer transactions, such as buying goods from supermarkets, dairies, and service stations. These transactions are workable because the minor normally pays before receiving the goods. The minor has already performed their side of the arrangement, so there is usually no need to enforce the contract against them later.

The problem is more significant when:

1. A business performs its side of the contract first.

2. The minor promises to perform later.

3. The business needs to enforce the minor’s future obligation.

The lecturer used car finance as an example. A finance company may advance money to fund a vehicle purchase, with repayment expected over time. If the borrower is a minor, the finance company may have difficulty enforcing the repayment obligation.

Businesses address this risk by:

• Refusing to contract with minors.

• Requiring an adult co-signer.

• Requiring an adult guarantor.

The lecturer identified statutory exceptions under which some contracts are enforceable against minors, including:

• Employment contracts.

• Life-insurance contracts.

• Contracts pre-approved by a court.

The lecturer stated that court pre-approval is virtually never used in practice.

The relevant point in a question involving a minor reaching adulthood is the time at which the contract was entered into. If the person was a minor when the contract was made, subsequently turning 18 does not change that fact.

Intoxication and contractual capacity

A contract may be voidable where:

1. A person is so intoxicated that they do not understand what they are doing.

2. The other party knows or appreciates that the person is in that condition.

The threshold is high. Mere intoxication, poor judgment, or reduced inhibitions is not enough. The person must not understand the nature of what they are agreeing to, and the other party must recognise that lack of understanding.

If the requirements are met, the intoxicated person may later avoid the contract and escape liability under it.

Evidence may be important. Relevant evidence could include:

• Witnesses who observed the person’s condition.

• The amount and type of alcohol consumed.

• Purchases made at a pub.

• CCTV footage.

• Independent observations of the person’s behaviour.

The lecturer emphasised that proving the required state of intoxication may be difficult, particularly where there are no independent witnesses or records.

Genuine consent

The lecture then moved to issues affecting whether the parties gave genuine consent. These issues may provide a legal remedy even where a contract appears to have been formed.

The first broad category was unfair treatment.

#### Duress

Duress involves entering a contract because of threats or coercion. The lecturer gave the example of holding a gun to someone’s head and demanding that they sign a contract.

A contract entered into under duress may be avoided, meaning the affected party may later escape its consequences.

The transcript specifically referred to threats involving:

• Physical harm to a person.

• Damage to property.

#### Undue influence

Undue influence was described as a “domination of the mind.” It commonly arises where there is an existing relationship of trust and confidence and one party unfairly influences the other to enter a contract.

Potential relationships mentioned included:

• Spouses.

• Parents and children.

• Lawyers and clients.

• Doctors and patients.

• Psychologists and patients.

The existence of a close or professional relationship does not automatically establish undue influence. The issue is whether one party unfairly used the relationship to influence the other’s decision to contract.

#### Unconscionability

Unconscionability was described as conduct that is “against conscience.” It may arise where one party takes advantage of another party’s serious disadvantage, such as:

• Poverty.

• Illiteracy.

• Illness.

The lecturer stressed that the legal threshold is high. Unconscionability is successfully argued only rarely. The disadvantage must be significant, and the other party must deliberately take advantage of it.

#### Misrepresentation

Misrepresentation concerns a false statement made by one party before the contract is entered into.

A misrepresentation may provide a legal remedy where:

• One party makes a false statement of fact.

• The statement is made before the contract is formed.

• The statement induces the other party to enter the contract.

The lecture did not develop the different categories or remedies for misrepresentation further.

#### Mistake

Mistake can occur where one or both parties are mistaken about something connected with the contract.

The lecturer described mistake as a difficult area. People frequently enter contracts while mistaken about some matter, so only more extreme situations are likely to create a remedy.

The lecture did not set out the detailed legal categories or tests for mistake.

A student asked whether the Treaty of Waitangi would be an example of a contract.

The lecturer’s answer was that contractual principles could not easily be applied. The Treaty was described as falling within a different area of law, particularly international law, which has its own rules concerning treaties and conventions.

Illegality

Contracts may be void because of illegality. Examples given included:

• Contracts contrary to a statute.

• Contracts to commit a crime.

• Contracts involving fraud.

• Contracts interfering with the administration of justice.

The lecturer used an agreement to rob a bank as an obvious example. If one participant withdraws before the robbery occurs, the other participant cannot sue for the profit that would supposedly have been earned from the illegal enterprise.

The lecturer noted that illegality is not necessarily the same as criminal illegality. An arrangement may be illegal without entering into it necessarily being a criminal offence. The consequences depend on:

• The specific legal rule involved.

• Why the arrangement is illegal.

• What the relevant legislation provides.

• Whether entering into the arrangement is itself an offence.

New Zealand courts may have power to reach a fair outcome even where a contract is void for illegality. This can matter where:

• The parties did not realise the arrangement was illegal.

• One party performed their obligations.

• The other party received a benefit without performing.

• Treating the contract as entirely void would create a loss for one party and a windfall for the other.

The lecturer linked this issue to the large volume of legislation and the possibility that commercial parties may unknowingly enter an unlawful arrangement.

Exclusion and limitation of liability

The lecture then moved from formation to the operation of contracts.

In non-consumer transactions, especially contracts between businesses or between a business and a government body, it is common for a contract to limit or exclude one party’s liability.

An exclusion clause may state that a party is not liable in specified circumstances. A limitation clause may cap liability at a particular amount.

An example was a sale contract stating that the seller is not liable if the product is defective. Such a term can substantially alter the risk allocation that a party might otherwise expect.

In commercial contracting, parties should identify:

• Who is legally responsible if something goes wrong.

• Whether liability is excluded.

• Whether liability is capped.

• The maximum financial exposure.

• Which party bears the relevant risk of loss.

The lecturer stated that contractual exclusions or limitations of liability for breach will usually be effective in non-consumer transactions. Special rules apply to consumer contracts, but those rules were not covered in detail.

A contract may also attempt to exclude liability for misrepresentation. Such a clause may be effective if it is fair and reasonable in the circumstances.

Tauranga City Council and Harrison Grierson example

The lecturer discussed a High Court case identified in the transcript as *Tauranga City Council and Harrison Grierson Holdings*, apparently decided around 2020.

The facts described were:

• Tauranga City Council wanted to build a transport hub and car-park building.

• Harrison Grierson was engaged as an urban-development, engineering, and design consultancy.

• Constructure Auckland was engaged to review the structural design.

• Construction began but stopped because of defective design.

• The Council abandoned construction and sold the land for $1.

• The Council brought claims against the firms involved.

• Harrison Grierson’s contract limited liability to five times its fee, stated in the lecture as approximately $2 million.

• The other firm had a similar but smaller limitation.

• The Council calculated its loss at approximately $26 million.

The issue was whether the Council could recover more than the contractual liability limits, partly in connection with statutory requirements relating to building.

The lecturer stated that the High Court held the limitations effective. The liabilities were therefore limited to approximately:

• $75,000 for one party.

• $2 million for the other party.

The lecturer also stated that the Council later brought an appeal that was apparently not heard and that the parties ultimately reached some form of out-of-court settlement. This later procedural account was expressed with uncertainty in the transcript.

The example demonstrates the practical importance of liability caps in engineering consultancy and construction contracts. A very large actual loss may still produce a much smaller recovery if the contract contains an effective limitation clause.

Contract problem: silence as acceptance

The first short problem involved:

• Kerry emailing Gust Engineering about a 4.5 kW vertical-axis wind turbine advertised for $7,300.

• Kerry stating that if Gust did not respond by a specified deadline, Kerry would consider the parties to have a deal.

• Gust not replying.

• Gust selling the turbine to a third party after the deadline.

The issue was whether a valid contract had been formed through silence.

The lecturer’s conclusion was that silence is generally not valid acceptance. Acceptance must be a final and unqualified expression of assent. Silence does not ordinarily communicate agreement.

Therefore:

• Kerry may have made an offer.

• Gust did not accept the offer merely by remaining silent.

• No contract was formed on those facts.

An offeror cannot unilaterally state that the offeree’s silence will count as acceptance.

Contract problem: promised gift of textbooks

The second problem involved a 23-year-old flatmate who announced while socialising that she was leaving university, had accepted a job at Google, and would leave her textbooks to the other people present. The textbooks were not subsequently left.

The lecturer identified two problems with treating this as an enforceable contract:

1. Lack of consideration.

2. Doubt about an intention to create legal relations.

There was nothing given in exchange for the promised textbooks. The statement therefore appeared to be a gratuitous promise or gift rather than a bargain supported by consideration.

The social setting also created doubt about whether the statement was intended to create legal legal relations. It may have been a casual social statement rather than a serious legal commitment.

The intoxication rule was also discussed in relation to the facts, but the lecturer considered that the evidence did not clearly establish that the speaker lacked understanding of what she was doing. The statement alone did not meet the high standard required for intoxication to affect capacity.

Negligence and tort

Negligence was introduced as a second major topic. It is part of private law, like contract law, and is a species of tort.

Tort means a civil or private wrong. The lecturer stated that tort generally involves an act or omission by which a person, without just cause or excuse, causes harm to another.

Negligence is concerned with liability for negligent acts or omissions. The lecturer said that there are approximately 15 to 17 torts, depending on how they are counted, but negligence is by far the most significant and may be particularly relevant to commercial activity.

In New Zealand, a person generally cannot sue in negligence for personal injury caused by an accident because of the ACC scheme. ACC compensates people for the consequences of accidents and also bars personal-injury proceedings of the kind that might otherwise be brought against the person responsible.

Negligence claims can still concern property damage and other forms of loss. The lecturer used a driving example:

• A driver exceeds the speed limit and is distracted by practising the trumpet.

• The driver crashes into another vehicle.

• The driver may be liable in negligence for damage to the other vehicle.

• The driver may also face criminal consequences for dangerous or careless driving.

• The civil negligence claim concerns compensation for property loss.

• Personal injury consequences are generally dealt with through ACC rather than a negligence lawsuit.

Contract and tort compared

The primary distinction between contract and tort is the source of the duty.

In contract:

• The parties have entered into an agreement.

• The parties’ obligations are fixed by their agreement.

• Liability for breach arises from the contractual relationship.

• A person who is not a party to the contract generally cannot be liable under it.

• A pre-existing agreement is required.

In tort:

• The duty is imposed by law rather than created by agreement.

• The duty may apply to people generally.

• Individuals must take reasonable care not to cause foreseeable harm to others.

• Liability does not depend on a particular contractual relationship.

For example, a driver has not previously promised every other road user not to damage their vehicle. The duty not to cause foreseeable harm nevertheless arises independently under tort law.

Negligence in engineering and commercial activity

An engineering consultant who is negligent in work performed for a client may be liable under the contract with that client. A claim will often be brought in contract because proving contractual liability may be easier and more direct.

Negligence may become more important where the affected person is a third party with no contract.

The lecturer’s engineering example involved:

1. A designer creating an electronic component for an industrial machine.

2. The designer contracting with the machine manufacturer.

3. A negligent design causing the machine to fail or explode.

4. The manufacturer selling the machine to an end user.

5. The end user suffering loss because of the defective design.

6. No direct contract existing between the designer and the end user.

The end user may potentially claim in negligence because the designer’s conduct caused foreseeable loss despite the absence of a contract. However, the lecturer stated that such claims are not particularly common and that the end user would more commonly sue the party from which the machine was purchased under contract.

The ginger-beer case and the neighbour principle

The lecturer identified the foundational negligence case as *Donahue and Stevenson*, described as a House of Lords decision concerning a bottle of ginger beer. The transcript’s case name may be an automated-transcription error.

The facts described were:

• Two friends were at a café.

• One friend purchased a bottle of ginger beer for the other.

• The bottle was opaque.

• After drinking it, the recipient discovered decomposed remains of a snail in the bottle.

• The recipient suffered gastric illness.

• The recipient had no contract with the café because she was not the purchaser.

• She sued the manufacturer of the ginger beer.

The case addressed whether the manufacturer could be liable despite the absence of a contract with the injured consumer. The lecturer stated that it was the first creation or recognition of the tort of negligence in the relevant common-law development.

The principle was expressed as a duty to take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions that can reasonably be foreseen as likely to injure one’s neighbour.

A “neighbour” was described as a person so closely and directly affected by one’s act that the person should be contemplated when considering the relevant acts or omissions.

The basic application is:

• If harm to another person is reasonably foreseeable, reasonable care may be required.

• A road user is a foreseeable person who may be harmed by dangerous driving.

• Vehicle owners in a car park may be foreseeable victims of an improperly played cricket ball.

• End users may be foreseeable victims of negligent engineering design.

Other torts

The lecturer gave several examples of torts besides negligence:

• Trespass: A property-based tort that may result in compensation for loss caused by unauthorised interference. Trespass may also be a criminal offence.

• Nuisance: Unreasonable interference arising from another person’s use of their property, such as conduct affecting an adjoining property.

• Conversion: The tort equivalent of theft, involving improper interference with or taking of another person’s property.

• Invasion of privacy: A relatively recent tort recognised by New Zealand courts, according to the lecture.

• Intimate partner violence: The lecturer stated that the Supreme Court of Canada had recognised this as a tort for the first time in the year of the lecture.

The common theme is the legal response to ways in which people cause harm or interfere with each other’s rights.
