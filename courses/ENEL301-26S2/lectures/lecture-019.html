The iron triangle

The lecture described the iron triangle, also called the golden triangle in some contexts. The commonly discussed constraints are:

• Scope

• Cost

• Time

Quality may also be included, producing four interacting constraints rather than a literal three-sided triangle.

The central principle is that project constraints are interdependent. It is generally not possible to improve or preserve all desired outcomes independently. A change in one constraint usually affects one or more others.

Examples:

• Increasing scope may require more time or cost.

• Improving quality may require additional time or resources.

• Reducing cost may require reducing scope, quality, or available time.

• Holding time and cost fixed may require accepting reduced scope or quality.

The lecture used the analogy that a product cannot simultaneously be maximally cheap, reliable, and fast without trade-offs.

Stakeholder analysis, management, and engagement

#### Stakeholder definition

• Newsletters

Interest-power/influence chart

A common stakeholder-analysis tool is an interest-power or interest-influence chart.

• Horizontal axis: stakeholder interest in the project

• Vertical axis: stakeholder power or influence over the project

The chart helps prioritise engagement.

Typical quadrants are interpreted as follows:

• High interest, high power/influence:

• Engage closely

• Maintain regular, meaningful involvement

• Low interest, low power/influence:

• Keep informed as appropriate

• Monitor with relatively limited effort

• Other combinations:

• Require strategies matched to the stakeholder’s power and interest

The position of a stakeholder can change during the project. A group that has little interest during construction may become highly interested when the project affects its user experience. Similarly, a stakeholder’s influence may increase at a particular approval, construction, operational, or end-of-life stage.

The lecturer used cloud-storage infrastructure as an example. End users may have little interest in how the system is physically constructed but may have significant interest in the quality of their user experience.

Work breakdown structures

Project planning begins by identifying the work required to deliver the project.

The lecturer described this as repeatedly breaking down the project into manageable tasks that one person or one thing can perform.

A work breakdown structure (WBS) is a decomposition of the work required to deliver a project.

A WBS:

• Starts from the project objective

• Identifies major deliverables or milestones

• Breaks deliverables into smaller components

• Continues until the tasks are sufficiently discrete and manageable

• Produces a list or hierarchical structure of required work

At the initial WBS stage, the focus is on what must be done. It does not initially specify:

• When the task will occur

• How long it will take

• Who will perform it

• How much it will cost

• The relationships between tasks

Those details are added during later planning and scheduling.

The lecturer also stressed that anything consuming time or money should be included. This can include:

• Technical work

• Administration

• Meetings

• Stakeholder management

• Procurement

• Ordering parts

• Other supporting activities

Flat-tyre example

The lecturer used changing a flat tyre to demonstrate hierarchical decomposition.

At a high level, the task could be represented as:

• 1.1 Stop the car

• 1.2 Change the tyre

• 1.3 Drive away

The “change the tyre” activity can then be decomposed into:

• Get tools and spare parts

• Lift the car with a jack

• Remove the flat tyre

• Replace it with the spare

• Lower the car

• Put the tools away

The first activity can be decomposed further:

• Open the boot

• Remove the jack

• Remove the jack handle or driver

• Remove the nut wrench

• Remove the spare tyre

The purpose is not to assign times or sequence the activities at this stage. It is to ensure that the required work has been identified and broken into actionable units.

Task sequencing and dependencies

Once the WBS and subtasks have been identified, the tasks can be sequenced.

The lecture introduced four dependency types.

#### Finish-to-start (FS)

Task B cannot start until Task A has finished.

This is the most common dependency type. It represents tasks that occur in series.

Example:

• A tyre cannot be replaced until the old tyre has been removed.

• A report cannot be submitted until it has been completed and edited.

#### Start-to-start (SS)

Task B cannot start until Task A has started.

The tasks may then proceed in parallel.

Example:

• A kettle can be turned on at the same time as a toaster is started when preparing breakfast.

The important relationship is between the starts of the two activities, not necessarily their completion times.

#### Finish-to-finish (FF)

Task B cannot finish until Task A has finished.

This dependency is useful when multiple activities must be completed by a coordinated point.

Examples:

• Different components of a meal may need to finish at approximately the same time so they can be served together.

• Design work may not be able to finish until an environmental impact assessment has been completed.

#### Start-to-finish (SF)

Task B cannot finish until Task A has started.

This is described as the least common dependency type.

Example:

• A legacy software system may not be shut down until the replacement system has started and is operating.

The four dependency abbreviations are therefore:

• FS: Finish-to-start

• SS: Start-to-start

• FF: Finish-to-finish

• SF: Start-to-finish

Estimating activity durations

After sequencing, the project team estimates how long each discrete activity will take.

Duration estimates may be based on:

• Engineering judgement

• Previous projects

• The resources available

• The complexity of the activity

• Procurement and delivery lead times

• Uncertainty

• Known constraints

• Potential delays

Some project-management software allows uncertainty factors to be applied and can run Monte Carlo simulations to estimate the likely duration of a larger project. However, the lecturer stated that many practical estimates use a single number based on judgement and past experience.

Duration estimation should consider all resources required to complete the task. Thinking through resources may reveal missing activities, requiring the team to return to the WBS and add them.

Examples of additional activities include:

• Ordering parts

• Waiting for parts to arrive

• Completing approvals

• Coordinating subcontractors

• Performing administrative work

• Allowing time for stakeholder meetings

Procurement lead times can significantly affect the project. Parts may need to be ordered early, especially if delivery or customs creates uncertainty.

Contingency and assumptions

The lecturer recommended including contingency in project plans to account for possible delays.

Potential sources of delay include:

• Customs delays

• Late delivery of parts

• Illness

• A team member failing to contribute as expected

• Subcontractor work taking longer than planned

• Software development overruns

A simple example involved a three-person assignment expected to take approximately 12 days, with an additional four-hour buffer for the possibility that someone becomes sick or does not contribute fully.

In another practical example, the lecturer described using a spreadsheet to record hours spent on consultancy work, remaining work, and a 10% buffer. Logging actual hours helps compare progress with the estimate and determine whether the project remains on track.

All constraints and assumptions should be documented. This allows later users of the plan to understand the basis of the estimates and distinguish known facts from planning assumptions.

Gantt charts

Planning, sequencing, and duration estimation form the basis of a Gantt chart.

A Gantt chart displays activities against time and can show:

• Task start and finish times

• Duration

• Task overlap

• Sequence

• Progress

• Potential scheduling conflicts

The lecturer did not teach the construction of Gantt charts in detail. Excel can be used for a basic chart, while project-management software can automatically handle task relationships and sequencing.

Project monitoring and control

The lecture returned to the main project constraints:

• Scope

• Quality

• Time

• Cost

These are monitored throughout the project. Other factors may also be monitored depending on the project-management framework.

A project monitoring and control plan establishes what happens when project conditions change.

The general process described was:

1. Start the project.

2. Monitor actual progress against the plan.

3. Identify a deviation, such as a delay or cost overrun.

4. Identify the consequences.

5. Assess the risk and effect on scope, time, cost, and quality.

6. Decide whether the deviation is acceptable.

7. If necessary, initiate a formal project change.

8. Obtain the required approval.

9. Communicate and implement the change.

10. Continue monitoring.

A minor deviation may be accepted if its risk and consequences are sufficiently low. A significant deviation may require a formal revision to the project.

For example, if a project is running over time and cost but quality is acceptable, the team may need to negotiate a reduction in scope to meet the remaining time and cost constraints.

If changes become sufficiently serious, the client may need to approve a revised project. In extreme cases, the contract could be terminated.

Change registers and authority

Project changes are normally recorded in a change register.

Change requests may be escalated through different levels of organisational authority. The required authority depends on factors such as:

• Financial value

• Risk

• Scope impact

• Time impact

• Organisational significance

The lecturer illustrated this with a hypothetical university building project:

• A $10,000 cost overrun might be approved by a lower-level manager.

• A $1 million cost overrun would likely require approval from a much more senior university authority.

The general principle is that people have delegated authority limits. If a change exceeds a person’s approval threshold, another person’s authorisation is required.

The lecturer also gave a personal example in which a client could not approve a single $30,000 proposal but could approve $10,000. The lecturer responded by proposing three separate $10,000 stages. This example was presented as an anecdote about delegated financial authority, not as a general recommendation for bypassing approval controls. In practice, splitting work to avoid an approval threshold could raise governance or procurement concerns and would need to comply with the organisation’s rules.
