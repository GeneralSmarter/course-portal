Key concepts

• Life cycle inventory: Compilation of the raw-material inputs and environmental emissions associated with delivering a functional unit.

• Impact assessment: Conversion of inventory flows into potential environmental impacts such as climate change, smog, and ecotoxicity.

• Characterisation factor: A factor representing the relative contribution or potency of an emission for a particular environmental impact category.

• Characterised result: The sum of inventory flows multiplied by their relevant characterisation factors.

• Interpretation: Examination of why the results occur, including the influence of assumptions, data, functional unit, system boundary, and impact-assessment model.

• Five whys: Repeatedly asking why a result occurs to develop a deeper understanding of its causes and identify flawed assumptions or data.

• Sensitivity analysis: Testing how results change when important assumptions or parameters are varied.

• Uncertainty analysis: Quantifying the effect of uncertain input data on LCA results.

• Monte Carlo simulation: Repeatedly varying uncertain inputs within specified bounds and recalculating the model to obtain a distribution or confidence interval for the result.

• Burden shifting: Reducing an impact in one area while increasing an impact elsewhere.

• Streamlined LCA: A rapid, approximate LCA using simplified data and calculations, often without compliance with a formal standard.

• Full LCA: A more comprehensive study using detailed inventories, software, databases, uncertainty analysis, and typically relevant ISO standards.

• Attributional LCA: Describes environmental impacts associated with an existing product or system, generally assuming that future emissions resemble historical emissions.

• Consequential LCA: Estimates the consequences of changes in demand, including displacement effects and changes in marginal supply.

• Marginal supply: The production or supply that responds to an incremental change in demand.

• Average supply: The average environmental intensity of the existing supply mix.

• Input-output LCA: Uses economic-sector data and flows of money through the economy to estimate environmental impacts.

• Hybrid LCA: Combines process-based LCA with input-output LCA.

• Recycling allocation: The method used to assign environmental burdens and benefits between products and material life cycles when recycling occurs.

Detailed notes

Completing the LCA process

The lecturer referred to the four-step LCA process discussed previously:

1. Goal and scope definition.

2. Inventory development.

3. Impact assessment.

4. Interpretation.

Inventory development involves compiling data and constructing a model. The model produces a list of environmental flows associated with delivering the functional unit. These flows may include:

• Raw materials extracted from the environment.

• Energy and other inputs.

• Emissions released to the environment.

Two systems may deliver the same functional unit but have different environmental flows. Impact assessment is used to translate those flows into potential environmental impacts.

Examples of impact categories mentioned include:

• Climate change.

• Smog.

• Ecotoxicity.

• Resource extraction.

Characterisation factors

Different substances contribute differently to the same impact category. For greenhouse gases, each gas has a different characterisation or potency factor.

Examples given in the lecture included:

• Carbon dioxide: factor of 1.

• Methane: approximately 21 in one model, or up to approximately 28 depending on the model.

• Nitrous oxide: higher than methane.

• Hydrofluorocarbons: factors in the thousands.

For a single impact category, inventory flows are multiplied by their characterisation factors and summed. This converts different emissions into a common reference unit, such as carbon-dioxide equivalents.

LCA software can perform many of these calculations simultaneously using matrix algebra. The inventory can be multiplied by characterisation factors for multiple environmental indicators at once.

Interpretation of results

A lower numerical result does not automatically mean that one system is universally better. The preferred system can depend on:

• The goal and scope.

• The functional unit.

• System boundaries.

• Assumptions.

• Inventory data.

• Data quality.

• The impact-assessment model.

• The treatment of recycling.

• The environmental indicators selected.

Interpretation should therefore focus on why the results have their particular values, rather than simply reporting which option has the lowest result.

The arrows in the four-step LCA process can point backwards because interpretation may reveal that earlier stages need to be revisited. For example:

• An assumption may be inappropriate.

• Inventory data may be weak.

• The functional unit may not represent the intended comparison.

• A system boundary may omit an important process.

• The selected impact-assessment model may affect the conclusion.

A result may need to be checked against alternative models or recalculated after improving the assumptions and data.

The five whys

The lecturer recommended applying the five whys to LCA interpretation:

1. Identify a result that needs explanation.

2. Ask why the result has that value.

3. Examine the explanation and ask why again.

4. Continue repeatedly.

5. Use the resulting chain of reasoning to identify the underlying driver.

This can reveal that a result depends on:

• A questionable assumption.

• Estimated rather than measured data.

• A poorly selected database value.

• A particular life-cycle stage.

• A modelling choice rather than a physical difference.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis examines how the results change when key assumptions are varied.

For example, in an LCA of a laptop, electricity consumption during the use phase may be important. The model could be run with:

• Lower-than-baseline electricity use.

• Baseline electricity use.

• Higher-than-baseline electricity use.

The range of results gives an indication of the envelope of plausible outcomes. Sensitivity analysis is especially important when a result depends heavily on a parameter that is uncertain or difficult to measure.

Data uncertainty and Monte Carlo simulation

There is no perfect dataset for LCA. Input data may have uncertainty because values are:

• Estimated.

• Taken from generic databases.

• Based on averages.

• Measured with limited precision.

• Dependent on geographic or technological conditions.

In a full LCA, the effect of data uncertainty can be assessed using Monte Carlo simulation.

The process described was:

1. Assign uncertainty bounds to model inputs.

2. Randomly vary the input values within those bounds.

3. Run the LCA calculation.

4. Record the result.

5. Repeat the process thousands of times.

6. Analyse the resulting distribution of outcomes.

The output can provide an interval around results such as a greenhouse-gas footprint. The lecturer described this as effectively producing error bars or a confidence interval.

The lecturer stated that this type of uncertainty analysis is generally not available in streamlined LCA, but can be used in full LCA.

Communicating results and limitations

LCA results should be clearly communicated together with:

• The main result.

• The important assumptions.

• The dominant contributors.

• Key uncertainties.

• Limitations.

• The interpretation of trade-offs.

• Any conditions under which the conclusion changes.

A potentially important input should not be presented as precise if it was only guessed or estimated. For example, electricity use in the use phase may be a major contributor but may not have been directly measured.

Burden shifting

Burden shifting occurs when reducing one environmental burden causes another burden to increase. The lecturer compared this to “whack-a-mole”: reducing one impact can cause another impact to appear elsewhere.

Burden shifting may occur:

• Between environmental impact categories.

• Between life-cycle stages.

• Between different products or systems.

• Between regions or economic sectors.

Attributional LCA

Attributional LCA is the most common type of LCA according to the lecture.

It generally assumes that:

• Future emissions are represented by historical or average emissions.

• An increase in demand produces a proportional increase in emissions.

• Existing average production conditions are suitable proxies for the system being studied.

The slope of an emissions-versus-production relationship represents an emission factor, such as tonnes of CO2 equivalent per tonne of product.

Attributional LCA is relatively straightforward to explain and calculate, but it may not represent the actual consequences of a change in demand.

Consequential LCA

Consequential LCA attempts to model the consequences arising from a change in demand. It considers:

• Changes in demand.

• Displacement effects.

• Marginal production.

• Economic ripple effects.

• Changes in the products or services purchased as a result of the original decision.

Consequential LCA is not related to consequentialist ethics; the term refers to modelling consequences in the economy.

#### Supply-constrained markets

In a supply-constrained market:

1. Increased demand can increase price.

2. The higher price may cause some consumers to choose a cheaper alternative.

3. The environmental impact of that alternative may need to be included.

4. The spending saved by the consumer may be redirected to other goods or services.

5. Those additional purchases can create further environmental impacts.

#### Beef, chicken, and beer example

The lecture used a simplified example:

• Beef was described as relatively greenhouse-gas intensive.

• If beef becomes expensive, a consumer may switch to chicken.

• Chicken was described as having a lower CO2-equivalent impact than beef.

• The money saved may be spent on additional chicken or on another product such as beer.

• A consequential model may therefore include the impacts of the changed purchasing pattern rather than simply assigning the average impact of beef.

The example was illustrative rather than a complete quantitative model.

#### Marginal versus average supply

The environmental impact of marginal supply can be either lower or higher than the average supply.

Examples discussed:

• Video conferencing may replace travel to face-to-face conferences. The marginal supply associated with video conferencing may have a lower impact than the displaced travel.

• In New Zealand, normal electricity generation relies significantly on hydroelectricity, giving a relatively low average impact. At high demand, gas-fired peaking generation may be used, meaning marginal electricity can have a higher impact than average electricity.

• In Australia, the average electricity impact was described as relatively high because of brown and black coal. The marginal supply was described as potentially better than the average, depending on the generation mix.

These relationships depend on the country, time, operating conditions, and supply system.

#### Limitations of consequential LCA

Consequential LCA is difficult because it requires modelling:

• Economic responses.

• Substitution.

• Marginal suppliers.

• Displacement.

• Historical economic relationships.

• Ripple effects across sectors.

The lecturer stated that consequential models still use historical data to forecast future changes, although they use that data differently from attributional models.

Consequential LCA may be useful for policy questions, but its complexity can make it difficult to explain to decision-makers and the public. The lecturer noted that a model that cannot be clearly explained may not be trusted, even if its underlying calculations are technically defensible.

Recycling allocation

The lecturer stated that recycling remains an unresolved issue in LCA. Different recycling allocation methods can produce substantially different results.

The lecture presented four broad approaches but stated that students were not required to memorise the individual processes. The important point is that the chosen method can strongly affect the outcome and must be applied consistently.

#### Recycling credit and displacement

One approach gives a recycling credit because recycled material is assumed to displace virgin material production.

This can produce negative emission factors for recycling. A negative factor does not mean that recycling physically removes emissions from the atmosphere. It is an accounting consequence of assigning a credit for avoided virgin-material production.

#### Multiple life cycles and cascaded recycling

A material may be recycled through several successive product life cycles. Some materials degrade during recycling, including paper and some plastics.

One approach distributes impacts across the successive life cycles and may use average values across those cycles. This is sometimes described as cascaded recycling.

#### Economic cut-off

The economic cut-off approach attempts to identify the point where a material changes from waste to a valuable input.

Impacts before the cut-off may be assigned to the first product, while impacts after the cut-off may be assigned to the second product.

The difficulty is that the point at which material becomes economically valuable is difficult to define consistently.

#### Recycled content and end-of-life cut-off

Another approach considers the mixture of virgin and recycled material entering a product at the beginning of its life, while cutting off recycling activity at the end of life.

The exact accounting method should be stated clearly because selecting different methods can change the apparent performance of materials such as glass, plastics, or metals.

Sensitivity analysis can be used to test the effect of different recycling approaches.

Input-output LCA

Input-output LCA divides the economy into sectors and uses economic accounts to trace monetary flows between those sectors.

The approach can estimate environmental impacts associated with spending a certain amount of money. For example, it could estimate the environmental impacts associated with spending $10 on bread.

Input-output LCA can be useful for:

• Estimating economy-wide impacts.

• Capturing indirect effects.

• Filling gaps where process-level emission factors are unavailable.

• Estimating impacts associated with services or expenditure.

The resulting factors are often dollar-based emission factors.

#### Limitations of input-output LCA

Input-output LCA has several limitations:

• It is difficult to explain clearly.

• It is not fully traceable to individual physical processes.

• The system boundary may be limited to the national economy represented by the accounts.

• The sector resolution may be poor.

• Different products may be grouped into the same sector.

• Economic averages may not represent the actual product or process being studied.

• Monetary price may be confused with physical production.

The lecturer gave examples of potential artefacts:

• An input-output model for beef may show glass inputs, even though cows do not eat glass. This can result from monetary relationships within national accounts.

• A sector may group different fruit products together, causing bananas, kiwifruit, citrus, or other products to receive the same factor even when their actual production systems differ.

• If the model assumes an average beef price of $30 per kilogram but the actual product costs $50 per kilogram, the calculated impact may change because of the price relationship rather than a real change in physical production.

Input-output LCA therefore has a useful role but must be treated carefully.

Hybrid LCA

Hybrid LCA combines:

• Process-based LCA for processes that can be modelled in detail.

• Input-output LCA for indirect processes or areas where process data are missing.

It can help fill data gaps. The lecturer gave the example of estimating the environmental impact associated with an engineer travelling to or working at a site, where some impacts may be modelled through direct process data and others through economic expenditure factors.

Equations and definitions

Characterised impact result

For an impact category:

\[

I = \sum_i E_i \times CF_i

\]

where:

• \(I\) is the characterised impact result.

• \(E_i\) is the inventory flow of substance or activity \(i\).

• \(CF_i\) is the characterisation factor for substance or activity \(i\).

For greenhouse-gas emissions, the result may be expressed in kg CO2 equivalent.

Streamlined emission calculation

\[

E = \sum_i A_i \times F_i

\]

where:

• \(E\) is the total estimated emission or impact.

• \(A_i\) is the activity data or quantity of input \(i\).

• \(F_i\) is the emission factor for input \(i\).

Examples of activity data include:

• Kilowatt-hours of electricity.

• Kilograms of material.

• Number of batteries.

• Dollars spent in an input-output model.

Carbon-dioxide equivalent

Different greenhouse gases are converted into a common unit using characterisation factors. Carbon dioxide has a reference factor of 1. Other gases have larger factors according to the selected model.

The transcript gave approximate examples:

• CO2: 1.

• Methane: 21 or up to 28 depending on the model.

• SF6: approximately 6,500.

These factors must be treated as model-dependent rather than universal constants.

Emission factor

An emission factor relates an activity or quantity of product to an environmental impact.

Examples include:

• kg CO2 equivalent per kWh of electricity.

• kg CO2 equivalent per kg of material.

• tonnes CO2 equivalent per tonne of product.

• CO2-equivalent emissions per dollar spent.

Attributional relationship

The lecture described an attributional relationship as approximately proportional:

\[

\Delta E \propto \Delta D

\]

where:

• \(\Delta E\) is the change in emissions.

• \(\Delta D\) is the change in demand.

This represents the assumption that increased demand causes a proportional increase in emissions based on existing average conditions.

Monte Carlo simulation

A Monte Carlo analysis repeatedly evaluates:

\[

I^{(k)} = f\left(x_1^{(k)}, x_2^{(k)}, \ldots, x_n^{(k)}\right)

\]

where:

• \(I^{(k)}\) is the result from simulation run \(k\).

• \(x_i^{(k)}\) is a randomly sampled value for uncertain input \(i\).

• \(f\) is the LCA calculation model.

The collection of results \(I^{(1)}, I^{(2)}, \ldots, I^{(N)}\) gives a distribution showing the effect of input uncertainty.

Burden shifting

Burden shifting is not a single equation. It occurs when a change produces:

\[

\Delta I_{\text{reduced}} < 0

\]

for one impact category or life-cycle stage, while producing:

\[

\Delta I_{\text{increased}} > 0

\]

for another category or stage.

Important definitions

• Functional unit: The quantified reference service or function delivered by the system being assessed.

• Inventory flow: An input taken from, or emission released to, the environment.

• Impact category: The environmental problem being assessed, such as climate change or ecotoxicity.

• Characterisation: Conversion of inventory flows into a common impact-category metric.

• System boundary: The defined set of processes included in the assessment.

• Marginal supply: The supply that changes in response to a small change in demand.

• Average supply: The average supply mix over the selected system and period.

• Data resolution: The level of detail with which materials, products, processes, sectors, or locations are represented.

• Potential environmental impact: A modelled estimate of possible environmental effects, not a direct measurement of actual effects.
