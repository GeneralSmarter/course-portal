## Main concepts

- Engineering practice extends beyond technical correctness. Engineers must consider project viability, risk, stakeholders, collaboration, law, ethics, sustainability, and financial and social consequences.
- Legal compliance is a minimum boundary, not necessarily a complete ethical justification.
- Ethical decisions can be examined through:
  - Deontological reasoning: duties, rules, laws, and professional codes.
  - Consequentialist reasoning: likely outcomes and overall benefits and harms.
  - Virtue ethics: the conduct expected from an honest, courageous, compassionate, and responsible engineer.
- Therac-25 demonstrates that software, interfaces, testing, and safety controls can create direct physical consequences.
- Boeing 737 MAX illustrates how technical failures can interact with organisational culture and commercial pressure.
- Engineering is a socio-technical discipline. Technical outcomes depend on communication, relationships, trust, culture, and processes.
- Effective collaboration requires:
  - A clearly shared objective.
  - Purposeful use of differences in knowledge, experience, culture, and ways of thinking.
  - Technical and social interaction.
  - Trust and rapport.
  - Explicit processes for communication, accountability, conflict management, and quality assurance.
- Explicit knowledge is formally recorded or taught. Tacit knowledge develops through experience, observation, relationships, and social interaction.
- The task–relationship–process triangle diagnoses collaboration through:
  - Task: what must be achieved.
  - Relationship: whether people can trust and work with one another.
  - Process: how work, communication, checking, and conflict resolution occur.
- The input–process–output model links team composition and context to collaboration processes and resulting performance or behavioural outcomes.
- Engineering work should be judged by outcomes and benefits, not only by completed outputs or deliverables.
- Requirement clarification is shared by clients and engineers. Engineers must ask questions when requirements are unclear.
- Guru bias is the tendency to treat an experienced or recognised expert as automatically correct.
- Professional codes may involve complaints, appeals, reprimands, financial penalties, suspension, expulsion, or separate legal action. Suspected misconduct should be handled through evidence-based due process.
- Trust, cultural understanding, and stakeholder relationships contribute to social licence to operate.

## Equations and worked patterns

- No mathematical equations were presented in either summary.
- Ethical decision pattern:
  1. Identify affected stakeholders.
  2. Check duties, laws, and professional codes.
  3. Compare likely benefits and harms.
  4. Consider whether the decision reflects appropriate professional virtues.
  5. Justify the decision and acknowledge conflicts between frameworks.
- Engineering failure analysis pattern:
  1. Identify the technical failure modes.
  2. Identify organisational and commercial contributors.
  3. Identify affected stakeholders.
  4. Identify neglected professional duties.
  5. Propose technical, process, and organisational controls.
- Collaboration diagnosis pattern:
  1. Define the intended task or outcome.
  2. Examine trust and working relationships.
  3. Examine communication, quality assurance, accountability, and conflict processes.
  4. Separate controllable conditions from conditions that can only be influenced or accepted.
- Team-startup pattern:
  - Define the shared purpose.
  - Agree on communication methods.
  - Assign responsibilities and deadlines.
  - Define success and required work standards.
  - Establish accountability, escalation, conflict, and review procedures.
  - Record the agreements in a team charter.
- Output versus outcome:
  - Output: the produced deliverable.
  - Outcome: the benefit, change, or value created by the engineering work.
