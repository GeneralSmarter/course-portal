## Main concepts

- Professional engineering requires more than technical correctness. Engineers must consider project viability, financial exposure, risk, stakeholders, teamwork, and social and environmental consequences.
- Legal compliance is a minimum boundary, not a complete ethical justification.
- Three ethical approaches were introduced:
  1. Deontological reasoning examines duties, laws, rules, and professional codes.
  2. Consequentialist or utilitarian reasoning compares likely outcomes and overall benefit, including harms imposed on minorities.
  3. Virtue ethics asks what an honest, courageous, compassionate, and professionally responsible engineer would do.
- A defensible ethical decision should identify affected parties, examine duties and codes, compare consequences, and assess the professional values expressed by the decision.
- Therac-25 illustrates that software behaviour, interfaces, testing, and safety controls can directly cause physical harm.
- The Boeing 737 MAX case illustrates how technical failures can interact with organisational culture and commercial pressure.
- Collaboration, communication, organisational culture, and stakeholder coordination can determine whether technically sound work succeeds.

- No equations were presented in the available Lecture 1 summary.
- Ethical case-analysis pattern:
  1. Identify affected stakeholders.
  2. Identify applicable duties, laws, codes, and professional responsibilities.
  3. Compare likely benefits and harms.
  4. Consider what relevant professional virtues require.
  5. Identify technical and organisational contributors.
  6. Justify the final decision while acknowledging conflicts or trade-offs.
- Failure-case pattern:
  - Determine the technical failure mode.
  - Examine testing, interfaces, and safety controls.
  - Identify organisational and commercial pressures.
  - State who was harmed or placed at risk.
  - Identify neglected professional duties.
