02 · BUILD THE MODEL

Main concepts

  • Professional engineering extends beyond technical correctness. It includes project viability, risk management, stakeholder coordination, communication, sustainability, law, intellectual property, and ethical responsibility. (Lecture 1)
  • Legal compliance is a minimum boundary, not a complete ethical justification. Ethical analysis can use:
  • Deontological reasoning: duties, rules, laws, and professional codes.
  • Consequentialist or utilitarian reasoning: likely outcomes and overall benefit, while considering harm to minorities.
  • Virtue ethics: what an honest, courageous, compassionate, and responsible engineer would do. (Lecture 1)
  • Therac-25 demonstrates how software, interfaces, testing, and safety controls can produce direct physical harm. The Boeing 737 MAX cases demonstrate how technical failures interact with organisational culture and commercial pressure. (Lecture 1)
  • Engineering is a socio-technical discipline. Technical outcomes depend on communication, relationships, culture, trust, and working processes as well as technical knowledge. (Lecture 2)
  • Effective collaboration requires a shared objective, deliberate use of differences, purposeful technical and social interaction, trust, and explicit processes for communication, accountability, conflict, and quality assurance. (Lecture 2)
  • Explicit knowledge is written, recorded, formally taught, or directly communicated. Tacit knowledge develops through experience, observation, relationships, and social interaction. Effective engineers need both. (Lecture 2)
  • A team charter should establish purpose, communication methods, responsibilities, accountability, conflict procedures, success criteria, task allocation, timing, and expected work standards. (Lecture 2)
  • The task–relationship–process triangle diagnoses collaboration:
  • Task: what must be achieved.
  • Relationship: whether people can trust and work with one another.
  • Process: how communication, checking, coordination, and conflict resolution occur. (Lecture 2)
  • Engineering projects should be justified by outcomes, meaning the benefit or value created, rather than only by outputs such as reports, designs, or completed systems. (Lecture 2)
  • Professional codes may involve complaints, appeals, reprimands, financial penalties, suspension, expulsion, or separate legal proceedings. Suspected misconduct should be handled through evidence-based due process, including an opportunity to respond. (Lecture 2)
  • Trust, cultural understanding, and stakeholder relationships contribute to an organisation’s social licence to operate. (Lecture 2)

03 · WORK THE MECHANICS

Equations and worked patterns

  • No mathematical equations were presented in either source summary.
  • Ethical decision pattern:
  1. Identify affected stakeholders.
  2. Test the decision against duties, laws, and professional codes.
  3. Compare likely benefits and harms.
  4. Consider whether the decision reflects appropriate professional virtues.
  5. Explain where the ethical frameworks agree or conflict. (Lecture 1)
  • Engineering-failure analysis pattern:
  1. Identify the technical failure mode.
  2. Identify organisational and commercial contributors.
  3. Identify affected stakeholders.
  4. Identify neglected professional duties.
  5. Propose technical, process, and communication controls. (Lecture 1)
  • Collaboration diagnosis pattern:
  1. Define the intended task and outcome.
  2. Check whether relationships provide sufficient trust and openness.
  3. Check whether communication, accountability, review, and conflict processes are explicit.
  4. Separate conditions the team can control, influence, or not realistically change. (Lecture 2)
  • Input–process–output pattern:
  • Inputs: team composition, experience, task design, and context.
  • Processes: communication, conflict management, procedures, and agreed ways of working.
  • Outputs and outcomes: performance, behavioural consequences, and the value created. (Lecture 2)
  • Quality-assurance pattern: use internal review to detect errors before work is passed to the next person or released, protecting both technical performance and working relationships. (Lecture 2)
