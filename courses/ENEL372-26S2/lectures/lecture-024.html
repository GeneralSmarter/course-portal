Detailed notes

LCR rectifier loads

The lecture begins with a full-bridge rectifier supplying an LCR load. The load contains:

• A resistor.

• An inductor.

• A capacitor.

A capacitor is common in practical rectifiers, including the input stages of computer and laptop power supplies. A physical inductor may not be present in the same form, but an inductor and resistor can sometimes be used as an approximate model of complex filtering or load dynamics.

The lecturer emphasises that simulation models can lump complicated real-world behaviour into simpler equivalent circuits. This makes an LCR model useful even when the actual power supply contains switching devices, control circuitry, MOSFETs, parasitic elements, and other filtering techniques.

Capacitive and inductive smoothing

Capacitive smoothing is normally discussed as smoothing the voltage. A capacitor resists rapid changes in voltage, so it reduces voltage ripple.

Inductive smoothing is normally discussed as smoothing the current. An inductor resists rapid changes in current, so it reduces current ripple.

The lecturer warns that statements such as “the capacitor is infinite” or “the inductor is infinite” are mathematical assumptions. No real capacitor has infinite capacitance and no real inductor has infinite inductance. These assumptions are used because they make the circuit equations much easier to solve and may be approximately valid under suitable operating conditions.

A large inductance does not automatically guarantee continuous conduction. The required inductance also depends on the load. For example, the lecture compares a 200 Ω load with a much lower-resistance solar-car motor load of approximately 3 Ω. A large resistance changes the current and therefore changes the inductance required to keep the current above zero.

Continuous and discontinuous conduction

In continuous conduction, the inductor current remains above zero throughout the relevant cycle.

In discontinuous conduction, the inductor current falls to zero for part of the cycle. The lecturer identifies discontinuous conduction as undesirable in the examples under discussion because it can produce:

• Abrupt changes in voltage.

• Transients.

• Ringing.

• Additional sensitivity to parasitic capacitance and inductance.

• Less accurate simple analytical models.

The lecture’s simulation example uses a relatively large resistance and a large inductor, yet the current still reaches zero. This demonstrates that “inductively smooth” is an approximate, application-dependent concept rather than a universal property.

Increasing the inductance reduces the jump associated with discontinuous conduction. Once the circuit reaches continuous conduction, the jump disappears. Near the boundary between discontinuous and continuous conduction, the jump may still exist but becomes small.

Practical and simulation non-idealities

An ideal inductor-only model may produce an abrupt voltage transition when the current reaches zero. In a real circuit, parasitic capacitances and inductances would affect the transient response and could produce ringing rather than the idealised waveform.

To model a real system more accurately, additional parasitic inductances and capacitances may need to be included.

Harmonics and frequency doubling

A single-phase full-wave rectifier flips the negative half-cycle of the AC waveform into the positive direction. The resulting rectified waveform has two pulses per original AC period.

Therefore, the lowest non-DC ripple frequency is twice the source frequency:

• 50 Hz source → 100 Hz second harmonic.

• 60 Hz source → 120 Hz second harmonic.

• 400 Hz source → 800 Hz second harmonic.

For the DC-side voltage of a single-phase full-wave rectifier, the second harmonic commonly dominates the ripple analysis.

This should be kept separate from the AC-side current waveform. A square-like AC current waveform is represented by odd harmonics, such as the third, fifth, seventh, and ninth. The AC-side harmonic structure is therefore not necessarily dominated by the second harmonic.
