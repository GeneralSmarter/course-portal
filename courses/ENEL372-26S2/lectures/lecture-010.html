Overview

Lecture 10 introduces practical circuit-layout considerations, primarily for printed circuit boards (PCBs). The central concern is controlling electrical noise caused by parasitic resistance, capacitance, and inductance arising from the physical arrangement of components and traces.

The lecture focuses on:

• PCB traces, component placement, and layout strategy.

• Noise generation, coupling, and transmission.

• Ground and power trace design.

• Ground and power planes.

• Minimising loop area and stray inductance.

• Layout considerations for high-speed, digital, analogue, mixed-signal, and power-electronic circuits.

• Separation of functional circuit groups.

• Decoupling capacitors and their frequency-dependent behaviour.

The lecture emphasises practical rules of thumb rather than detailed electromagnetic analysis. A rigorous analysis of PCB layout would require solving Maxwell’s equations for the relevant PCB geometries and component structures.

• A PCB consists broadly of conducting traces that electrically interconnect carefully positioned components.

• “Trace” is the industry-standard term used in the transcript; “track” is also commonly used in New Zealand.

• Correct electrical connections alone do not guarantee a good physical layout.

• Layout affects:

• Noise generated by a circuit.

• The circuit’s susceptibility to external noise.

• Parasitic or stray resistance, capacitance, and inductance.

• The required level of layout care depends on:

• Operating frequency.

• Whether the circuit is digital, analogue, mixed-signal, or power electronic.

• Component count.

• Budget, technology, and operational constraints.

• Ground and power traces are usually the most important traces to design correctly because they connect to many components and can distribute noise widely.

• Traces should generally be short. High-current traces should also be wide.

• Large loops increase stray inductance and improve the ability of a circuit to receive or radiate electromagnetic noise.

• Ground and power traces should be routed close together over long distances where practical. This increases their parasitic capacitance, which provides a low-impedance path for high-frequency noise between them.

• General signal traces should not be routed together in parallel for long distances if they carry different signals, because parasitic capacitance can cause capacitive coupling and crosstalk.

Detailed notes

1. Circuit layout and PCB construction

Circuit layout is the physical placement of circuit components and the electrical connections between them.

A PCB is described broadly as:

• A collection of conductive traces.

• Interconnections between positioned components.

• A physical implementation of a circuit originally designed schematically or on paper.

There are many possible physical arrangements that can provide the same electrical connections. However, these arrangements do not necessarily perform equally well. The physical geometry introduces parasitic effects that are not represented fully in an ideal schematic.

The lecture concentrates mainly on PCBs because they are the form of circuit construction engineers are likely to encounter professionally. Vero board and breadboard construction are treated as less representative of professional circuit production, although the same general layout principles can apply to project prototypes.

2. Why layout matters

Layout affects both the creation and susceptibility of a circuit to electrical noise.

A poor layout can:

• Create unwanted electromagnetic fields.

• Allow noise to couple between nearby traces.

• Increase current-loop area.

• Increase parasitic inductance.

• Increase parasitic capacitance between conductors.

• Create high-impedance current paths.

• Distribute noise through shared ground or power conductors.

• Produce voltage spikes when current changes rapidly.

A good layout attempts to minimise unwanted parasitic effects while preserving the required electrical connections.

Detailed electromagnetic analysis is possible, but it would require analysis of the PCB geometry, component geometry, and relevant electromagnetic fields. The lecturer states that the practical approach in normal design is generally to use established layout practices and rules of thumb rather than solving the full field equations.

3. Circuit type determines layout priorities

Different circuit categories require different levels and types of layout attention.

Important classification questions include:

• What is the normal operating frequency or range of frequencies?

• Is the circuit high-frequency or low-frequency?

• Is it primarily digital or analogue?

• Is it mixed-signal?

• Is it primarily a power-electronic circuit?

• How many components are present?

• What budget and technology constraints apply?

• Are there restrictions on component selection or other operational requirements?

A mixed-signal circuit combines digital and analogue circuitry. Digital and switched power-electronic sections can generate significant noise, while analogue sections may contain sensitive low-level signals. Their physical separation is therefore important.

Power-electronic circuits require particular attention because they may carry high currents and may switch current rapidly, producing high \(di/dt\).

4. Ground and power traces

For most circuits, ground and power traces are among the most important traces to lay out correctly because they connect to many components.

Noise on a ground or power conductor can be conducted into every component connected to that conductor. The layout should therefore minimise both noise coupling into these traces and noise transmission along them.

The lecturer recommends:

• Keeping ground and power traces short.

• Making high-current ground and power traces wide.

• Avoiding large loops.

• Routing ground and power traces close together for long runs where possible.

• Positioning components so that these requirements can be met naturally.

Routing power and ground traces together increases their effective parasitic capacitance. This is beneficial for power and ground because the capacitance presents a lower impedance to high-frequency noise, attenuating that noise between the two conductors.

The same strategy is not generally desirable for unrelated signal traces. Parallel signal traces carrying different signals can develop significant parasitic capacitance and crosstalk.

5. Example of single-sided PCB layout

For a single-sided PCB, all conductive traces must be placed on one side of the board.

The lecture describes an example containing:

• A thick ground trace.

• A thick power trace.

• IC pads between or near the power and ground traces.

• Long parallel sections of power and ground trace.

The components are arranged so that the power and ground traces:

• Remain relatively short.

• Run together over long sections.

• Have increased parasitic capacitance between them.

• Connect efficiently to the IC pads.

This arrangement is only practical because the ICs have been positioned to support the desired trace geometry. Component placement therefore directly enables better trace layout.

6. Component placement

Component placement is presented as one of the most important parts of PCB design.

The lecturer gives the approximate rule of thumb that an experienced PCB designer may spend:

• Approximately 70% of the design time positioning components.

• Approximately 30% of the design time completing the trace layout.

This is not presented as a precise engineering law, but as an indication of the importance of placement.

Good component placement can:

• Shorten power and ground paths.

• Shorten signal paths.

• Reduce loop areas.

• Reduce parasitic resistance and inductance.

• Reduce the need for long, looping traces.

• Make it easier to route power and ground together.

• Support a continuous ground or power plane.

• Reduce the probability of bottlenecks or interrupted return paths.

If component placement is poor, trace routing may require long detours even when the circuit is electrically correct.

7. Trace width, length, and current

Ground and power traces may carry substantial current, particularly in power-electronic circuits.

Wide traces reduce:

• Resistance.

• Current density.

• Voltage drop.

• Heating associated with resistive losses.

• Some unwanted parasitic impedance.

Short traces reduce:

• Resistance.

• Stray inductance.

• Loop area.

• The ability to couple or radiate electromagnetic noise.

General signal traces usually carry less current and are often made narrower to conserve PCB area. They should still be kept as short as practical, but their width is normally determined more by signal integrity, manufacturing, current capacity, and space constraints than by the same requirements as high-current power traces.

There is a trade-off: shortening a general signal trace should not create a large loop in the associated power or ground return path. The overall signal-and-return-current geometry must be considered.

8. Loops, electromagnetic noise, and corners

Current flows in closed loops. The physical area enclosed by a current path strongly influences its stray inductance.

Large loops:

• Increase stray inductance.

• Are more effective at receiving electromagnetic interference.

• Are more effective at radiating electromagnetic noise.

• Increase the voltage generated by rapidly changing current.

The lecturer also warns against sharp corners, especially 90-degree corners, because current density can become concentrated around sharp corners. This can increase electromagnetic radiation, particularly in high-frequency circuits.

The preferred hierarchy presented is:

1. Avoid unnecessary loops.

2. Use 45-degree corners instead of 90-degree corners.

3. Use curved corners where the available tools and design requirements permit.

The lecturer notes that the sharp-corner issue is especially relevant to high-frequency circuits, although avoiding sharp corners is still considered good general practice.

9. Ground and power planes

Ground and power planes are the natural extension of making ground and power traces wider.

Planes can:

• Reduce current density.

• Reduce trace resistance.

• Reduce stray inductance.

• Provide a low-impedance current path.

• Potentially provide electromagnetic shielding for other traces if designed appropriately.

The primary purpose of a ground plane is to provide the shortest and lowest-impedance return-current path, especially for signals carrying rapidly changing current.

A plane is not automatically effective. Poor plane geometry can create:

• Narrow current bottlenecks.

• High-impedance sections.

• Unwanted loops.

• Longer return-current paths.

• Increased stray inductance.

Examples of poor plane use include:

• Removing a large section of the plane so current must pass through a narrow neck.

• Creating a hole or void that forces the return current to travel around it.

• Arranging components so that the return path forms a large loop.

• Placing a signal trace over a discontinuity in the reference plane.

The plane should ideally remain continuous underneath the associated signal trace. This allows the return current to flow directly beneath the signal, keeping the loop area small.

10. Two-layer and four-layer boards

The lecture describes a two-layer PCB with a ground plane on one layer and a power plane or signal traces on the other.

A four-layer PCB may contain:

• Top-layer traces and components.

• Internal power and ground planes.

• Bottom-layer traces or components.

The close spacing between a signal layer and its adjacent reference plane reduces the physical separation between outgoing and return current. This reduces the cross-sectional area of the current loop and therefore reduces stray inductance.

The transcript’s diagrams cannot be inspected from the text alone, so the exact layer arrangement of every example is not fully recoverable. The general principle stated is that a continuous reference plane should be located close to the relevant signal path.

11. General signal traces

General traces are defined in the lecture as traces other than ground and power traces.

They tend to:

• Have higher resistance than large power or ground paths.

• Travel between different parts of the circuit.

• Form more complicated or loop-like pathways.

• Have greater opportunity for noise coupling and transmission.

• Be narrower because there are often many of them and PCB area is limited.

General traces should generally:

• Be kept as short as practical.

• Avoid unnecessary loops.

• Be routed directly from one component to the next where possible.

• Avoid long parallel runs alongside unrelated signals.

• Be separated from noisy or sensitive functional groups as appropriate.

Unlike power and ground traces, unrelated general signal traces should not be intentionally routed together over long distances. Parallel routing increases the area over which parasitic capacitance exists and increases the likelihood of crosstalk.

12. Inductive loop minimisation

The lecture presents a driver and receiver IC connected by a high-speed signal trace.

Even when the signal trace is short, current must return through a ground path to complete the circuit. The outgoing trace and return path form a closed loop.

The lecturer states that stray inductance is proportional to the area of this loop:

\[

L_{\text{stray}} \propto A_{\text{loop}}

\]

A high-speed signal or rapidly changing current can therefore produce a significant voltage across the stray inductance:

\[

v = L\frac{di}{dt}

\]

Consequences of a large loop and high \(di/dt\) include:

• Voltage transients around the loop.

• Electromagnetic radiation.

• Noise coupling.

• Possible damage to an integrated circuit in a power-electronic application.

The best solution is to provide a close, continuous ground-plane return directly beneath the signal trace. This gives the return current a low-impedance path and minimises the loop area.

13. High-\(di/dt\) power-electronic loops

The lecture applies loop-area minimisation to a switching power converter associated with a solar-car project.

The example includes:

• A solar-panel input.

• Input capacitors.

• A buck converter.

• A low-side switch.

• A freewheeling diode.

• A power inductor.

• An output capacitor.

• A load, identified as a motor.

A switching loop carries current while the switch is closed. When the switch opens, the current changes rapidly, producing high \(di/dt\).

The stray inductance of the physical loop can be modelled as a small series inductor. The voltage generated by this inductance is:

\[

v_L = L_{\text{stray}}\frac{di}{dt}

\]

If the switch turns off rapidly and the loop has significant stray inductance, the resulting voltage spike may be large enough to damage the MOSFET or other components.

A mitigation technique is to place an additional capacitor physically close to the relevant switching components. At the high-frequency component of the current, the capacitor provides a short local path. This:

• Diverts the rapidly changing current through the nearby capacitor.

• Reduces the physical loop area.

• Reduces the effective inductance of the high-\(di/dt\) loop.

• Reduces the voltage spike generated by \(L\,di/dt\).

The capacitor must be suitable for high-frequency operation. A capacitor with unsuitable parasitic inductance or resistance may not provide the intended high-frequency bypass path.

14. Functional-group separation

Digital, analogue, and power-electronic sections should be separated physically where possible.

The purpose is to prevent:

• Digital switching noise from entering analogue circuitry.

• Switched power-electronic currents from disturbing sensitive analogue signals.

• Noise currents from sharing inappropriate return paths.

• Unwanted capacitive and inductive coupling between functional groups.

The ideal solution, if constraints allow, is to place different functional groups on separate PCBs:

• One PCB for digital circuitry.

• One PCB for analogue circuitry.

• One PCB for power electronics.

This is often impractical because of size, cost, mechanical, wiring, and system-integration constraints. Therefore, different functional regions often need to coexist on a single PCB.

For a single-board implementation, the lecturer recommends careful physical separation and appropriately arranged planes.

15. Star-ground connections

When several functional groups need a common ground reference, they can be connected using a star-ground arrangement.

In a star ground:

• Each functional group has a separate ground connection.

• The connections meet at a common central point.

• Shared ground currents are discouraged from forming loops through other functional groups.

• Each section remains referenced to the same common potential while reducing unwanted interaction.

On a single PCB, a local arrangement can approximate a star ground by bringing separate ground regions to a central connection point.

The exact implementation depends on the circuit and current paths. The lecture’s main principle is to prevent noisy return currents from flowing through sensitive analogue-ground paths.

16. Analogue, digital, and power plane separation

If analogue and digital ground or power regions are separated, traces must be routed so that they do not undermine the separation.

The lecturer specifically warns against:

• Routing a digital general signal trace across an analogue ground region.

• Allowing digital traces to pass across analogue-plane boundaries.

• Overlapping analogue and digital power or ground planes.

• Creating capacitive coupling paths between noisy digital regions and sensitive analogue regions.

A digital trace crossing near or across an analogue region can capacitively couple switching noise into the analogue ground. Similarly, overlapping digital and analogue planes increases capacitance between the regions and provides a path for noise transfer.

17. Capacitive coupling and crosstalk

Capacitance between traces increases with overlapping area and decreases with separation distance. The lecture states the qualitative relationship:

\[

C \propto \frac{A}{d}

\]

where:

• \(C\) is the parasitic capacitance.

• \(A\) is the overlapping or facing area.

• \(d\) is the separation distance.

Long parallel traces have a relatively large coupling area and can therefore produce significant parasitic capacitance.

To reduce capacitive coupling:

• Avoid routing unrelated traces in parallel for long distances.

• Increase separation between traces.

• Cross traces on different PCB layers where appropriate.

• Minimise the parallel overlap area.

The unwanted transfer of AC signals between adjacent traces is commonly called crosstalk.

18. Decoupling capacitors

Decoupling capacitors are connected in parallel between power and ground.

Their intended behaviour is:

• At DC, they appear approximately as open circuits and do not significantly alter the supply voltage.

• At AC, within their effective frequency range, they provide a low-impedance path between power and ground.

• Noise on the power or ground network is therefore attenuated.

The technique only works if the capacitor continues to behave capacitively at the frequencies of interest.

Different capacitor technologies have different useful frequency ranges. The transcript gives approximate values:

• Large aluminium electrolytic capacitors: recommended use up to approximately 100 kHz.

• Tantalum electrolytic capacitors: approximately up to 1 MHz.

• Mica or film capacitors: approximately up to 500 MHz.

• Ceramic capacitors: described as the best option for high-frequency operation.

These values are lecture-specific approximate figures, not universal component limits. Actual performance depends on component construction, package, layout, parasitics, voltage, temperature, manufacturer data, and the definition of “useful” impedance.

19. Non-ideal capacitor behaviour

Real capacitors contain parasitic elements, particularly:

• Equivalent series resistance (ESR).

• Equivalent series inductance (ESL).

These parasitics create a series-resonant response.

Below the self-resonant frequency, the capacitor generally behaves capacitively and its impedance decreases as frequency rises.

At the self-resonant frequency:

• The impedance reaches a minimum.

Above the self-resonant frequency:

• The equivalent series inductance dominates.

• The component begins to behave more like an inductor.

• Its impedance increases with frequency.

This explains why simply selecting a capacitor with a large nominal capacitance value does not guarantee effective high-frequency decoupling.

The lecturer notes that a smaller capacitor can have lower impedance at high frequency than a much larger capacitor of a different technology. The transcript gives an example in which, above approximately 20 MHz, a 10 nF film capacitor may have lower impedance than a 10 µF capacitor, although the exact component types and values in the spoken example are somewhat unclear.

Equations and definitions

Circuit layout

Circuit layout is the physical placement of components and the electrical connections between them on a circuit implementation such as a PCB.

Trace

A conductive path used to electrically interconnect circuit components on a PCB. The transcript also refers to traces as “tracks”.

Stray or parasitic capacitance

Unintended capacitance between conductors caused by their physical proximity, facing area, and separation.

Qualitatively:

\[

C_{\text{parasitic}} \propto \frac{A}{d}

\]

where \(A\) is the effective overlapping area and \(d\) is the separation distance.

Stray or parasitic inductance

Unintended inductance associated with the physical geometry of conductors and current loops.

For a loop:

\[

L_{\text{stray}} \propto A_{\text{loop}}

\]

as stated qualitatively in the lecture, where \(A_{\text{loop}}\) is the enclosed loop area.

Inductor voltage relationship

The voltage across an inductance is:

\[

v_L = L\frac{di}{dt}

\]

A large inductance or rapid current change produces a large voltage.

High-\(di/dt\) loop

A physical current loop in which current changes rapidly with time. Such loops are especially important in switching power electronics because they can generate large inductive voltage spikes.

Crosstalk

Unwanted transfer of a signal from one trace or circuit region to another, often through parasitic capacitive or inductive coupling.

Ground plane

A large conductive region used to provide a low-impedance ground connection and, importantly, a short return-current path beneath signal traces.

Power plane

A large conductive region used to distribute a supply voltage with lower resistance and inductance than narrow traces.

Star ground

A grounding arrangement in which separate functional groups connect independently to a common central ground point, reducing shared return paths and unwanted ground-current loops.

Decoupling capacitor

A capacitor connected between power and ground to provide a low-impedance path for AC noise while approximately isolating the DC supply conditions.

ESR

Equivalent series resistance. The effective resistance associated with a real capacitor’s internal and connection losses.

ESL

Equivalent series inductance. The effective inductance associated with a real capacitor’s internal structure, leads, package, and connections.

Self-resonant frequency

The frequency at which the capacitive reactance and parasitic inductive reactance cancel sufficiently for the component impedance to reach a minimum. Above this frequency, the capacitor can behave inductively.
