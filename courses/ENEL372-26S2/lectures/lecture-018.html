Key concepts

• Power electronics is concerned with efficient electrical power conversion, rather than simply signal-level electronics.

• High efficiency is primarily achieved by operating semiconductor switches in two states:

• Fully on

• Fully off

• In the ideal case:

• An off-state switch carries no current.

• An on-state switch has zero voltage drop or zero resistance.

• Therefore, the switch dissipates negligible power.

• Inductors and capacitors provide temporary or transient energy storage with comparatively low losses.

• Switching converters are also called switch-mode converters.

• Practical converters are not perfectly lossless, but well-designed modern systems may have losses of approximately 1–2%, according to the lecture.

• Filters are generally built from inductors and capacitors when low loss is required. Resistor-based filters dissipate power.

• Feedback is commonly used to regulate converter operation when the input source or load changes.

• The controller compares a measured output or system variable with a reference and adjusts a control variable affecting the switching converter.

• The four fundamental conversion classes are determined by whether the input and output are AC or DC.

• A converter may be implemented as multiple conversion stages, such as:

• DC to AC followed by AC to DC for an overall DC-to-DC function.

• AC to DC, followed by further conversion stages, for some AC-to-AC systems.

• Electrical isolation may be included between stages to address nonlinearity, matching, or safety requirements.

• Device selection depends on the application, especially:

• Required current

• Required voltage

• Switching frequency

• Conduction loss

• Gate-drive requirements

• Ability to turn on and off under control

• MOSFETs are generally preferred for very high switching frequencies.

• IGBTs provide a compromise between MOSFET-like gate control and BJT-like output behaviour.

• Thyristors handle very high voltage and current but are generally much slower than MOSFETs and IGBTs.

Switching operation and efficiency

The basic operating principle is to use semiconductor devices as switches rather than operating them continuously in their linear regions.

In the ideal case:

• When the switch is off, current is zero.

• When the switch is on, voltage across it is zero.

• Since switch power is approximately the product of voltage and current, power dissipation is low in either ideal state.

Inductors and capacitors are used with the switches to store and transfer energy. They are described as low-loss temporary or transient energy-storage components.

Linear power conversion is possible, such as with a linear voltage regulator, but it is treated separately from the technical field of power electronics in this course. The lecture gives approximately 80% as an upper efficiency figure for some linear-regulator applications, while noting that many practical cases are less efficient. Inductors in switching systems also enable functions that cannot be obtained from linear systems in the same way.

Generic power-electronic system

A general converter arrangement consists of:

1. An input source with voltage and current.

2. An input filter.

3. A switching converter.

4. An output filter.

5. A load.

6. A measurement and feedback-control system.

The input may be AC or DC and may be regulated or unregulated. The input filter and output filter are generally constructed from inductors and capacitors to minimise losses.

The converter changes the voltage and current waveform while transferring approximately the same power, apart from losses. If the input power is represented by \(P_X\), the output power \(P_Y\) is slightly lower because of converter losses.

A load or source may vary. Measurements are therefore fed to a controller. The controller compares the measured quantity with a reference and generates a control variable that modifies switching operation. This allows the converter to compensate for changes in the source or load.

Most practical power-electronic systems use some form of feedback. The course project is described as involving a controlled power converter rather than only an uncontrolled converter.

Regulated and unregulated sources

A regulated source has controlled parameters. For an AC source, these may include:

• Voltage magnitude

• Frequency

• Possibly other waveform parameters

The lecture uses mains electricity as an example of a regulated AC source. The voltage and frequency are controlled, while the current depends on the connected load.

Examples of unregulated sources include:

• A small wind turbine, where voltage magnitude and frequency may vary with available energy and operating conditions.

• A battery, whose terminal voltage changes as it discharges.

A regulated output does not necessarily have completely constant parameters at every instant. It means that the relevant parameters are controlled, commonly using feedback.

Diodes

A diode has two terminals:

• Anode

• Cathode

For conventional forward conduction:

• The anode must be positive relative to the cathode.

• A silicon diode typically requires approximately 0.7 V forward bias before substantial conduction in a simple practical model.

When the cathode is positive relative to the anode, the diode is reverse biased and ideally blocks current.

A practical diode has:

• A non-zero forward voltage.

• A finite forward resistance.

• A small reverse leakage current, described in the lecture as being in the nanoampere region.

• A reverse-breakdown region at sufficiently high reverse voltage.

Reverse breakdown may involve avalanche conduction. Unless the diode is specifically designed for operation in that region, excessive reverse voltage can damage or destroy it.

For initial power-electronics analysis, the ideal-diode model is used:

• Forward biased: short circuit, or zero resistance.

• Reverse biased: open circuit, or zero current.

Enhancement MOSFETs

An enhancement MOSFET is a voltage-controlled current device. For an N-channel enhancement MOSFET:

• The relevant control voltage is the gate-to-source voltage, \(V_{GS}\).

• The device remains off when \(V_{GS}\) is below its threshold voltage.

• The lecture gives approximately 2–3 V as a typical threshold-voltage range for a power MOSFET.

• Conventional power MOSFETs are normally driven at approximately 10 V, well above threshold, to ensure operation in the low-resistance ohmic region.

The lecture focuses on two operating regions for switching:

• Cutoff: the MOSFET is off and drain-to-source current is approximately zero.

• Ohmic region: the MOSFET is on and its drain-to-source resistance \(R_{DS(on)}\) is treated as small, ideally zero.

An enhancement MOSFET has an inherent body diode between drain and source. This is a parasitic component resulting from the physical construction of the device and cannot be avoided in a conventional enhancement MOSFET.

MOSFETs are very fast switching devices. The lecture states that good devices may switch in tens of nanoseconds.

Bipolar junction transistors

A bipolar junction transistor is a current-controlled current device.

For switching:

• Approximately zero base current places the transistor in cutoff.

• Applying sufficient base current enables collector current and emitter current.

• The device provides current gain.

Power BJTs have lower current gain than small-signal BJTs because their construction is intended to carry larger currents. The lecture gives a power-BJT current gain of approximately 50–80. This means that substantial base current may be needed when controlling a large collector current.

A Darlington pair uses two bipolar transistors in a cascaded configuration. The overall current gain is approximately enhanced by multiplying the gains of the two devices. The lecture indicates that this arrangement has a disadvantage, although the specific disadvantage is not clearly stated in the transcript.

Compared with MOSFETs:

• BJTs are slower to switch.

• At larger currents, a BJT may have a lower collector-emitter voltage than the drain-source voltage of a comparable MOSFET.

• This can give an effectively lower conduction resistance and better conduction efficiency in some high-current applications.

• BJTs can handle higher currents than MOSFETs in some application ranges, but the exact comparison depends on device ratings and operating conditions.

Insulated-gate bipolar transistors

An IGBT combines features of a MOSFET and a BJT:

• Its input is controlled by an insulated gate, like a MOSFET.

• It is voltage controlled.

• Its output behaviour is more like a bipolar transistor.

• It can have a relatively low collector-emitter saturation voltage.

• It offers good efficiency at high currents.

• It switches faster than a BJT because the gate is voltage controlled.

The lecture presents IGBTs as a compromise:

• Faster and easier to control than BJTs.

• Better suited to high-current operation than many MOSFETs.

• Not as extremely fast as MOSFETs.

Thyristors

Thyristors are used for very high-voltage and very high-current switching. Devices mentioned include:

• Silicon-controlled rectifier (SCR)

• Gate-turn-off thyristor (GTO)

• Triac

• MOS-controlled thyristor (MCT)

#### Silicon-controlled rectifier

An SCR behaves approximately like a diode whose forward conduction can be controlled by a gate pulse.

Operating sequence:

1. Under reverse bias, the SCR blocks current like a reverse-biased diode.

2. Under forward bias, it remains off until a suitable gate pulse is applied.

3. A gate pulse triggers conduction.

4. Once triggered, it latches on and behaves approximately like a forward-conducting diode.

5. The gate cannot turn a standard SCR off.

6. It turns off only when the circuit current falls to zero and the device becomes reverse biased.

#### Gate-turn-off thyristor

A GTO provides controlled turn-on and turn-off:

• A gate pulse can turn it on under forward bias.

• A suitable gate signal can turn it off while it is forward biased.

• The turn-off pulse requires a substantial current, which reduces overall efficiency.

#### Triac

A triac is approximately a bidirectional SCR:

• It can conduct in either current direction.

• A gate pulse can trigger conduction in the direction set by the applied bias.

• It cannot normally be turned off by the gate.

• It stops conducting when the current falls to zero or the bias conditions change appropriately.

• A gate pulse can then trigger conduction in the opposite direction.

#### MOS-controlled thyristor

An MCT is described as a thyristor with added MOS-controlled turn-on and turn-off functionality. It is intended for very high current and voltage applications.

Equations and definitions

Electrical power

For a general electrical system:

\[

P = VI

\]

where:

• \(P\) is electrical power.

• \(V\) is voltage.

• \(I\) is current.

For a converter:

\[

P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}} + P_{\text{loss}}

\]

Using the lecture’s notation:

\[

P_X = P_Y + P_{\text{loss}}

\]

where:

• \(P_X\) is input power.

• \(P_Y\) is output power.

• \(P_{\text{loss}}\) represents losses in the converter and associated components.

For an ideal converter:

\[

P_{\text{loss}} = 0

\]

For a practical converter:

\[

P_{\text{out}} < P_{\text{in}}

\]

Efficiency

Converter efficiency is defined as:

\[

\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}

\]

Equivalently:

\[

\eta = 1 - \frac{P_{\text{loss}}}{P_{\text{in}}}

\]

The lecture states that well-designed modern power-electronic systems may have losses of approximately 1–2%, implying efficiencies near—but below—100%.

Ideal switching states

For an ideal switch:

• Off state:

\[

i_{\text{switch}} = 0

\]

• On state:

\[

v_{\text{switch}} = 0

\]

Switch power is:

\[

p_{\text{switch}} = v_{\text{switch}} i_{\text{switch}}

\]

Thus, the ideal switch dissipates zero power in either steady state.

Ideal diode model

Forward-biased ideal diode:

\[

v_D = 0

\]

Reverse-biased ideal diode:

\[

i_D = 0

\]

A practical silicon diode has an approximate forward voltage of:

\[

V_F \approx 0.7\text{ V}

\]

The exact forward voltage depends on current, temperature, diode type, and device construction.

MOSFET switching condition

For an N-channel enhancement MOSFET:

• Approximate off condition:

\[

V_{GS} < V_{TH}

\]

• On-state drive condition:

\[

V_{GS} > V_{TH}

\]

where:

• \(V_{GS}\) is the gate-to-source voltage.

• \(V_{TH}\) is the threshold voltage.

The lecture states that power MOSFET threshold voltages are commonly approximately 2–3 V, while conventional power MOSFETs may be driven at approximately 10 V to ensure strong conduction.

In the ideal on-state model:

\[

R_{DS(on)} \approx 0

\]

Conversion definitions

• Rectifier: AC-to-DC converter.

• Inverter: DC-to-AC converter.

• DC-to-DC converter: changes one DC voltage/current level to another.

• AC-to-AC converter: changes an AC source into an AC output, potentially with altered voltage, current, frequency, or waveform.

• Switch-mode converter: a converter that uses semiconductor switching and energy-storage elements rather than continuous linear operation.

• Feedback control: measuring a system variable, comparing it with a reference, and adjusting converter operation to reduce the error.
