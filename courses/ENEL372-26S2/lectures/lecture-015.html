Overview

This lecture completes the design of nested feedback control loops for a motor-driven mechanical load:

• Inner torque/current control loop.

• Middle speed control loop.

• Optional outer position control loop.

The main design principle is to separate the bandwidths of the nested loops. Each outer loop should be substantially slower than the loop inside it, so that the inner loop appears approximately instantaneous or unity-gain from the perspective of the outer loop.

The lecture then applies similar control ideas to the solar-car project, where the controlled variable is solar-panel voltage rather than motor torque, speed, or position. The solar-panel voltage is regulated through the duty ratio of a buck converter controlled by a TL494 controller IC.

Key concepts

• Torque control in the motor is effectively current control because motor torque is proportional to armature current.

• The current-control bandwidth should be below the converter switching frequency to avoid reacting to switching-related current and voltage ripple.

• A practical current-control bandwidth is approximately one-tenth of the switching frequency, although one-fifth is given as a possible upper limit.

• For nested control loops, the speed-control bandwidth should be approximately one-tenth of the torque/current-control bandwidth.

• If a position loop is required, its bandwidth should again be approximately one-tenth of the speed-control bandwidth.

• With a 20 kHz switching frequency:

• Current/torque bandwidth: approximately 2 kHz.

• Speed bandwidth: approximately 200 Hz.

• Position bandwidth: approximately 20 Hz if the one-tenth rule is applied three times.

• The lecture also describes 200 Hz as a possible position-control bandwidth for a 20 kHz switching frequency. This appears inconsistent with the stated three-stage one-tenth reduction; see Caveats.

• The inner current loop can be treated as unity gain by the speed loop when it is sufficiently faster than the speed loop.

• The speed loop uses PI control because a non-zero motor current is required to maintain a non-zero speed under load, even when speed error is zero.

• The position loop uses proportional control because zero position error should correspond to zero speed; no residual speed is required at the final position.

• A PI speed controller is designed to provide a target phase margin of approximately 45°.

• A PI controller has a zero that can compensate part of the phase lag and change the open-loop gain slope.

• A solar-panel voltage controller requires integral control because a non-zero buck-converter duty ratio must be maintained even when voltage error is zero.

• The TL494 error amplifier can be used with an external capacitor to implement integral control.

• The solar-panel voltage must be scaled using a voltage divider so it can be compared with the TL494’s approximately 5 V reference.

• A practical integral time constant for the solar-car controller is stated as approximately 0.1–0.5 s.

• Proportional control can be added to the solar-panel controller if the voltage tracking response is inadequate, but it may not be necessary.

Detailed notes

1. Completion of the torque/current-control design

The previous lecture derived the transfer functions for the inner torque-control loop. Since motor torque is proportional to armature current, controlling torque can be treated as controlling current.

The controller was simplified by choosing the controller weightings so that a pole and a zero cancel. The lecture then selects the control-loop cutoff frequency or bandwidth based on the converter switching frequency.

The control system should not respond strongly to disturbances associated with switching ripple. Switching produces current ripple and a smaller amount of voltage ripple. If the current-control loop bandwidth is too high, the controller may attempt to respond to this ripple rather than only to the useful lower-frequency dynamics.

The lecture gives the design range

\[

f_c \approx \frac{f_\mathrm{sw}}{5}\text{ to }\frac{f_\mathrm{sw}}{10}

\]

with one-tenth preferred in practice because it provides more separation from switching-frequency noise.

At the cutoff frequency, the magnitude of the relevant open-loop transfer function is selected to be unity:

\[

|G(j\omega_c)|=1

\]

Using the previously derived simplified current-loop gain, the integral and proportional controller gains can be selected from the motor parameters, converter source voltage, and desired cutoff frequency.

The spoken transcript gives the relationships:

\frac{K_P}{K_I}=\frac{L_A}{R_A}

The lecturer states that the pole-zero simplification guarantees stability over the operating range and produces a fast torque-control loop. For a 20 kHz switching frequency, a one-tenth bandwidth gives approximately 2 kHz.

2. Nested speed-control loop

The next loop is the speed-control loop. It is slower than the inner torque/current loop.

If the current controller is functioning correctly, the actual armature current approximately equals the commanded armature current:

\[

i_A \approx i_{A,\mathrm{ref}}

\]

Therefore, from the perspective of the speed controller, the inner current loop can be approximated as a unity-gain block.

Motor torque is proportional to armature current:

\[

T_M = K_T i_A

\]

The difference between motor torque and load/resisting torque is the residual torque that accelerates the mechanical system:

\[

T_\mathrm{res}=T_M-T_L

\]

The mechanical relationship is

\[

J\frac{d\omega}{dt}=T_\mathrm{res}

\]

or

\[

\frac{d\omega}{dt}=\frac{T_\mathrm{res}}{J}

\]

Integrating gives the speed response. In the Laplace domain, the mechanical plant contributes an integrator:

\[

\frac{\Omega(s)}{T_\mathrm{res}(s)}=\frac{1}{Js}

\]

The speed feedback is taken from the motor side. Motor speed is compared with the commanded or ordered speed.

The speed-control bandwidth must be sufficiently below the torque/current-control bandwidth so that the speed controller does not interact with the dynamics of the inner loop. The lecture again recommends a factor of approximately ten:

\[

f_{c,\omega}\approx \frac{f_{c,i}}{10}

\]

Thus,

\[

f_{c,\omega}\approx \frac{f_\mathrm{sw}}{100}

\]

for the practical one-tenth choice at both levels.

3. Why speed control uses PI control

When the measured speed equals the commanded speed, the speed error is zero. However, the motor still requires armature current to produce torque and overcome the load torque.

Therefore, the controller must retain a non-zero output at zero steady-state speed error. Integral action provides this residual control output.

A proportional-only speed controller would produce zero current command when the speed error reached zero. That would generally remove the motor torque required to maintain the desired speed under load.

The speed controller is therefore selected as a PI controller:

\[

G_c(s)=K_P+\frac{K_I}{s}

\]

or, in pole-zero form,

\[

G_c(s)=K_P\left(1+\frac{K_I}{K_Ps}\right)

\]

The speed-loop open-loop plant is approximately the motor torque constant followed by the mechanical integrator:

\[

G_\mathrm{plant}(s)\approx \frac{K_T}{Js}

\]

The transcript refers to the controller and plant combination as having two poles and one zero. The two poles are associated with the PI integrator and mechanical integrator, while the PI zero contributes positive phase over an appropriate frequency range.

4. Speed-loop phase margin and Bode-plot design

Without suitable placement of the PI zero, the open-loop system has two poles and therefore:

• Gain slope: approximately \(-40\ \mathrm{dB/decade}\).

• Phase: approaching \(-180^\circ\).

This creates a risk that the gain magnitude may reach unity while the phase is close to \(-180^\circ\), producing poor stability or instability.

The PI zero changes the gain slope from approximately \(-40\ \mathrm{dB/decade}\) to \(-20\ \mathrm{dB/decade}\) above its corner frequency.

For a first-order zero:

• The phase contribution starts near \(0^\circ\).

• It reaches approximately \(45^\circ\) at its corner frequency.

• It approaches \(90^\circ\) at frequencies sufficiently above the corner.

The zero is placed so that its corner frequency coincides with the desired speed-loop crossover frequency, where the open-loop magnitude is unity. At that frequency, the zero contributes approximately \(+45^\circ\), giving a phase margin of approximately \(45^\circ\) for the two-pole system.

The design condition is stated as

\[

\left|\frac{K_P}{K_I}\right|=\omega_c

\]

or equivalently, depending on the definition used,

\[

\frac{K_I}{K_P}=\frac{1}{\omega_c}

\]

The transcript says “\(K_P/K_I\) is equal to 1” at one point, but the surrounding discussion indicates that the intended condition is that the PI zero be located at the selected crossover frequency. The exact notation should be checked against the preceding lecture’s controller definition.

At the crossover frequency, the complex expression is arranged to have real and imaginary parts of equal magnitude, giving a phase angle of \(45^\circ\). The magnitude of a term such as \(1+j\) is

\[

|1+j|=\sqrt{2}

\]

Using the selected crossover frequency and motor/mechanical parameters, the lecture gives the speed-loop integral gain in the form

\[

K_I=

\frac{J\omega_c^2}{\sqrt{2}K_T}

\]

The proportional gain is then obtained from the relationship between proportional and integral gains. The lecture states the resulting relationship as

\[

K_P=\frac{K_I}{\omega_c}

\]

The selected speed-loop cutoff is approximately one-tenth of the current-control bandwidth, or approximately one-hundredth of the converter switching frequency.

The resulting controller is intended to be fast and stable, with a nominal 45° phase margin. It is not specifically optimised for minimum rise time, overshoot, or other performance criteria.

5. Optional position-control loop

Position control is only required when the application needs the load to reach a specified position and stop. If the application only requires speed control, the position loop does not need to be designed.

When position control is required, the torque/current and speed controllers are embedded inside the position loop. The inner loops are treated as unity-gain blocks if they are sufficiently faster than the position loop.

The position and speed relationship is

\[

\omega=\frac{d\theta}{dt}

\]

Therefore,

\[

\Theta(s)=\frac{\Omega(s)}{s}

\]

The speed-to-position plant contributes another integrator:

\[

\frac{\Theta(s)}{\Omega(s)}=\frac{1}{s}

\]

Measured position is obtained using a position sensor such as a rotary encoder and compared with the commanded position.

At the desired final position:

• Position error is zero.

• Desired speed is zero.

• No non-zero residual speed command is needed.

Consequently, a proportional controller is sufficient:

\[

G_c(s)=K_P

\]

The open-loop transfer function is approximately

\[

G_\mathrm{OL}(s)=\frac{K_P}{s}

\]

This system has a maximum phase lag of approximately \(-90^\circ\), so the lecturer states that it is not expected to become unstable under the simplified model.

The position-loop crossover frequency is selected below the speed-loop bandwidth, again using approximately a factor of ten:

\[

f_{c,\theta}\approx \frac{f_{c,\omega}}{10}

\]

At the selected crossover frequency, unity gain requires

\[

\left|\frac{K_P}{j\omega_c}\right|=1

\]

so

\[

K_P=\omega_c

\]

for the normalised plant used in the lecture.

6. Solar-car control application

The solar-car system is considered from the power-flow perspective:

\[

\text{solar panel}\rightarrow\text{buck converter}\rightarrow\text{motor}

\]

The gearbox is not included in the simplified power-flow discussion.

The project uses a TL494 controller IC. The control objective is to extract maximum power from the solar panel. For a given set of light conditions, the maximum-power point occurs at a particular panel voltage.

The panel voltage is therefore the controlled variable:

1. Measure the solar-panel voltage.

2. Compare the measured voltage with a commanded or reference voltage.

3. Feed the voltage error into the TL494 control circuitry.

4. Adjust the buck-converter duty ratio.

5. Use the duty-ratio change to regulate the panel voltage at the desired maximum-power-point voltage.

At steady state, the measured panel voltage equals the commanded voltage, so the voltage error is zero. This does not mean that the buck-converter duty ratio becomes zero. A non-zero duty ratio must remain to maintain the required panel voltage.

Because a non-zero control output is required at zero steady-state error, integral control is necessary.

The solar-car dynamics are relatively slow:

• Motor torque and speed changes are slow compared with electrical switching dynamics.

• Light conditions are expected to remain nearly constant over the race or assessment.

• Surface irregularities are treated as relatively small disturbances.

• The voltage-control bandwidth should be selected so that the rest of the system appears approximately static while the controller tracks slow changes in panel conditions.

The lecturer describes the controller as PI in principle, but states that the proportional component may not need to be explicitly designed because the system’s inherent gain may provide sufficient proportional behaviour. Integral control remains essential.

7. Implementing the solar-panel integral controller

The TL494 includes an error amplifier. An op-amp-style differential input is used to compare:

• A scaled version of the measured solar-panel voltage.

• The commanded panel-voltage reference.

An integrator can be implemented by placing a capacitor in the feedback path of the error-amplifier circuit.

The panel voltage may be approximately 17 V under some lighting conditions, while the TL494 reference is approximately 5 V. A voltage divider is therefore required to scale the panel voltage to a suitable level.

The voltage divider should be adjustable or selected to accommodate different maximum-power-point voltages under different lighting conditions.

The TL494 reference voltage is stated as approximately

\[

V_\mathrm{ref}=5\ \mathrm{V}

\]

For the integral circuit, the lecture gives the integral time constant as

\[

T_I=R_1C

\]

and the integral gain as

\[

K_I=\frac{1}{T_I}=\frac{1}{R_1C}

\]

A practical time constant is stated as approximately

\[

T_I\approx 0.1\text{ to }0.5\ \mathrm{s}

\]

The lecturer warns that a much smaller time constant, such as 0.01 s, can cause stability problems in this slow system.

If panel-voltage tracking is not sufficiently close, proportional control can be added by introducing a resistor into the appropriate feedback path. The proportional gain is given as

\[

K_P=\frac{R_2}{R_1}

\]

The value of \(R_2\), and therefore the proportional gain, is to be determined experimentally during operation with the actual solar panel. The lecturer expects that many designs will work adequately without explicitly implementing this proportional component.
