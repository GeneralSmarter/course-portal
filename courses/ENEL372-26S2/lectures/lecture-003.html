The lecture focused on:

• The efficiency advantage of switching converters over linear regulators.

• Duty ratio and pulse-width modulation.

• Buck converter topology and energy recovery.

• Steady-state converter assumptions.

\[

V_{\text{out}} = D V_{\text{s}}

\]

• The boundary between continuous and discontinuous inductor current.

• Capacitor-current ripple and energy transfer between the inductor and capacitor.

The analysis assumes ideal components unless stated otherwise, including an ideal switch, ideal diode, ideal inductor, and ideal capacitor.

Key concepts

• Linear voltage conversion uses a controlled series element, often a bipolar junction transistor, behaving approximately like a variable resistance. The excess input energy is dissipated, resulting in lower efficiency.

• Switching voltage conversion controls energy transfer by rapidly switching semiconductor devices between fully off and fully on states.

• In the ideal switching approximation, a fully off or fully on semiconductor dissipates little power.

• Inductors and capacitors act as energy-storage elements. They allow a switched waveform to be converted into a more constant DC output.

• Pulse-width modulation (PWM) controls the average output by changing the fraction of each switching period for which the switch is on.

• The duty ratio is the fraction of one switching period during which the switch is on:

\[

D = \frac{T_{\text{on}}}{T}

\]

Since \(f = 1/T\):

\[

D = T_{\text{on}}f

\]

• A buck converter is a step-down DC–DC converter. Under ideal continuous-conduction operation, its output voltage is lower than or equal to its input voltage:

\[

V_{\text{out}} = D V_{\text{s}}

\]

• The buck converter’s diode provides a path for inductor current when the controllable switch is off. This enables recovery and continued delivery of stored inductor energy to the load.

• Continuous inductor current means that the inductor current varies but does not reach zero.

• Discontinuous inductor current occurs when the current reaches zero and remains there for part of a switching period.

• In steady state, the inductor-current waveform repeats every switching period. Therefore, the net change in inductor current over one period is zero.

• The average voltage across an inductor is zero in steady-state periodic operation.

• The average current through a capacitor is zero in steady-state periodic operation, and its average voltage does not change from period to period.

• For an ideal converter, average input power equals average output power.

• The output current is approximately constant when the load voltage is constant and the load is purely resistive.

• Capacitor-current ripple cancels the inductor-current ripple at the output node, leaving approximately constant load current.

• The switch current is equal to the inductor current while the switch is on and is zero while the switch is off. It is therefore discontinuous even when the inductor current is continuous.

• Output power can be calculated from DC quantities. Input power requires the RMS value of the pulsed source current.

Detailed notes

Linear versus switching conversion

A linear voltage regulator measures its output voltage, compares it with a reference, and adjusts a series-pass transistor. The transistor acts approximately as a variable resistance. When the input voltage is greater than the required output voltage, the difference is dissipated as heat or other resistive loss.

Linear regulators can provide a stable, low-ripple DC output, but their efficiency is generally poor when the voltage drop across the regulator is significant.

A switching converter instead controls power transfer by switching a semiconductor device periodically. When the switch is on, it ideally has very small voltage across it. When it is off, it ideally has very small current through it. Consequently, the semiconductor’s power dissipation can be much lower than that of a linear series element.

The switching waveform itself is not normally suitable as the final DC output because it alternates between different voltage levels. Inductors, capacitors, and diodes are used to store, transfer, and filter energy.

Basic switching-converter concept

The basic example consisted of:

• A DC source.

• A controllable semiconductor switch, identified in the transcript as an n-channel enhancement MOSFET.

• A resistive load.

When the switch is closed or on, the source voltage is transferred to the load. When the switch is open or off, the load is disconnected from the source and the load voltage is approximately zero in this simplified circuit.

The switching occurs periodically at a fixed switching frequency. Each period contains:

• An on interval, \(T_{\text{on}}\).

• An off interval, \(T_{\text{off}}\).

The duty ratio controls the proportion of the period spent in the on state. For the simple unfiltered switching circuit, the average load voltage is:

\[

\overline{V}_{\text{load}} = D V_{\text{s}}

\]

This average relationship motivates PWM: changing pulse width changes the average output value.

Buck-converter topology

The buck converter contains:

• A DC input voltage source, \(V_{\text{s}}\).

• A controllable switch.

• An inductor.

• A filter capacitor.

• A load, treated mainly as purely resistive in this lecture.

• A diode for the inductor’s freewheeling or energy-recovery path.

The inductor stores energy while the switch is on. When the switch is turned off, the inductor attempts to maintain current in the same direction. The diode becomes forward biased and provides a path for the inductor current.

The capacitor and inductor together smooth the switched waveform and support a relatively constant output voltage.

Continuous inductor-current assumption

The lecture assumes continuous inductor current unless stated otherwise. This means:

• The inductor current may rise and fall.

• It does not fall to zero during the switching period.

• Its average value may be positive.

• Its ripple is periodic in steady state.

Continuous conduction is not strictly required for the converter to operate, but it simplifies analysis and is generally preferred because it reduces current stress and losses compared with discontinuous operation.

Steady-state assumptions

The following steady-state conditions were used:

• The inductor-current waveform is periodic.

• The waveform repeats exactly from one switching period to the next.

• The average change in inductor current over a period is zero:

\[

\Delta i_L\big|_{\text{period}} = 0

\]

• The average voltage across the inductor is zero:

\[

\overline{v_L}=0

\]

• The average capacitor current is zero:

\[

\overline{i_C}=0

\]

• The average change in capacitor voltage is zero:

\[

\Delta v_C\big|_{\text{period}}=0

\]

• For the ideal converter:

\[

P_{\text{in}}=P_{\text{out}}

\]

Non-ideal losses are acknowledged but not included in the main derivation.

Switch-on interval

When the controllable switch is on:

• The switch is treated as a short circuit.

• The diode is reverse biased and treated as an open circuit under the ideal-diode assumption.

• The inductor sees the voltage:

\[

v_L = V_{\text{s}}-V_{\text{out}}

\]

Using the inductor relationship:

\[

v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}

\]

the inductor current has a positive slope when \(V_{\text{s}}>V_{\text{out}}\). Therefore, the current ramps upward during the on interval.

The on interval is:

\[

T_{\text{on}}=DT

\]

The increase in inductor current is:

\[

\Delta i_{L,\text{on}}

=

\frac{(V_{\text{s}}-V_{\text{out}})DT}{L}

\]

The current rises from its minimum value, \(I_{\min}\), to its maximum value, \(I_{\max}\).

Switch-off interval

When the controllable switch is off:

• The input source is disconnected from the switching path.

• The inductor releases energy.

• The inductor current continues in the same direction.

• The diode becomes forward biased and provides the current path.

• Under the ideal-diode assumption, the inductor is connected across the output network.

The inductor voltage reverses relative to the on-state reference:

\[

v_L=-V_{\text{out}}

\]

The current therefore ramps downward.

The off interval is:

\[

T_{\text{off}}=(1-D)T

\]

The decrease in inductor current is:

\[

\Delta i_{L,\text{off}}

=

-\frac{V_{\text{out}}(1-D)T}{L}

\]

The current falls from \(I_{\max}\) back to \(I_{\min}\).

Derivation of the ideal buck relationship

In periodic steady state, the total change in inductor current over one switching period is zero:

\[

\Delta i_{L,\text{on}}+\Delta i_{L,\text{off}}=0

\]

Substituting the on-state and off-state changes:

\[

\frac{(V_{\text{s}}-V_{\text{out}})DT}{L}

-

\frac{V_{\text{out}}(1-D)T}{L}=0

\]

Multiplying by \(L/T\):

\[

(V_{\text{s}}-V_{\text{out}})D

-

V_{\text{out}}(1-D)=0

\]

Expanding:

\[

V_{\text{s}}D

-

V_{\text{out}}D

-

V_{\text{out}}

+

V_{\text{out}}D=0

\]

The two \(V_{\text{out}}D\) terms cancel:

\[

V_{\text{s}}D-V_{\text{out}}=0

\]

Therefore:

\[

V_{\text{out}}=D V_{\text{s}}

\]

This is also the result obtained from the volt-second balance condition:

\[

\overline{v_L}=0

\]

For an ideal buck converter, the voltage conversion ratio is:

\[

\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_{\text{s}}}=D

\]

Continuous and discontinuous conduction

The inductor current is continuous when its minimum value remains above zero.

The transition to discontinuous conduction occurs when the lower edge of the inductor-current ripple just reaches zero. If the load current is represented by \(I_R\), and the inductor-current ripple has peak-to-peak magnitude \(\Delta i_L\), the boundary condition was described as:

\[

I_R=\frac{\Delta i_L}{2}

\]

At this boundary:

\[

I_{\min}=0

\]

If the load current becomes lower while the ripple remains the same, the inductor current reaches zero before the next switching cycle. It then remains at zero for part of the period.

Discontinuous operation is possible, but it increases the RMS current relative to the average current and therefore increases resistive losses. It can reduce converter efficiency and complicate the analysis.

Capacitor current

The load current is treated as approximately constant because the output voltage and resistive load are treated as constant. Applying Kirchhoff’s current law at the output node:

\[

I_R=i_L-i_C

\]

Rearranging:

\[

i_C=i_L-I_R

\]

Since \(I_R\) is constant, the capacitor current contains the ripple component of the inductor current.

The capacitor current has the same general triangular shape and slope magnitude as the inductor-current ripple, but with the opposite polarity relative to the load-current balance. This allows the capacitor to absorb or supply the difference between the varying inductor current and the approximately constant load current.

• Positive capacitor current, using the lecture’s sign convention, means current flows into the capacitor and the capacitor is charging.

• Negative capacitor current means the capacitor is discharging.

• Over one steady-state period, the average capacitor current is zero.

Switch/source current

When the switch is on, the switch current equals the inductor current:

\[

i_{\text{sw}}=i_L

\]

When the switch is off, the source-side switch current is zero:

\[

i_{\text{sw}}=0

\]

Therefore, the source/switch current waveform consists of the inductor-current ramp during the on interval and zero current during the off interval:

• It begins at \(I_{\min}\).

• It ramps to \(I_{\max}\) over \(DT\).

• It falls abruptly to zero when the switch turns off.

• It remains at zero during the off interval.

• It jumps back to the next cycle’s \(I_{\min}\) when the switch turns on.

This current is discontinuous even when the inductor current is continuous.

Input and output power

For the resistive load, the output power can be expressed as:

\[

P_{\text{out}}=V_{\text{out}}I_R

\]

Using Ohm’s law:

\[

P_{\text{out}}

=

\frac{V_{\text{out}}^2}{R}

=

I_R^2R

\]

For the input power:

\[

P_{\text{in}}

=

V_{\text{s,rms}}I_{\text{s,rms}}

\]

Because the source voltage is a constant DC value:

\[

V_{\text{s,rms}}=V_{\text{s}}

\]

However, the source current is pulsed and varying, so its RMS value must be calculated from the waveform. It cannot simply be replaced by its average value.
