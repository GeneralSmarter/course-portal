Key concepts

• A rectifier converts AC electrical power into DC electrical power.

• The rectifier output is not normally perfectly constant DC. The AC component superimposed on the mean output is called voltage ripple.

• A good rectifier produces:

• A nearly constant output voltage.

• A high proportion of useful DC power.

• An AC-side current waveform close to a sinusoid.

• Low harmonic distortion.

• A small phase difference between supply voltage and input current.

• Rectifier analysis considers two sides:

• Output/DC side: primarily the rectifier output voltage.

• Input/AC side: primarily the current drawn from the AC source.

• A rectifier connected to a sinusoidal mains supply may draw a non-sinusoidal current, even if the source voltage is sinusoidal.

• Non-sinusoidal current contains harmonic components.

• THD is a quantitative measure of the harmonic distortion of the current waveform relative to its fundamental component.

• Phase displacement between voltage and current reduces the useful real power transferred for given RMS voltage and RMS current.

• Harmonics and phase displacement can increase current, losses, conductor requirements, and grid-management difficulty.

• Rectifier efficiency compares DC output power with AC input power.

• For a resistive load:

• DC power is calculated using the mean output voltage.

• AC input power is associated with the RMS voltage or current.

• RMS values are affected by waveform fluctuations. For the same mean value, additional variation generally increases the RMS value.

• A constant output waveform has equal mean and RMS values and represents the ideal limiting case for a rectifier.

• The variable substitution \( \sigma = \omega t \) changes one complete period into the interval \(0\) to \(2\pi\), removing explicit dependence on frequency from many waveform calculations.

• For a half-wave rectifier, the negative half-cycle is blocked, so the output is zero during that half-cycle.

• For a bridge rectifier:

• One pair of diodes conducts during the positive source half-cycle.

• The other pair conducts during the negative source half-cycle.

• Current through the resistive load remains in the same direction for both source half-cycles.

• In the lecturer’s LTspice diode model, approximately 0.8 V must be overcome before significant forward conduction occurs.

• Two conducting diodes in a bridge path require approximately \(2 \times 0.8 = 1.6\ \text{V}\) of source voltage to overcome both forward drops.

Rectifier purpose and measurement sides

The lecturer framed a rectifier as a device that converts an AC supply into a DC output.

The two sides of the rectifier are analysed differently:

• On the output side, the main quantity of interest is the output voltage. The desired result is a voltage that is as close as possible to constant DC.

• On the AC input side, the main quantity of interest is usually the current waveform drawn from the supply. The source voltage may be nominally sinusoidal, but the rectifier may draw a distorted current.

• \(230\ \text{V RMS}\)

The lecturer noted that the source voltage provided by the mains may be sinusoidal, but connecting a rectifier can result in a current waveform that is not sinusoidal.

Output voltage ripple

A rectifier output generally contains:

• A mean or average DC component.

• A time-varying AC component around that mean.

The lecturer described ripple intuitively as the fluctuation of the output voltage around its mean value.

The ripple voltage can be represented as the deviation:

\[

v_{\text{ripple}}(t) = v_D(t) - V_D

\]

where:

• \(v_D(t)\) is the instantaneous rectifier output voltage.

• \(V_D\) is the mean or average output voltage.

The RMS ripple magnitude is obtained by taking the RMS value of this time-varying deviation. The ripple factor then expresses the ripple relative to the mean output voltage:

\[

r = \frac{V_{\text{ripple,rms}}}{V_D}

\]

Using the relationship between total RMS voltage and its DC component:

\[

V_{\text{ripple,rms}}

=

\sqrt{V_{D,\text{rms}}^2 - V_D^2}

\]

the ripple factor can also be written as:

\[

r

=

\frac{\sqrt{V_{D,\text{rms}}^2 - V_D^2}}{V_D}

\]

The transcript’s wording around “take the RMS” and “divide by the mean” is partially corrupted, but it clearly identifies ripple as the RMS fluctuation relative to the mean output voltage.

A perfectly constant output has no ripple:

\[

V_{D,\text{rms}} = V_D

\]

and therefore:

\[

V_{\text{ripple,rms}} = 0,

\qquad

r = 0

\]

Input current waveform and harmonics

The lecturer stressed that rectifiers can draw a current waveform that differs substantially from a sine wave.

Possible non-sinusoidal shapes include waveforms with:

• Sharp pulses.

• Trapezoidal sections.

• Additional oscillations.

• Other distortions from the ideal sinusoidal shape.

A non-sinusoidal periodic waveform can be represented as a sum of sinusoidal components. These include:

• The fundamental component, at the supply frequency.

• Higher-order harmonic components, at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency.

The lecturer identified THD as a common metric for describing harmonic content in the current waveform.

The transcript describes the fundamental as a perfect sine wave and the actual AC current as a non-sinusoidal waveform. The RMS size of the harmonic or distortion component is compared with the RMS value of the fundamental.

A standard expression consistent with the lecture description is:

\[

\mathrm{THD}

=

\frac{I_{\text{harmonics,rms}}}{I_{1,\text{rms}}}

\]

where \(I_{1,\text{rms}}\) is the RMS value of the fundamental current and \(I_{\text{harmonics,rms}}\) is the combined RMS value of the higher-order harmonic currents.

Equivalently, if the total current RMS consists of the fundamental and harmonics:

\[

I_{\text{harmonics,rms}}

=

\sqrt{I_{\text{rms}}^2-I_{1,\text{rms}}^2}

\[

\mathrm{THD}

=

\frac{\sqrt{I_{\text{rms}}^2-I_{1,\text{rms}}^2}}{I_{1,\text{rms}}}

\]

Phase displacement and power-system effects

The lecturer also discussed the phase relationship between the AC voltage and current.

If voltage and current are out of phase, their instantaneous product is reduced at some points in the cycle. The lecturer described this as reducing the amount of useful real power transferred for a given voltage and current magnitude.

For sinusoidal voltage and current, real power is conventionally expressed as:

\[

P = V_{\text{RMS}} I_{\text{RMS}} \cos\phi

\]

where \(\phi\) is the phase angle between voltage and current.

A phase displacement can result in:

• Reduced power factor.

• Increased current for a required real-power transfer.

• Greater losses in cables.

• Larger or more expensive conductors.

• Additional stress on the power network.

For an ideal half-wave rectifier:

• The negative half-cycle is blocked.

\[
