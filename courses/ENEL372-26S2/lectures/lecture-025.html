Detailed notes

Accuracy of the inductor-sizing approximation

The previous lecture derived an analytical formula for the minimum inductor value. The major implicit assumption was that all current ripple could be represented by the second harmonic.

A practical rectifier waveform contains higher harmonics as well. The second harmonic is treated as the predominant component, while the higher harmonics are ignored to obtain a simple analytical expression. Ignoring these additional harmonics causes the calculated ripple to be smaller than the actual ripple. Therefore, the real inductor required should be larger than the value predicted using only the second harmonic.

The lecturer compared the analytical result with an LTspice simulation:

• Analytical inductor value: approximately 0.21.

• Simulated minimum inductor value: approximately 0.23.

• Approximate error: 10%.

The transcript does not clearly state the units of these values. The lecturer considered the difference acceptable for most ground-based applications, although the additional mass could matter in aerospace or solar-car applications.

Capacitor design and filter behaviour

The rectifier and load were treated as an LCR filter. The capacitor primarily reduces voltage ripple on the DC side, while the inductor primarily reduces current ripple.

The design process involves examining how changes in filter parameters affect the natural frequency and attenuation. The lecturer discussed attenuating a component at approximately 100 Hz. To increase attenuation at 100 Hz, the filter characteristic should be shifted to a lower frequency.

The qualitative effects discussed were:

• Increasing capacitance lowers the natural frequency and reduces voltage ripple.

• Increasing inductance lowers the natural frequency and reduces current ripple.

• Lowering the filter natural frequency increases attenuation at a selected higher frequency.

• Increasing resistance can change the damping and filter response, but adding physical resistance causes power loss and heat generation, reducing efficiency.

The lecturer described the filter as second order. If damping is too low, the response may exhibit a peak near its natural frequency. This peak can amplify noise or ripple near that frequency. A small amount of pass-band ripple may provide a sharper transition, but it represents a trade-off between pass-band flatness and attenuation.

The lecturer distinguished between:

• Time-domain ripple: variation of voltage or current with time.

• Frequency-domain pass-band ripple: variation in the magnitude response within the pass band.

Filter design must consider more than attenuation alone. Cost, mass, physical size, efficiency, and the required ripple level also affect the choice of capacitance, inductance, and resistance.

Three-phase diode-bridge rectifier

The lecture then moved to three-phase rectifiers. The detailed diode-by-diode analysis was presented mainly for understanding and was explicitly described as not directly examinable in that detailed form.

A three-phase diode bridge has six diodes. At any instant:

• One upper diode is forward biased and supplies the positive output.

• One lower diode is forward biased and provides the return path.

• The remaining four diodes are reverse biased.

The conducting pair is determined by the phase voltages:

• The phase with the highest instantaneous voltage determines the positive-side conducting diode.

• The phase with the lowest instantaneous voltage determines the negative-side conducting diode.

This is because current flows from the greatest instantaneous potential toward the lowest instantaneous potential through the load.

For example, during an interval from 0° to 60°, the red phase was described as the highest voltage and the green phase as the lowest. Current therefore flows out of the red phase through its upper diode, through the load, and back through the lower diode associated with the green phase.

As the phase voltages change, the conducting pair changes. In the following interval, the blue phase may become the lowest phase and replace the green phase in the return path. The lecturer used intervals such as 0°–60° and 60°–120° to illustrate these commutations.

The detailed switching analysis can be performed by comparing the instantaneous phase voltages. A diode is conducting when it is forward biased and is blocked when it is reverse biased.

Three-phase ripple and output smoothness

A single-phase full-wave rectifier produces two output pulses per input period. Its dominant ripple component is associated with the second harmonic.

A three-phase bridge rectifier produces six output pulses per input period. Its dominant ripple component is associated with the sixth harmonic.

The higher ripple frequency provides two advantages:

• The DC voltage is smoother for the same filter size.

• A given filter can attenuate the ripple more easily because it is operating at a higher frequency relative to the desired DC component.

The lecturer also stated that the AC-side current is generally better and closer to a sinusoid in the three-phase case.

The output current through the load can remain approximately continuous when inductance is present, although some ripple remains. The amount of current ripple depends on the inductor size and other circuit conditions.

Constant-current load versus resistive load

For a constant-current load, the load current is represented as essentially constant. In a circuit with a sufficiently large inductor, the current would be nearly constant but could still contain some ripple.

For a purely resistive load, the current follows the applied voltage, so some current ripple is expected. However, in the three-phase example, the ripple was small and the resistive-load current waveform was visually very similar to the constant-current waveform.

The lecturer stated that:

• The two models produce similar current waveforms in the example.

• Their FFTs are similar.

• The resistive-load model contains additional higher-frequency components, but these have relatively low amplitude.

• The additional components therefore have little effect on the overall THD in the case shown.

• The constant-current model is easier to analyse and captures most of the behaviour relevant to the current-draw analysis.

This makes the constant-current load a useful minimal model even when the actual load is resistive or more complicated.

Constant-voltage load and behavioural voltage model

The lecturer next considered a constant-voltage load and compared it with a more complicated circuit containing inductors and an RC load.

The purpose was to reproduce the voltage applied by the full three-phase rectifier without explicitly analysing every switching state.

The model was constructed by:

2. Taking the absolute values where required.

4. Applying the resulting behavioural voltage to a simpler equivalent circuit.

The lecturer noted that a piecewise construction using Heaviside functions would be possible but cumbersome because the switching instants would need to be determined. The behavioural-source approach was much simpler for simulation and comparison.
