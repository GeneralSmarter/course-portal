Detailed notes

AC-coupled amplification

The lecture begins with AC-coupled amplifier circuits. A capacitor is used to pass the AC signal from one module to another while removing the steady-state DC offset. The signal may be amplified at each stage, with a capacitor between stages preventing the DC operating point of one stage from being transferred directly into the next.

A cascaded amplifier consists of multiple amplifier stages. Each stage can provide additional gain, and AC coupling allows the stages to have separate DC bias conditions. The lecturer described a signal that is first shifted in DC level, then amplified, and subsequently coupled through a capacitor that removes the offset before another amplification stage.

The circuits discussed include both inverting and non-inverting op-amp configurations. The inverting case has the signal applied to the negative input terminal; the non-inverting case has the signal applied to the positive input terminal.

Biasing with a single supply

A single-supply circuit is useful in portable, automotive, marine, aerospace, and other applications where multiple power supplies would increase cost, weight, or complexity.

The signal is commonly biased around:

\[

V_{\text{bias}} \approx \frac{V_{CC}}{2}

\]

This allows the output waveform to swing above and below the bias point while remaining within the supply rails. If the bias point is poorly chosen, one side of a sine wave may clip before the other. Correct biasing maximises the available output range.

The lecturer emphasised that a nominally DC source, including a battery, is not perfectly constant. Supply fluctuations can enter the amplifier and interfere with the intended signal and bias conditions.

Op-amp feedback and stability

An op amp was described as a very high-gain amplifier containing feedback control. In normal closed-loop operation, feedback causes the voltages at the positive and negative input terminals to be approximately equal, subject to the usual operating assumptions.

Using block-diagram algebra, the closed-loop transfer function was described in the form:

\[

T(s) = \frac{A(s)}{1 + A(s)\beta(s)}

\]

where:

• \(A(s)\) is the open-loop amplifier transfer function.

• \(\beta(s)\) is the feedback factor.

• \(A(s)\beta(s)\) is the loop gain.

• \(T(s)\) is the closed-loop transfer function.

The lecturer distinguished the closed-loop transfer function from the open-loop transfer function. The open-loop gain can be very large, but it varies with frequency. A simple op-amp model may include one or more poles, producing gain roll-off and phase lag.

For stability assessment, the loop response must be considered at the frequency where the phase reaches approximately \(-180^\circ\). The lecturer stated that positive gain margin and positive phase margin indicate stability in the example being discussed. If the gain at the \(-180^\circ\) crossing becomes positive in decibels, the gain margin becomes negative and the system is unstable.

Increasing \(\beta\) increases the loop gain. This may improve response speed or reduce transient effects, but excessive feedback can reduce gain margin and cause instability. The amplifier model is also not necessarily valid over all frequencies. Temperature, frequency, resonances, and unmodelled higher-order dynamics can change the transfer function and effective system order.

The lecturer warned that a model appearing to be second- or third-order may fail near resonant or high-frequency regions, where additional poles and dynamics can become important.

Supply noise in single-supply circuits

A basic single-supply bias network can allow supply fluctuations to enter the op amp directly. The lecturer described this as poor power-supply noise rejection.

A resistor divider that produces half the supply voltage also divides supply fluctuations by approximately two. If the divider produces:

\[

V_{\text{bias}} = \frac{V_{CC}}{2}

\]

then a fluctuation \(\Delta V_{CC}\) produces approximately:

\[

\Delta V_{\text{bias}} = \frac{\Delta V_{CC}}{2}

\]

The corresponding attenuation in decibels was given as:

\[

20\log_{10}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \approx -6.0\ \text{dB}

\]

Thus, the simple divider provides only about 6 dB of voltage attenuation of the supply fluctuation. The lecturer referred to this in the context of power-supply noise rejection.

Supply noise entering the op amp can act as an additional input signal. In a cascaded amplifier, this disturbance may propagate through later stages. It can alter the effective dynamics, create resonances, and contribute to instability.

High-pass filtering of the input signal

The input coupling capacitor and its associated resistance form a high-pass filter. This filter blocks the input DC offset while passing the AC signal.

The high-pass cutoff frequency should be substantially lower than the wanted signal frequency. If the cutoff is too close to the signal frequency, the signal will be attenuated.

For a first-order RC high-pass or low-pass filter, the cutoff frequency is normally expressed as:

\[

f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}

\]

The transcript does not explicitly state this formula, but the lecturer discusses selecting resistors and capacitors to achieve specified cutoff frequencies.

The lecturer used an example with a signal frequency of approximately 100 Hz. The high-pass cutoff was to be chosen at about one-tenth of the signal frequency, giving an approximate cutoff of 10 Hz. This was presented as a rule of thumb rather than a universal design requirement.

Zener-diode bias/reference

The lecturer discussed using a Zener diode as an alternative to a resistor divider for generating a more stable bias voltage. The intended bias voltage remains approximately:

\[

V_{\text{bias}} \approx \frac{V_{CC}}{2}

\]

so the selected Zener voltage should be compatible with the required bias point.

A Zener diode operated in its reverse-breakdown region can maintain an approximately constant voltage over a range of current. The lecturer described a current range between \(I_{Z,\min}\) and \(I_{Z,\max}\), within which the Zener voltage remains relatively constant.

Advantages:

• Improved bias-voltage stability.

• Better attenuation of supply-voltage fluctuations.

• Current variations produce relatively small voltage variations within the operating range.

Disadvantages:

• The Zener diode consumes current and therefore power.

• A voltage drop across the Zener arrangement reduces efficiency.

• The Zener voltage may fall if the supply voltage drops too far.
