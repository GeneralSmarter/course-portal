Key concepts

• A three-phase rectifier produces a higher average DC output voltage and lower ripple than a comparable single-phase rectifier.

• The relevant AC voltage for a three-phase bridge rectifier is generally a line-to-line voltage, formed by subtracting two phase voltages.

• In a balanced three-phase system, phase voltages are separated by 120°.

• The difference between two equal-amplitude sinusoidal phase voltages is another sinusoid at the same frequency, with a different amplitude and phase.

• For a balanced system, the line-to-line RMS voltage is √3 times the phase-to-neutral RMS voltage:

VLL = √3 Vs

where Vs is the phase-voltage RMS value.

• The average of a periodic waveform is independent of the chosen time origin. The waveform can therefore be shifted to make the integration easier.

• Symmetry allows the average over the complete rectified waveform to be obtained from a smaller interval, such as half of one pulse.

• A three-phase six-pulse rectifier has a dominant output ripple component at six times the supply angular frequency:

ωripple = 6ω

• The corresponding dominant ripple component is the sixth harmonic.

• In the Fourier-series analysis, the sixth-harmonic coefficient represents peak amplitude. LTspice FFT results may instead be displayed as RMS amplitudes, requiring conversion before comparison.

• The sixth-harmonic approximation is not exact because other harmonics are present, but it provides a useful design estimate.

• An output inductor reduces current ripple. The inductor value can be selected using the estimated ripple amplitude.

• Continuous conduction requires the ripple current amplitude to remain below the average DC current. If the ripple is too large, current reaches zero and conduction becomes discontinuous.

• Discontinuous conduction can produce undesirable transients because an inductor resists abrupt changes in current.

• Source inductance changes the rectifier waveforms and introduces additional commutation effects and voltage drops.

• Start-up at an unfavourable phase can produce large current and voltage transients.

• A temporary series resistance can limit start-up current, although it causes power loss and heating.

• A capacitor reduces output-voltage ripple. As capacitance becomes very large, the voltage ripple approaches zero in the idealised case.

• A nonlinear circuit does not generally preserve a sinusoidal waveform. A sinusoidal input to a nonlinear system produces a nonsinusoidal output containing harmonics.

• THD is dimensionless and is normally reported as a percentage.

• Three-phase rectifiers generally provide lower THD and better overall performance than comparable single-phase rectifiers at similar normalised load conditions.

Three-phase line-to-line voltage

A three-phase supply consists of three sinusoidal phase voltages of the same frequency and amplitude, separated by 120°.

A line-to-line voltage is the difference between two phase voltages. For example:

vLL(t) = vred(t) − vgreen(t)

The difference remains sinusoidal at the same frequency, but its amplitude and phase differ from those of the individual phase voltage.

The lecturer distinguishes instantaneous and RMS quantities:

• Lowercase v denotes a time-varying instantaneous voltage.

• Uppercase V denotes a constant or RMS quantity in the notation being used.

• Vs is treated as the phase-voltage RMS value.

• VLL is treated as the line-to-line RMS value.

For a balanced three-phase system:

VLL = √3 Vs

The derivation uses the 120° phase displacement and trigonometric identities. The lecturer explicitly states that proving the line-to-line relationship is not examinable, but students must understand that line-to-line voltage is different from phase voltage and must know the √3 relationship.

The line-to-line waveform can be written conveniently as a cosine by choosing the time origin at its maximum. Changing the time origin does not change the average value of a periodic waveform.

Average output voltage of a three-phase rectifier

The rectifier output is formed from the largest positive line-to-line voltage at each instant. The resulting waveform consists of repeated positive pulses.

To calculate the average output voltage, the lecturer uses two simplifications:

1. Shift the waveform in time so that a peak occurs at t = 0.

2. Use waveform symmetry to integrate over only part of the waveform.

The rectified waveform has six pulses per supply cycle. Because of symmetry, the average over the whole waveform is equal to the average over a suitably selected half-pulse or equivalent interval.

The line-to-line waveform is expressed in cosine form. The peak line-to-line voltage is related to the RMS line-to-line voltage by:

VLL,peak = √2 VLL

The average is obtained by integrating the cosine over the selected interval and dividing by the interval length. The standard result for an ideal three-phase, six-pulse diode bridge is:

Vd,avg = (3√2/π)VLL

or approximately:

Vd,avg ≈ 1.35VLL

The lecture transcript does not clearly display every intermediate integration limit or symbol, but the lecturer states that the result is the average voltage at the output of a three-phase rectifier.

The lecturer emphasises that this average output can be greater than the peak of one phase-to-neutral supply voltage. This is not a contradiction because the rectifier output is based on line-to-line voltages, whose peak value is √3 times the phase-voltage RMS value after the relevant RMS-to-peak conversion.

The relatively high output voltage must be considered when assessing voltage stress and possible voltage surges.

The important design relationship is:

• Larger L produces smaller current ripple.

• Higher ripple frequency produces smaller current ripple for the same ripple-voltage amplitude.

• A three-phase rectifier has a higher ripple frequency than a single-phase rectifier, so its output inductor can achieve lower current ripple for comparable conditions.

Continuous and discontinuous conduction

The average DC current is denoted approximately by ID. The sixth-harmonic ripple current has amplitude I6,peak.

To maintain continuous conduction, the ripple must not drive the instantaneous current to zero. A sufficient condition in the sinusoidal ripple approximation is:

I6,peak < ID

If:

I6,peak ≥ ID

the instantaneous current can reach zero, leading to discontinuous conduction.

The lecturer notes that discontinuous conduction is undesirable because it introduces transients. Inductors resist abrupt changes in current, so forcing the current to zero can create large voltage responses and stress components.

The design procedure is therefore:

1. Estimate the sixth-harmonic voltage ripple.

2. Calculate the corresponding current-ripple amplitude.

3. Select an inductance large enough that the ripple remains below the average DC current.

4. Check the result using simulation or waveform plots.

The lecturer says that students may memorise the final design formula, but they should understand the principles because an examination question may use a variation of the standard problem.

Start-up voltage and current surges

At start-up, the rectifier may be connected at an unfavourable phase angle. This can cause a large transient current.

A common current-limiting technique is to insert a large resistance during start-up. The resistance reduces the initial current surge and can make start-up more controlled.

The disadvantage is power dissipation:

• The resistor converts electrical energy into heat.

• It reduces efficiency.

• It may be acceptable during development or for short start-up intervals.

• It can be impractical if the start-up energy is large or repeated frequently.

Summary waveforms and source inductance

The lecture compares waveforms for rectifier circuits with and without source inductance.

For a single-phase example, an inductor and resistor are used as the load. The lecturer notes that examination questions more commonly use an LCR circuit, although the LR case is useful for understanding the waveforms.

Source inductance changes the current waveform by introducing a finite slope during commutation. The waveform may show:

• A current ramp rather than an instantaneous change.

• Ripple in the inductor current.

• Voltage drops or notches associated with commutation.

• Possible waveform excursions below zero in some plotted cases.

The lecturer says that students should understand the main effect of source inductance, but should not focus on every small waveform feature unless a question specifically asks for it.

A constant-current source can be used as an approximation for the load when analysing the rectifier waveforms.

Capacitive filtering and nonlinear behaviour

An RC circuit is used to demonstrate capacitive filtering.

Adding a capacitor across the rectifier output:

• Stores charge near the voltage peaks.

• Supplies load current between peaks.

• Reduces the output-voltage ripple.

• Produces increasingly small ripple as capacitance increases.

In the idealised limit of very large capacitance, the ripple approaches zero.

The lecturer describes the rectifier and filter as nonlinear. More precisely, a diode rectifier is a nonlinear system and produces a nonsinusoidal output from a sinusoidal input. A linear system driven by a sinusoid produces a sinusoidal response at the same frequency, whereas a nonlinear system can generate harmonics.

The lecturer notes that THD and related spectral measures can be considered for inverters as well. For a rectifier, output-voltage ripple is a major concern; for an inverter, the quality and harmonic distortion of the AC output are especially important.

Equations and definitions

RMS and peak conversion

For a sinusoidal voltage:

Vpeak = √2 Vrms

Vrms = Vpeak / √2

Balanced three-phase voltages

For a balanced three-phase supply:

• Phase displacement = 120°.

• Three phase voltages complete one cycle over 360°.

The line-to-line voltage is the difference between two phase voltages:

vab(t) = va(t) − vb(t)

For a balanced system:

VLL = √3 Vs

where:

• Vs is the phase-to-neutral RMS voltage.

• VLL is the line-to-line RMS voltage.

Three-phase rectifier average output

For an ideal three-phase six-pulse rectifier:

Vd,avg = (3√2/π)VLL

Approximately:

Vd,avg ≈ 1.35VLL

This result assumes the ideal rectifier conditions used in the lecture. Source inductance, device voltage drops, commutation overlap, and discontinuous conduction can alter the actual output.

Ripple frequency

For a three-phase six-pulse rectifier:

ω6 = 6ω

where:

• ω is the supply angular frequency.

• ω6 is the angular frequency of the dominant sixth-harmonic output ripple.

The corresponding frequency is:

f6 = 6f

For a single-phase full-wave rectifier, the dominant ripple frequency is approximately 2f.

Fourier-series representation

A periodic waveform can be represented as:

v(θ) = A0 + Σ[An cos(nθ) + Bn sin(nθ)]

The sixth-harmonic coefficients are obtained from integrals over one period. The generic cosine coefficient is:

A6 = (1/π)∫0^2π v(θ)cos(6θ)dθ

The lecture states that symmetry gives:

B6 = 0

The sixth-harmonic amplitude is estimated from the resulting coefficient. The exact coefficient convention should be checked because some Fourier-series definitions place a factor of 2 in the DC term or use different notation.

Inductor impedance

At angular frequency ω:

ZL = jωL

The magnitude is:

|ZL| = ωL

At the sixth harmonic:

|ZL,6| = 6ωL

Approximate sixth-harmonic current ripple

Using the sixth-harmonic voltage amplitude:

I6,peak ≈ V6,peak / (6ωL)

This is an approximation based on treating the sixth-harmonic component independently and neglecting other harmonics and nonideal circuit effects.

Continuous-conduction condition

Using the sinusoidal ripple approximation:

Iinstantaneous ≈ ID ± I6,peak

A practical condition for avoiding zero current is:

I6,peak < ID

If the ripple amplitude is too large relative to the DC current, the current reaches zero and conduction becomes discontinuous.

THD

THD is a dimensionless measure of harmonic content, commonly expressed as a percentage. The lecture does not provide the full THD formula, but the quantity compares the RMS content of harmonic components with the fundamental component.

Normalised load

The normalised load is described as a current divided by the short-circuit current:

Inormalised = Iload / Isc

This is dimensionless and allows rectifier performance to be plotted independently of absolute source conductance and frequency, subject to the assumptions of the normalisation.
