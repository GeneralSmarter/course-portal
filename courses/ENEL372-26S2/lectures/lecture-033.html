Main topics:

• Resistor Johnson noise and op-amp voltage and current noise.

Key concepts

• Resistors generate thermal, or Johnson, noise.

• Semiconductor devices in op amps generate shot noise and other internal noise.

• Op-amp noise is commonly represented by:

• Input-referred voltage-noise density, \(e_n\).

• Input-referred current-noise density, \(i_n\).

• Independent noise sources are combined by adding their mean-square values, then taking the square root.

• Noise is not normally added by directly adding amplitudes.

• A current-noise source produces an equivalent voltage-noise contribution when it flows through an effective resistance.

• The current-noise contribution increases with source or equivalent resistance.

• High source resistance generally requires an op amp with low current noise.

• Low source resistance generally makes low op-amp voltage noise more important.

• Bipolar-input op amps tend to have low voltage noise, according to the lecture.

• The best op amp depends on the complete application, not simply on which device has the lowest value of one noise specification.

• Feedback resistors and source resistance can dominate the total noise, even when the op amp itself is relatively low-noise.

• Noise spectral density varies with frequency and must be considered at the operating frequency.

• Wideband noise accumulates over bandwidth.

• A low-pass filter reduces the integrated noise power by limiting the frequency range.

• Analogue filtering is required before an ADC when unwanted high-frequency components could alias into the sampled signal.

• The op-amp selection guide uses a mathematical equivalent resistance:

\[

R_\text{op} = \frac{e_n}{i_n}

where \(e_n\) and \(i_n\) must be expressed consistently.

• The lecture distinguishes between:

• \(R_\text{eq}\): the actual equivalent resistance of the circuit.

• \(R_\text{op}\): an artificial or mathematical resistance associated with the op amp’s voltage and current noise.

Detailed notes

Noise sources in an op-amp circuit

The lecture begins with an amplifier noise analysis involving resistors and an op amp.

The identified sources include:

• Johnson noise from each resistor.

• Op-amp input voltage noise.

• Op-amp input current noise.

• Semiconductor-related noise, described in the lecture as shot noise.

The noise sources are treated as contributions at the input of the amplifier. The lecturer emphasises that noise is still a real voltage or current even though its value varies randomly.

For an inverting amplifier, the resistor network contributes noise through the effective resistance seen at the input. The feedback network and source resistance both affect the total input-referred noise.

Combining independent noise sources

The lecturer repeatedly stresses that statistically independent noise contributions are combined using the sum of their squares:

\[

x_\text{total} =

\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2+\cdots}

\]

The same principle applies to voltage-noise and current-noise contributions. The lecturer relates this to variance and standard deviation: mean-square quantities are added, and the square root is taken at the end.

The practical consequence is that a source that is much larger than the others can dominate the total noise. Very small contributions may be neglected when making an engineering approximation, but they should first be compared quantitatively.

Op-amp voltage and current noise

The op amp contributes two principal input-referred noise quantities:

• Voltage-noise density, \(e_n\), usually specified in \(\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\).

• Current-noise density, \(i_n\), usually specified in \(\mathrm{A}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\).

The current noise flows through a resistance and therefore produces an equivalent voltage-noise contribution. A simplified form is:

\[

e_{n,i} = i_n R

\]

For an effective resistance \(R_\text{eq}\), the corresponding mean-square contribution is proportional to:

\[

i_n^2 R_\text{eq}^2

\]

This explains why current noise becomes increasingly important as the source resistance increases.

Effect of source resistance

The source resistance is a major factor in selecting an op amp.

For a relatively small source resistance:

• The current-noise contribution is multiplied by a small resistance.

• Current noise may therefore be negligible.

• Voltage noise and resistor Johnson noise may dominate.

• An op amp with low voltage noise can be a good choice.

For a large source resistance:

• The current-noise term is multiplied by a large resistance.

• The resulting equivalent voltage noise can become dominant.

• An op amp with very low current noise, such as the AD549 example, may be preferred even if its voltage-noise density is not the lowest.

The lecturer gives an example in which the source resistance is increased to approximately \(1\ \mathrm{M\Omega}\). Under these conditions, an op amp with very low current noise becomes a much better choice than the low-voltage-noise device used for the lower-resistance example.

The central design principle is:

• High source resistance: prioritise low current noise.

• Low source resistance: prioritise low voltage noise, while also considering resistor noise and bandwidth.

Dominant noise in the worked example

In the first worked example, the lecturer states that the feedback resistors dominate the total noise.

This is useful diagnostically:

• Changing the op amp may have little effect if the resistor noise is already much larger.

• Selecting an op amp whose own noise is small compared with the resistor contribution is generally sufficient.

• There is no benefit in paying substantially more for a very low-noise op amp if fixed resistor noise dominates the circuit.

The best op amp can change when the source resistance changes. A device that is appropriate for a low-resistance source may be poor for a high-resistance source.

Wideband noise and noise density

The lecturer distinguishes between noise density and total noise.

Noise density describes noise per unit bandwidth. Total noise over a frequency range is obtained by integrating the noise power spectral density over that range.

For a constant white-noise density, the total mean-square noise increases with bandwidth:

\[

v_{n,\mathrm{rms}}^2

=

\int_{f_\mathrm{L}}^{f_\mathrm{H}}

e_n^2(f)\,df

\]

If \(e_n\) is constant over the band:

\[

v_{n,\mathrm{rms}}

=

e_n\sqrt{B}

\]

where \(B=f_\mathrm{H}-f_\mathrm{L}\).

The lecture describes cumulative wideband noise as increasing with frequency because the integrated quantity contains positive noise-power contributions. There is no negative noise power in this calculation.

Filtering unwanted noise

The practical response to wideband noise is to limit the bandwidth.

A low-pass filter:

• Passes the desired signal band.

• Attenuates higher-frequency noise.

• Reduces the integrated noise power.

• Can improve the signal-to-noise ratio.

The lecturer describes an example in which adding a filter halves the noise power. The exact filter response and numerical conditions are not stated clearly in the transcript.

For white noise, noise power is proportional to bandwidth:

\[

P_n \propto B

\]

More generally, the output noise depends on the noise spectral density and the squared magnitude response of the filter:

\[

v_{n,\mathrm{out}}^2

=

\int_0^\infty

S_n(f)\lvert H(f)\rvert^2\,df

\]

Analogue filtering and ADCs

The lecturer warns that high-frequency noise must be removed before analogue-to-digital conversion.

If an ADC samples a signal containing frequency components above the Nyquist frequency, those components can alias into the sampled band. The result can be distortion or interference that cannot be removed reliably after sampling.

The recommended signal chain is:

1. Analogue filtering at the receiver or circuit front end.

2. ADC sampling.

3. Additional digital filtering if required.

The lecturer particularly emphasises the need for analogue filtering in RF and other high-frequency applications. Digital filtering alone cannot undo aliasing that has already occurred.

Practical design trade-offs

Noise reduction involves trade-offs among:

• Op-amp price.

• Resistor values.

• Source resistance.

• Required bandwidth.

• Circuit complexity.

• Required signal precision.

• Manufacturing volume.

• Application requirements.

Medical applications may justify more expensive components because low noise and precision are especially important. In a cost-sensitive high-volume product, a slightly noisier but much cheaper design may be preferable if it meets the specification.

Op-amp selection guide

The lecturer describes an analogue-device op-amp selection graph.

The graph is described as a log-log plot involving:

• Equivalent circuit resistance.

• Johnson-noise level.

• Op-amp voltage-noise density.

• Op-amp current-noise density.

• A derived op-amp resistance \(R_\text{op}\).

The selection procedure is approximately:

1. Determine the circuit’s equivalent resistance, \(R_\text{eq}\).

2. Determine the resistor Johnson-noise contribution.

3. Use an op-amp data sheet or selection graph to identify \(e_n\).

4. Determine \(R_\text{op}=e_n/i_n\).

5. Select an op amp for which \(R_\text{op}\) is close to \(R_\text{eq}\), while also considering the shaded acceptable region and non-noise specifications.

The lecturer states that this ratio is a mathematical selection tool, not a claim that the op amp physically contains a resistor equal to \(R_\text{op}\).

Why matching \(R_\text{op}\) and \(R_\text{eq}\) works

The total input-referred voltage-noise contribution can be written schematically as:

\[

e_{n,\mathrm{total}}^2

\approx

e_n^2

+

i_n^2R_\text{eq}^2

+

e_{n,R}^2

\]

where:

• \(e_n\) is op-amp voltage noise.

• \(i_nR_\text{eq}\) is the voltage generated by op-amp current noise.

• \(e_{n,R}\) represents resistor-related noise.

Using:

\[

R_\text{op}=\frac{e_n}{i_n}

\]

gives:

\[

e_n=i_nR_\text{op}

\]

so the op-amp-dependent part becomes approximately:

\[

e_{n,\mathrm{op}}^2

=

i_n^2

\left(R_\text{op}^2+R_\text{eq}^2\right)

\]

The lecture then introduces an approximation involving the equivalent resistance and argues that the relevant ratio is minimised when:

\[

R_\text{op}\approx R_\text{eq}

\]

The lecturer also introduces a parameter \(\gamma\), described as a ratio between the two resistance quantities. The function to be minimised is described as having the form:

\[

f(\gamma)=\gamma+\frac{1}{\gamma}

\]

Its minimum occurs at:

\[

\gamma=1

\]

This corresponds to matching \(R_\text{op}\) and \(R_\text{eq}\).

The lecture states that the approximation may differ from exact real-world calculations by approximately 5% in representative cases, although the basis of that percentage is not shown in the transcript.

Interpreting the selection graph

The lecturer explains that moving through the graph can identify regions of acceptable op-amp choices.

• Moving horizontally changes the relationship between voltage noise and \(R_\text{op}\).

• Moving along a slope can preserve approximately the same current-noise contribution while reducing voltage noise.

• Devices in the shaded region are described as good choices.

• The exact axes, slopes, and shaded-region boundaries cannot be reconstructed reliably from the transcript alone.

The key takeaway is not to select an op amp solely because it has the smallest voltage-noise or current-noise specification. Instead, compare the op amp’s noise characteristics with the resistance environment of the circuit.

Equations and definitions

Johnson-noise voltage density

For a resistor \(R\) at absolute temperature \(T\), the voltage-noise spectral density is:

\[

e_{n,R}=\sqrt{4kTR}

\]

where:

• \(e_{n,R}\) is voltage-noise density in \(\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\).

• \(k\) is Boltzmann’s constant:

\[

k\approx1.38\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}

\]

• \(T\) is absolute temperature in kelvin.

• \(R\) is resistance in ohms.

The corresponding mean-square noise over bandwidth \(B\) is:

\[

v_{n,R}^2=4kTRB

\]

and the RMS noise voltage is:

\[

v_{n,R}=\sqrt{4kTRB}

\]

The transcript repeatedly refers to the term \(4kT\), but some exact displayed equations are not audible.

Op-amp current-noise contribution

The current-noise voltage generated across an effective resistance is:

\[

e_{n,i}=i_nR

\]

For the circuit’s effective resistance:

\[

e_{n,i}=i_nR_\text{eq}

\]

The corresponding mean-square contribution is:

\[

e_{n,i}^2=i_n^2R_\text{eq}^2

\]

Root-sum-square combination

For uncorrelated noise sources:

\[

e_{n,\mathrm{total}}

=

\sqrt{

e_{n1}^2+

e_{n2}^2+

e_{n3}^2+\cdots

}

\]

Equivalently, the mean-square noise powers are added first.

Equivalent resistance

The lecture defines the circuit equivalent resistance approximately as the combination of the source resistance and the feedback-network resistance:

\[

R_\text{eq}

\approx

R_\text{source}

+

R_\text{feedback, effective}

\]

The exact topology-dependent expression should be taken from the circuit being analysed. In several places, the transcript refers to a parallel resistance from the feedback network plus the source resistance.

Op-amp equivalent resistance

The mathematical op-amp resistance is:

\[

R_\text{op}=\frac{e_n}{i_n}

\]

This is not a physical resistor inside the op amp. It is a derived quantity used to compare the op amp’s voltage-noise and current-noise specifications.

Approximate optimum

The lecture’s selection rule is:

\[

R_\text{op}\approx R_\text{eq}

\]

This follows from minimising a function described as:

\[

f(\gamma)=\gamma+\frac{1}{\gamma}

\]

with:

\[

\gamma=\frac{R_\text{op}}{R_\text{eq}}

\]

The minimum occurs at:

\[

\gamma=1

\]

Wideband noise

For a frequency-dependent noise spectral density:

\[

v_{n,\mathrm{rms}}^2

=

\int_{f_\mathrm{L}}^{f_\mathrm{H}}

S_v(f)\,df

\]

For a white voltage-noise density \(e_n\):

\[

v_{n,\mathrm{rms}}=e_n\sqrt{B}

\]

where \(B=f_\mathrm{H}-f_\mathrm{L}\).

With a filter transfer function \(H(f)\):

\[

v_{n,\mathrm{out}}^2

=

\int_0^\infty

S_v(f)\lvert H(f)\rvert^2\,df

\]

Noise figure

Noise figure is a measure of the degradation in signal-to-noise ratio caused by a circuit or device. In linear form:

\[

F=

\frac{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{in}}}

{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{out}}}

\]

In decibels:

\[

NF=10\log_{10}(F)

\]

The lecture gives an example result of approximately \(2.85\ \mathrm{dB}\), but the exact formula and circuit values are not fully recoverable from the automated transcript.

ADC sampling requirement

To avoid aliasing, unwanted analogue frequency components should be attenuated before sampling. The basic Nyquist condition is:

\[

f_\mathrm{sample}>2f_\mathrm{max}

\]

for the highest frequency component that is allowed to reach the ADC. In practice, an analogue anti-aliasing filter is used because real filters have finite roll-off.
