Key concepts

• A full-bridge rectifier converts both half-cycles of an AC waveform into the same polarity at the output.

• In each conducting half-cycle, two diodes conduct while the other two are reverse-biased.

• With a simplified forward drop of approximately 0.8 V per diode, the conducting path has an approximate total drop of 1.6 V.

• For a 12 V input, the real output peak may therefore be approximately 10.4 V under this simplified model.

• For high-voltage systems such as a 230 V supply, the same diode drop is a much smaller relative error. At low voltage, it can significantly affect the available output voltage.

• A full-wave rectifier produces twice as many output pulses per input period as a half-wave rectifier.

• More pulses per period increase the effective ripple frequency and make filtering easier.

• A capacitively smoothed output is treated mathematically as an approximately constant DC voltage when the capacitance is sufficiently large.

• An inductor opposes rapid changes in current and can maintain current flow even when the rectified source voltage falls.

• A large capacitor can produce a nearly constant output voltage, but the corresponding AC-side current may become highly non-sinusoidal.

• Non-sinusoidal input current contains harmonics that can adversely affect the supply grid.

• Total harmonic distortion quantifies the harmonic content relative to the fundamental component.

• Total power factor accounts for both phase displacement and waveform distortion.

• LTSpice FFT results must be interpreted carefully because the displayed values are RMS quantities, not necessarily sinusoidal amplitudes.

• FFT analysis should use a steady-state, periodic section of the waveform rather than a transient or a non-integer number of cycles.

• Three-phase rectifiers generally produce lower THD than comparable single-phase rectifiers, according to the qualitative trend discussed.

• Harmonic standards specify separate maximum permissible currents for different harmonic orders. Passing one harmonic, such as the third, does not imply that all higher harmonics pass.

Detailed notes

Full-bridge rectifier operation

The lecture begins with an AC source, illustrated as a 12 V waveform, connected to a rectifier. The output of an ideal full bridge is the absolute-value-like version of the input waveform: both positive and negative input half-cycles produce output voltage with the same polarity.

For one polarity of source voltage, diodes D1 and D2 conduct while D3 and D4 are reverse-biased. During the opposite polarity, the conducting and reverse-biased diode pairs swap by symmetry. The load current therefore retains the same direction even though the source voltage reverses.

The lecturer explains the conduction threshold using a practical diode model. If two diodes are in the conducting path and each has an approximately 0.8 V drop, current begins to flow only when the source voltage magnitude exceeds approximately 1.6 V. Between −1.6 V and +1.6 V, the simplified model predicts zero current. At exactly zero source voltage, all circuit voltages and currents in the illustrated simplified case are treated as zero.

Effect of diode drops

Full-wave versus half-wave rectification

A half-wave rectifier passes only one half-cycle and produces zero output during the other half-cycle. A full-wave bridge uses both half-cycles, producing twice as many output pulses per period.

The lecturer identifies two consequences:

1. The average DC output is higher for the full-wave case than for the half-wave case under comparable conditions.

2. The ripple frequency is higher for the full-wave case, so the output is easier to filter.

The lecture gives a rectification-effectiveness value of approximately 0.81 for the full-bridge case being discussed and identifies it as substantially higher than the half-wave value. The lecturer also gives a form-factor-related value of approximately 1.11 in the rectifier discussion.

The waveform from a half-wave rectifier is described as particularly unlike clean DC because it repeatedly falls to zero. Such a waveform is more difficult to filter than a waveform with more frequent pulses.

Capacitive smoothing and constant-voltage load

A capacitively smoothed rectifier uses a capacitor to reduce voltage ripple. The capacitor supplies current to the load during portions of the cycle when the rectified source is not providing sufficient current. In the lecture discussion, the capacitor acts as a temporary current source and fills in the troughs of the rectified waveform.

The idealised mathematical case assumes a very large, effectively infinite capacitance. Under this assumption:

• The output voltage is treated as a straight, constant DC line.

• The load is treated as a constant-voltage load.

• Voltage ripple caused by the rectified source is neglected.

The lecturer notes that a truly infinite capacitor does not exist physically. A sufficiently large real capacitor may nevertheless provide an approximation that is adequate for a mathematical model. A value around 1 mF is mentioned informally as an example of a relatively large capacitor, but the transcript does not specify the circuit voltage, load, frequency, or ripple requirement needed to determine whether that value is actually sufficient.

The ideal constant-voltage approximation does not imply that every possible circuit ripple disappears. Other circuit effects may still create current ripple or other non-ideal behaviour.

Inductively smoothed current

Adding an inductor changes the current waveform. An inductor resists abrupt changes in current and tends to maintain current flow when the source voltage would otherwise cause the current to stop.

The lecturer describes the inductor as continuing to supply current after the rectified source voltage has fallen below the level that would normally sustain conduction. This can produce a current waveform that is continuous or nearly continuous on the DC side, depending on the circuit and inductance.

However, the current drawn from the AC supply can become highly non-sinusoidal. The lecturer warns that this type of current waveform would be undesirable if widely replicated across many homes because it would inject harmonics into the grid.

The resulting concern is not only whether the rectifier delivers useful DC power, but also how much distorted current it draws from the AC supply. Total harmonic distortion provides a numerical measure of this distortion.

Harmonics and THD

A distorted periodic current can be represented as a fundamental component plus harmonic components. The RMS current includes the contributions of all harmonics.

In principle, the complete RMS value requires the sum of the squared RMS values of the fundamental and every harmonic up to arbitrarily high order. In practical calculations, the harmonic series is truncated, or an FFT is used to estimate the relevant components.

Phase displacement and supply impact

If the voltage and current waveforms are out of phase, their product produces less real power for a given RMS current than if they were aligned. The supply authority must still provide the current required by the load, so poor phase factor can require higher current and larger conductors for the same useful power transfer.

The lecturer identifies practical consequences:

• Increased current for the same real power.

• Larger required conductors.

• Higher infrastructure cost.

• Greater concern when a rectifier design is deployed widely rather than used as an isolated experiment.

The lecturer states that phase may not have been directly examined previously, but it remains an important design consideration, particularly for commercial products.

The standard provides:

The lecturer identifies the Class A limits as the main focus in the discussion and states that the associated homework question is examinable because homework content is examinable.

A design must satisfy the applicable limit for every relevant harmonic order. Meeting the limit for the third harmonic does not automatically guarantee compliance for higher odd harmonics such as the fifth, seventh, or ninth. Each specified harmonic must be checked individually.

Compensation may therefore require capacitive elements, depending on the overall design objective.
