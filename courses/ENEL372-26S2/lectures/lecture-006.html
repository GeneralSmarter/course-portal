Overview

This lecture completes practical design considerations for the buck converter, focusing on MOSFET gate driving, low-side switching, driver current requirements, and the use of CMOS inverters. It then introduces the boost converter and derives its ideal steady-state voltage-conversion relationship.

Main topics:

• MOSFET gate-source voltage and on-state resistance.

• Gate capacitance, gate-drive current, and switching speed.

• High-side versus low-side MOSFET placement.

• Driving a low-side MOSFET using a TL494 controller.

• BJT and CMOS gate-driver configurations.

• PWM inversion and correcting the logic polarity.

• Introduction to boost-converter operation.

• Ideal steady-state analysis of the boost converter.

• Derivation of:

\[

V_{\text{out}}=\frac{V_S}{1-D}

\]

The lecture relates the MOSFET-driver discussion to the solar-car project and indicates that the boost-converter design calculations will continue in the next lecture.

Key concepts

• Power buck converters commonly use enhancement-mode N-channel MOSFETs as controllable switches.

• An enhancement-mode MOSFET is off when \(V_{GS}=0\), and turns on when \(V_{GS}\) is sufficiently positive.

• A sufficiently large \(V_{GS}\) is required to obtain a low \(R_{DS(\text{on})}\).

• The MOSFET gate behaves approximately as a capacitance to the gate-drive circuit.

• Fast switching requires high positive and negative pulse currents to charge and discharge the gate capacitance.

• The gate driver must have low output impedance.

• A series gate resistor limits peak gate current and reduces the risk of excessive current.

• A large gate-to-source pull-down resistor ensures that the MOSFET remains off when the driver has no valid input signal.

• A high-side N-channel MOSFET requires a gate voltage approximately 10 V above its source voltage for full enhancement. This can require a separate or isolated supply.

• Moving the MOSFET to the low side allows the source to remain near the panel negative/reference, simplifying gate drive.

• The TL494 provides PWM generation, a reference voltage, error amplifiers, and output BJT stages, but its output stages may not provide sufficient current for fast direct MOSFET switching.

• A common-collector BJT stage is non-inverting and can increase available current, but its turn-off path may be slow.

• The boost converter stores energy in an inductor while the switch is closed, then transfers that energy to the output when the switch is open.

• In ideal steady-state operation, the average inductor voltage is zero.

• The average capacitor current is zero.

Detailed notes

MOSFET gate-drive requirements

The lecture begins by revisiting buck-converter design. Earlier design considerations included:

• Inductance.

• Output-filter capacitance.

• Input-filter capacitance.

• Switching frequency.

A further major consideration is the controllable MOSFET switch.

Power electronics commonly uses enhancement-mode N-channel MOSFETs. The relevant terminals are:

• Gate: control/input terminal.

• Drain.

• Source.

When the MOSFET is on, conventional current is described as flowing from drain to source. The MOSFET must be driven with a sufficiently large gate-to-source voltage:

\[

V_{GS}=V_G-V_S

\]

The objective is to turn the MOSFET fully on, producing a low drain-to-source on-state resistance, \(R_{DS(\text{on})}\). The lecture states that modern MOSFETs may have \(R_{DS(\text{on})}\) in the range of tens of milliohms or less, although the actual value depends on the device and specified gate voltage.

Gate capacitance and transient current

The gate-to-source connection behaves approximately as a capacitance, referred to in the lecture as \(C_{GS}\). A PWM signal must charge and discharge this effective gate capacitance rapidly.

The gate-drive circuit includes:

• A small series gate resistor, labelled \(R_G\), typically around 10 \(\Omega\) according to the lecture.

• A large resistor from gate to source, apparently labelled \(R_D\) or a similar designation in the ASR. Its exact label should be checked against the circuit diagram.

The series gate resistor limits the peak current used to charge or discharge the gate capacitance. The large gate-to-source resistor provides a defined off-state when there is no input signal. The lecture indicates that this resistor may be around \(1\,\text{M}\Omega\), though the exact design value depends on the circuit.

When a positive PWM pulse is applied:

1. The gate capacitance begins charging.

2. \(V_{GS}\) rises approximately exponentially according to the relevant RC time constant.

3. A large positive gate current initially flows.

4. The gate current falls toward zero as the gate approaches its final voltage.

When the MOSFET is turned off:

1. The gate capacitance must discharge.

2. A negative gate current pulse may occur if the driver actively pulls the gate down.

3. The discharge current can also have a large peak magnitude.

4. Fast switching requires both charging and discharging to occur rapidly.

For the solar-car project, the lecture estimates peak gate-drive currents of approximately 1–2 A. These are pulse currents rather than average currents. The average gate current can be much smaller, but the driver must tolerate the short-duration peaks.

Therefore, the gate driver must:

• Source high pulse current while turning the MOSFET on.

• Sink high pulse current while turning the MOSFET off.

• Have low output impedance.

• Limit the absolute peak current with an appropriate gate resistor.

High-side and low-side MOSFET placement

In the buck-converter arrangement considered in the lecture, the controllable MOSFET is initially positioned on the high side. One terminal is connected toward the positive supply.

For an N-channel MOSFET to turn on fully, the gate must be approximately 10 V above the source. In a high-side configuration, the source rises close to the input voltage when the MOSFET is on. If the solar panel provides approximately 15–17 V, the gate may need to be approximately 25–27 V relative to the panel negative in order to maintain \(V_{GS}\approx 10\text{ V}\).

The solar-panel voltage source alone does not provide this additional gate voltage. A high-side configuration would therefore require more complicated circuitry, such as:

• A separate isolated DC supply.

• A bootstrap or other specialised high-side gate-drive arrangement, although this specific option was not explicitly developed in the transcript.

The lecture then uses properties of the application to justify moving the MOSFET to the low side:

• The DC motor does not require an earth-referenced common connection to the solar-panel negative.

• In a series circuit, the same current flows through the series-connected components.

• The MOSFET can therefore be moved from the high-side position to the low-side position without changing the series current path.

With a low-side N-channel MOSFET:

• The source is referenced close to the panel negative.

• A gate voltage of approximately 10 V above the source can be generated directly from the available panel/control supply.

• No separate isolated 10 V supply is required for the basic gate-drive arrangement.

• The output load can have a different effective negative/reference relationship from the input source, provided the application permits this.

The lecture stresses that this is suitable for the motor load in the project, but the appropriateness of moving a switch to the low side depends on the grounding, reference, and load-interface requirements of the complete system.

TL494 controller

The TL494 is introduced as the controller being considered for the project. The lecture refers to its functional blocks, including:

• PWM output stages.

• Timing-resistor and timing-capacitor pins, referred to as \(R_T\) and \(C_T\), which set the switching frequency.

• An internal sawtooth oscillator.

• A reference-voltage output.

• Error amplifiers for feedback control.

The switching frequency is selected by choosing appropriate values for \(R_T\) and \(C_T\). Example frequencies mentioned include approximately 50 kHz and 90 kHz. The exact relationship between the timing components and frequency was not stated in the transcript.

The TL494 reference output can be used to establish a control-system reference. The lecture describes using a scaled representation of the solar panel’s desired maximum-power-point voltage. The measured panel voltage is compared with this ordered/reference value through an error amplifier.

The error-amplifier section is associated with feedback control. The lecturer refers to “PIA constants” in the ASR, which is uncertain and may refer to PI or PID-related controller parameters. The intended function is to determine how far the measured output is from the commanded or ordered value and use that error to influence PWM duty ratio.

The TL494 contains two error amplifiers, although the project may use only one.

TL494 BJT output stage

The TL494 PWM outputs use low-power BJT stages. The lecture states that the maximum output current is approximately 200 mA, including under pulse conditions.

The MOSFET gate is represented as an effective capacitance at the output of the controller. A BJT stage can provide current amplification, but the basic arrangement discussed has limitations.

A common-collector BJT amplifier:

• Has the collector connected directly to \(V_{CC}\) or the panel supply.

• Takes its output from the emitter.

• Is non-inverting with respect to phase.

• Provides increased output-current capability.

However, the output current remains limited by the controller/output-stage rating and the selected resistors.

During turn-on, the BJT charges the MOSFET gate capacitance. The lecture notes that a current limited to approximately 200 mA may be too small for the desired fast turn-on.

During turn-off, the PWM transistor turns off and becomes high impedance. A separate resistor is then required to discharge the MOSFET gate capacitance to ground. This discharge path may include two series-connected resistors, making turn-off relatively slow. The circuit would function, but it would not be an ideal fast gate driver.

CMOS inverter gate driver

A CMOS inverter is proposed as a better gate driver.

A CMOS inverter contains:

• A PMOS device that turns on when the input is low.

• An NMOS device that turns on when the input is high.

In either stable logic state, one transistor is strongly on and the other is off. The output impedance is therefore low in both states. This allows the inverter to:

• Source a high pulse current to charge the MOSFET gate.

• Sink a high pulse current to discharge the MOSFET gate.

• Switch the MOSFET on and off more rapidly.

• Reduce switching losses caused by spending less time in the MOSFET’s transition region.

The gate resistor is still required, approximately 10 \(\Omega\) according to the lecture, to limit peak current. A large gate-to-source resistor is also retained as a safety feature to ensure that the MOSFET remains off if the input signal is absent.

PWM inversion and polarity

A CMOS inverter reverses the input logic. Consequently:

• A high PWM input produces a low output.

• A low PWM input produces a high output.

• The effective on/off duty relationship is inverted.

This is problematic when the PWM signal is part of a feedback-control system, because the control loop may require increasing the command to increase the converter’s effective duty ratio.

The lecture proposes adding another inversion. The TL494 BJT output can be configured as a common-emitter amplifier rather than a common-collector amplifier:

• The emitter is connected to ground.

• A collector resistor is connected to the panel supply.

• The output is taken from the collector.

• The common-emitter stage produces an inverted PWM signal.

This inversion can compensate for the inversion introduced by the CMOS inverter, so that the signal reaching the MOSFET gate has the required non-inverting effective PWM relationship.

The project specifically requires the use of a CMOS inverter for gate drive. The lecturer instructs students to configure the TL494 output stage and inverter so that the final MOSFET-gate PWM polarity is correct.

Boost-converter introduction

The second major topic is the boost converter.

The boost converter contains elements similar to the buck converter:

• Power inductor.

• Diode.

• Filter capacitor.

• Resistive load.

• Controllable switch.

The main difference is the arrangement of these components. This rearrangement allows the converter to produce an output voltage higher than the source voltage.

A boost converter is therefore a step-up converter. The lecture contrasts this with the buck converter, which is a step-down converter.

The boost converter’s voltage-boosting behaviour depends on the inductor:

1. The inductor receives and stores energy while the switch is closed.

2. When the switch opens, the inductor attempts to maintain current in the same direction.

3. Its voltage reverses as required to maintain current continuity.

4. The inductor voltage adds to the source voltage at the output.

5. The output voltage can therefore exceed the input voltage.

Ideal steady-state assumptions

The initial boost-converter analysis uses the same steady-state assumptions previously used for the buck converter:

• Inductor-current waveform fluctuations are periodic.

• The average voltage across the inductor over one switching period is zero.

• The average current through the capacitor is zero.

• Input power equals output power when losses are neglected.

• The converter is initially treated as 100% efficient.

• The output-filter capacitor is sufficiently large that output-voltage ripple can initially be neglected.

Let:

• \(V_S\) be the input/source voltage.

• \(V_{\text{out}}\) be the output voltage.

• \(D\) be the switch duty ratio.

• \(T\) be the switching period.

• \(L\) be the inductor value.

Boost converter with switch closed

When the switch is closed:

• The diode is reverse-biased and behaves approximately as an open circuit.

• The inductor is directly connected across the source.

• The inductor voltage is approximately \(V_S\).

• The inductor receives energy from the source.

• The inductor current ramps upward linearly.

Using the inductor relationship:

\[

v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}

\]

the current slope is:

\[

\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{V_S}{L}

\]

If the switch remains closed for \(DT\), the increase in inductor current is:

\[

\Delta i_{L,\text{on}}=\frac{V_SDT}{L}

\]

Boost converter with switch open

When the switch opens:

• The switch path is open-circuit.

• The inductor current cannot instantly stop.

• The inductor reverses its voltage polarity to maintain current in the same direction.

• The diode becomes forward-biased.

• The inductor releases stored energy to the output.

• The inductor voltage is approximately \(V_S-V_{\text{out}}\).

Since a boost converter has \(V_{\text{out}}>V_S\), this voltage is negative under the stated polarity convention:

\[

v_L=V_S-V_{\text{out}}<0

\]

Therefore:

\[

\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{V_S-V_{\text{out}}}{L}

\]

The inductor current ramps downward during the switch-off interval, which lasts \((1-D)T\).

The magnitude of the current decrease is:

\[

\left|\Delta i_{L,\text{off}}\right|

=

\frac{(V_{\text{out}}-V_S)(1-D)T}{L}

\]

The transcript expresses this using the signed voltage change; the polarity convention should be kept consistent when using the equation.

Volt-second balance and boost ratio

In steady state, the average voltage across the inductor is zero. Equivalently, the positive and negative volt-seconds applied to the inductor over one switching period must cancel:

\[

V_SD T+(V_S-V_{\text{out}})(1-D)T=0

\]

Dividing by \(T\):

\[

V_SD+(V_S-V_{\text{out}})(1-D)=0

\]

Expanding and simplifying:

\[

V_S=V_{\text{out}}(1-D)

\]

Therefore:

\[

V_{\text{out}}=\frac{V_S}{1-D}

\]

For:

\[

0\leq D<1

\]

the ideal boost-converter output is at least equal to the input voltage, and for \(D>0\) it is greater than the input voltage.

As \(D\) approaches 1, the ideal equation predicts a very large output voltage. In a practical converter, losses, component ratings, control limits, discontinuous conduction, and other non-ideal effects limit the achievable output.

Equations and definitions

MOSFET gate-source voltage

\[

V_{GS}=V_G-V_S

\]

• \(V_{GS}\): gate-to-source voltage.

• \(V_G\): gate voltage.

• \(V_S\): source voltage.

An enhancement-mode N-channel MOSFET is off at approximately \(V_{GS}=0\) and turns on when \(V_{GS}\) is sufficiently positive.

MOSFET on-state resistance

\[

R_{DS(\text{on})}

\]

The effective drain-to-source resistance when the MOSFET is fully enhanced. A low value reduces conduction loss:

\[

P_{\text{cond}}\approx I_D^2R_{DS(\text{on})}

\]

The power-loss expression was not explicitly stated in the transcript but follows from the described resistance behaviour.

Gate RC time constant

For a simplified gate-drive model:

\[

\tau=R_{\text{drive}}C_{\text{gate}}

\]

The transcript specifically refers to the gate-to-source capacitance \(C_{GS}\), but practical MOSFET gate drive is normally determined by the device’s effective total gate charge and capacitances, not only \(C_{GS}\). Check the course model and datasheet treatment before using this simplification in design work.

Inductor voltage-current relationship

\[

v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}

\]

• \(v_L\): inductor voltage.

• \(L\): inductance.

• \(i_L\): inductor current.

For approximately constant inductor voltage over an interval:

\[

\Delta i_L=\frac{v_L\Delta t}{L}

\]

Boost converter, switch closed

\[

v_{L,\text{on}}=V_S

\]

\[

\Delta i_{L,\text{on}}=\frac{V_SD T}{L}

\]

The inductor current ramps upward.

Boost converter, switch open

\[

v_{L,\text{off}}=V_S-V_{\text{out}}

\]

\[

\Delta i_{L,\text{off}}

=

\frac{(V_S-V_{\text{out}})(1-D)T}{L}

\]

This is a signed quantity and is negative for an ideal boost converter operating with \(V_{\text{out}}>V_S\).

Inductor volt-second balance

\[

\overline{v_L}=0

\]

For the boost converter:

\[

V_SD T+(V_S-V_{\text{out}})(1-D)T=0

\]

Ideal boost-converter voltage ratio

\[

V_{\text{out}}=\frac{V_S}{1-D}

\]

• \(D\): switch duty ratio, \(0\leq D<1\).

• \(D=0\): ideal output equals input.

• \(D>0\): ideal output exceeds input.

• Increasing \(D\) increases the ideal output voltage.

Ideal power balance

\[

P_{\text{in}}\approx P_{\text{out}}

\]

The lecture initially assumes zero losses and therefore 100% efficiency:

\[

V_SI_S=V_{\text{out}}I_{\text{out}}

\]

The exact current relationship was not derived in the lecture transcript.
