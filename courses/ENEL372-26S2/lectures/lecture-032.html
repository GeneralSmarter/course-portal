Detailed notes

Continuous systems and digital processing

The lecturer began by contrasting analogue and digital approaches to electronics.

The real world is treated as continuous because physical quantities such as sensor outputs, voltages, currents, motor positions, and environmental signals vary continuously. Digital systems are useful because computers can process digital codes efficiently, but this requires conversion between domains.

A typical system therefore contains:

1. A physical, continuous input signal.

2. An ADC that samples and quantises the signal.

3. Digital processing.

4. A DAC that reconstructs a continuous output.

5. An actuator or other physical process.

The output is normally continuous because it must act on the real world, for example by moving a motor or controlling another physical system.

The lecturer described analogue electronics as useful because it can operate directly on continuous signals. Analogue circuits can also condition signals before conversion, including filtering or reducing their frequency content.

ADC resolution and quantisation

When a continuous signal is converted to a digital representation, its amplitude cannot generally be represented with infinite precision. The available amplitude range is divided into discrete levels. This process is quantisation.

Quantisation causes information loss because the exact analogue amplitude is replaced by the nearest available digital level. The transcript specifically distinguished this amplitude-resolution issue from the separate time-resolution issue caused by sampling.

Increasing the number of ADC bits improves amplitude resolution, but it does not eliminate the need to consider sampling rate, bandwidth, delay, storage, or processing limits.

Sampling rate and delay

Sampling a signal at discrete time intervals creates an unavoidable time interval between measurements. The lecturer described this as a delay. In a sensing system, this contributes to sensor delay. In an actuator path, conversion and processing can contribute additional delay before the command reaches the actuator.

The system may also behave as though the sampled signal is held constant between samples. This sample-and-hold behaviour contributes to the effective timing characteristics of the signal path.

The lecturer noted that ADC technology is becoming faster, but very high sampling rates remain expensive and generate large data volumes. Sampling at extremely high frequencies can quickly produce impractical storage requirements.

For this reason, it is often advantageous to reduce the signal bandwidth before conversion. Analogue electronics can perform this frequency reduction before the ADC, reducing:

• ADC speed requirements

• Data storage requirements

• Processing requirements

• System cost

Aliasing

The lecturer illustrated aliasing using a sine wave. If the sampling rate is insufficient relative to the signal frequency, the sampled points can appear to describe a much lower-frequency sine wave than the original signal.

The result is a signal artefact: the digital system interprets a high-frequency component as a different, lower-frequency component.

Aliasing cannot be corrected after the ADC if the original frequency information has already been lost. The lecturer therefore emphasised the use of an analogue anti-aliasing filter before the ADC.

An analogue anti-aliasing filter removes or attenuates frequency components that would otherwise be undersampled. A digital filter placed after the ADC is too late to prevent the ADC from misinterpreting those components.

Ideal and real components

The lecturer distinguished between ideal component models and real physical components.

An ideal component behaves exactly according to its mathematical description. Examples include:

• An ideal resistor with exactly the specified resistance

• An ideal capacitor with exactly the specified capacitance

• An ideal inductor with exactly the specified inductance

• An ideal diode following its assumed ideal characteristic

Real components can be measured and used in physical systems, but their behaviour includes effects not represented in the simplest ideal model.

Real circuit behaviour depends not only on the discrete components but also on:

• PCB geometry

• Track lengths

• Loops of conductor

• Component packaging

• Nearby conductors

• Unintended capacitance

• Unintended inductance

• Noise coupling

• Component tolerances

• Operating frequency

A conductor loop has inductive behaviour. Similarly, physical spacing and geometry can produce capacitance. These parasitics can become important as frequency increases.

Decoupling capacitors are used on circuit boards to reduce noise and help manage unwanted inductive effects in power and signal paths.

Frequency dependence of real components

At low frequencies, simple ideal models are often sufficiently accurate. As frequency increases, the parasitic properties become more significant.

The lecturer stated that at RF and even high-megahertz frequencies, a resistor, capacitor, or conductor may no longer behave as expected from its nominal label. A practical component can be represented as a combination of ideal elements.

The equivalent circuit is selected according to:

• Component type

• Component construction

• Operating frequency

• Required model accuracy

• Measurement or simulation purpose

At higher frequencies, a more complicated equivalent circuit may be required because more parasitic modes and resonances become significant.

Noise as a circuit-design consideration

The lecture then moved to noise.

Noise can significantly affect analogue circuits and image-processing systems. Although the instantaneous waveform of noise is random, its statistical properties can often be predicted and used in design.

The lecturer distinguished noise from deterministic disturbances such as AC hum:

• Random noise is described statistically.

• A deterministic disturbance has a predictable waveform or frequency.

• AC hum may often be modelled from its known frequency and amplitude.

The statistical properties of random noise allow engineers to estimate its effect and design circuits accordingly.

When selecting components, the designer should consider how much noise each component contributes. Noise should be incorporated into the circuit design rather than treated only as a problem discovered during testing.

White noise

White noise was described as a random signal with approximately uniform spectral amplitude across the frequency band of interest.

In the frequency domain, the Fourier transform of ideal white noise has approximately equal amplitude at each frequency. This makes white noise useful as a modelling input.

A transfer function can shape white noise to produce a different noise spectrum. For example:

• White noise can be applied to a low-pass shaping filter.

• The output then has less high-frequency content.

Signal-to-noise ratio

Signal-to-noise ratio compares the strength of a desired signal with the strength of unwanted noise.

For voltage quantities, the lecture described the ratio in terms of signal voltage and noise voltage. Because noise calculations are based on power or mean-square quantities, the squared voltage ratio is important.

The decibel form can be written as:

SNR(dB) = 20 log10(Vsignal,rms / Vnoise,rms)

Equivalently:

SNR(dB) = 10 log10(Vsignal,rms² / Vnoise,rms²)

A higher SNR indicates a stronger desired signal relative to noise.

The same concept can be applied to current quantities using signal current and noise current.

RMS noise values depend on bandwidth. Therefore, an SNR calculation using RMS noise must specify the operating or measurement bandwidth.

Filters can improve SNR by rejecting frequencies that contain unwanted noise. Possible filters mentioned included:

• Band-pass filters

• Notch filters

• Filters targeting a single interfering frequency

• Filters rejecting a broader band of noise

Referring noise to the input

Noise is conventionally referred to the input of an amplifier.

For a non-inverting amplifier, an input-referred noise quantity can be propagated to the output using the amplifier's gain. This provides a common basis for comparing:

• Op-amp voltage noise

• Op-amp current noise converted through source impedance

• Thermal noise from external resistors

• Other input-referred sources

The lecture described analysing separate noise contributions by setting other noise sources to zero. For example, to study resistor noise, the op-amp's equivalent input voltage and current noise sources can be set to zero temporarily.

This allows each contribution to be calculated independently before combining the results.

Independent noise sources are combined using their variances or mean-square values.

Vtotal,rms = √(V1,rms² + V2,rms² + V3,rms² + ...)

For uncorrelated noise voltages:

Vtotal,rms² = ΣVi,rms²

Therefore:

Y(s) = H(s)W(s)

• W(s) represents white-noise input

• H(s) is a shaping transfer function
