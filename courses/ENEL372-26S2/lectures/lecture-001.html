Core concepts and relationships

Power conversion by switching

A converter changes the electrical form supplied by a source into the form required by a load. Efficient power-electronic conversion uses semiconductor switches rather than continuously dissipative control elements. Ideally:

• an open/off switch carries no current, so its power dissipation is near zero;

• a closed/on switch has near-zero voltage across it, so its power dissipation is also near zero.

Real devices have conduction and switching losses, but a well-designed modern converter may keep total losses to roughly 1-2%, as stated in the lecture. Linear regulators can sometimes approach about 80% efficiency, but often perform considerably worse and cannot provide every conversion function available through switched energy storage.

The lecture's general power balance was:

P_input = P_output + P_loss

with inductors and capacitors treated as ideally lossless storage/filter elements in the introductory model.

Feedback and regulation

Source voltage and load demand can vary. A practical converter therefore measures an output quantity, compares it with a reference, and uses the resulting control action to alter the switching converter. This feedback loop regulates the desired output despite disturbances. The group project is not just a converter build; it is a controlled converter with feedback.

Main conversion categories

• AC to DC: rectification; the converter is a rectifier. It may be diode-based or controlled.

• DC to AC: inversion; the converter is an inverter.

• DC to DC: changes DC voltage/current levels. Some isolated implementations use an intermediate AC stage before returning to DC.

• AC to AC: may be implemented through multiple conversion stages depending on the required output.

The lecture also used electric-vehicle drives and multilevel inverters as application examples.

Semiconductor-device comparison

Diode

• Conducts from anode to cathode once forward biased.

• A silicon diode was described as beginning conduction at roughly 0.7 V forward bias.

• The introductory ideal model treats it as an open circuit when off and a short circuit when on.

Enhancement MOSFET

• A voltage-controlled device with an unavoidable body diode in its construction.

• V_GS = 0 corresponds to the off state in the discussed N-channel example.

• Typical power-MOSFET threshold voltage was stated as roughly 2-3 V, but conventional power MOSFETs are commonly driven around 10 V so they operate well into the ohmic/on region.

• Very fast switching makes MOSFETs attractive at high switching frequency.

• The ideal on-state model uses R_DS(on) ≈ 0, while real devices retain conduction loss.

BJT

• A current-controlled device: base current controls collector current.

• Slower than a MOSFET, but the lecture noted favourable high-current conduction behaviour in some operating regions.

• Device choice is therefore a trade-off between switching speed and current capability/loss.

IGBT

• Combines a MOSFET-like insulated-gate input with a BJT-like output.

• Offers voltage-controlled drive, relatively fast switching, and useful high-current efficiency.

• It sits between MOSFET and BJT characteristics rather than being the universal best option.

Thyristor family

• Used for very high voltage/current applications where MOSFETs, BJTs, and IGBTs may be insufficient.

• An SCR behaves like a forward-biased diode that begins conducting only after a gate pulse. Once latched on, the gate cannot turn it off; current must fall to zero and the device must become reverse biased.

• A GTO adds controlled turn-off but requires a substantial gate-current pulse.

• A triac provides bidirectional thyristor behaviour.

• These devices switch slowly and are suited to low-frequency, often mains-frequency, applications rather than high-frequency conversion.

Design trade-offs to remember

• Higher switching frequency can reduce passive-component size and improve control bandwidth, but raises switching-loss demands and narrows the suitable device set.

• MOSFETs dominate the high-frequency end; IGBTs and then thyristor-family devices extend toward higher current/voltage with lower practical switching frequency.

• Device selection must consider voltage, current, switching frequency, drive requirements, conduction loss, switching loss, and whether controlled turn-off is needed.
