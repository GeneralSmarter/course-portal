Detailed notes

Three-phase inverter and commutation

The motor has three phases, labelled A, B, and C. The inverter provides multiple possible switching combinations to direct current through the windings.

In normal BLDC operation:

• Two phases conduct current.

• The third phase is left floating.

• The conducting phases create the stator magnetic field.

• The rotor position determines when the inverter must change to the next switching combination.

• The sequence of combinations creates a rotating magnetic field and maintains motor rotation.

The lecture describes a split DC-link capacitor arrangement. The capacitors help reduce voltage ripple. The inverter switches determine how current enters one phase and leaves another.

The lecturer demonstrates the idea using switch pairs:

• T1 and T4:

• Current enters phase A, making A positive under the stated convention.

• Current exits through phase C.

• Phase B is floating and has zero intended current.

• The phase-current signs depend on the direction arrows in the circuit diagram.

• T1 and T6:

• T1 remains closed from the previous state.

• T6 is introduced for the next phase.

• The transcript identifies the resulting state as “B”.

• The transcript states that \(i_A > 0\), \(i_B < 0\), and phase C is floating.

• The next state uses T3 while T6 remains closed. The transcript appears to identify this as a C-to-B or similar state, but the exact notation is unclear.

The general commutation pattern is that one switch remains active between adjacent states while another switch changes to redirect current into the next phase. A complete controller must implement the required sequence in hardware and software.

Practical inverter hardware also includes:

• MOSFETs or equivalent switching devices.

• Diodes.

• DC-link capacitors.

• Protection against switching transients.

• A microcontroller to generate the gate-control signals.

• Rotor-position sensors or an alternative sensorless position-estimation method.

Rotor position and Hall-effect sensors

Hall-effect sensors detect the magnetic field from the permanent-magnet rotor.

The lecturer describes the sensor behaviour qualitatively:

• A north pole near a sensor causes a high output.

• A south pole near a sensor causes a low output.

• As the rotor turns, each sensor transitions between high and low.

• The three sensor outputs form a three-bit binary code.

Examples mentioned in the transcript include:

• 001

• 000

• 100

• 110

The complete sequence is not listed in the transcript, but each valid Hall code corresponds to a particular commutation state. The microcontroller reads the Hall signals and selects the appropriate inverter switching combination.

Hall sensors therefore provide two related functions:

1. They indicate the approximate rotor position.

2. They tell the controller when to change the active phase pair.

The sensor signals are not themselves the phase currents. They are logic-level position signals used to control the switches.

Four-pole motor and electrical cycles

The lecture focuses on a four-pole motor. A four-pole motor has:

• Two north poles and two south poles.

• Two pole pairs.

• Two electrical cycles per mechanical revolution.

For a motor with \(p\) pole pairs:

\[

N_\text{electrical cycles per mechanical revolution} = p

\]

Thus, for a four-pole motor:

\[

p = \frac{4}{2} = 2

\]

and:

\[

f_e = p f_m

\]

where:

• \(f_e\) is electrical frequency.

• \(f_m\) is mechanical rotational frequency.

• \(p\) is the number of pole pairs.

The lecturer distinguishes a mechanical cycle from an electrical cycle. A full mechanical revolution of the rotor produces two electrical cycles in the four-pole example.

One example gives a mechanical period of approximately 7.5 ms. For a four-pole motor, the corresponding electrical period is approximately half the mechanical period:

\[

T_e \approx \frac{7.5\ \text{ms}}{2} = 3.75\ \text{ms}

\]

Winding arrangement

The three motor phases are distributed around the stator. The lecturer shows that a phase winding can extend between opposite sides of the stator rather than being confined to one local tooth.

The phases are labelled A, B, and C. The physical winding arrangement determines:

• The magnetic field distribution.

• The number and location of magnetic poles.

• Whether the resulting waveform is closer to trapezoidal or sinusoidal.

The lecturer emphasises that inspecting the physical motor, windings, and permanent magnets helps clarify the meaning of “two-pole”, “four-pole”, and “pole pair”.

Idealised phase-current waveforms

The phase currents are shown as idealised waveforms associated with commutation. When the active phase pair changes:

• One phase current is switched off.

• Another phase becomes active.

• A third phase may become floating.

• Current does not necessarily change instantaneously in a real winding because the winding has inductance.

The winding time constant is described as:

\[

\tau = \frac{L}{R}

\]

where:

• \(L\) is winding inductance.

• \(R\) is winding resistance.

• \(\tau\) is the electrical time constant.

The idealised waveform may show an instantaneous transition, but a real oscilloscope trace would include:

• A finite current slope.

• Switching transients.

• Ringing.

• Parasitic effects.

• Diode-conduction intervals.

PWM introduces much faster switching within the larger commutation sequence. The high-frequency switching may not be visually resolved in the overall motor-current waveform.

Back EMF and floating-phase voltage

A floating phase has no intended current, but it can still have a measurable voltage. This voltage is caused primarily by the phase’s back EMF.

The lecturer explains that:

• A driven phase is connected to the inverter and carries current.

• A floating phase is disconnected from the main current path.

• Rotor motion through the magnetic field induces a voltage in the floating winding.

• The floating-phase voltage can therefore be used to infer rotor position.

The back EMF slope changes with motor speed:

• At low speed, the back EMF changes more slowly and has a smaller slope.

• At high speed, the back EMF changes more rapidly and has a larger slope.

• When a phase changes from driven to floating, the measured voltage can show a sudden transition.

• Losses prevent the practical waveform from reaching the ideal driving voltage exactly.

The transcript refers to a two-pole example used in an LTspice or similar simulation. The lecturer states that the simulation reproduces the qualitative voltage and current waveforms shown in the lecture.

Trapezoidal and sinusoidal back EMF

The lecturer contrasts two broad back EMF shapes:

• Trapezoidal back EMF:

• Common in conventional BLDC motors.

• Compatible with six-step commutation.

• Can provide high torque.

• Produces more torque ripple than sinusoidal operation.

• Sinusoidal back EMF:

• Can produce lower torque ripple.

• Requires appropriate winding design and control.

• May provide smoother startup and operation, according to the lecturer.

• Requires checking the actual motor’s back EMF profile rather than assuming the intended shape.

For a motor being designed for sinusoidal back EMF, the lecturer recommends manually rotating the rotor before energising the motor and measuring the induced voltage. The measured waveform can be compared with a sine wave. If the waveform is not sufficiently sinusoidal, the winding or mechanical design may need revision.

Ripple and waveform shape

A non-sinusoidal back EMF waveform contributes to torque ripple and current ripple.

The lecturer states that:

• A waveform closer to a sine wave generally produces less ripple.

• Sinusoidal operation produces lower torque ripple.

• Trapezoidal operation is common and can provide greater torque, but with increased ripple.

• Switching and commutation also contribute to waveform distortion and ripple.

• Motor losses and non-ideal switching prevent perfectly ideal waveforms.

PWM and effective motor voltage

PWM is used to reduce the effective voltage applied to a motor while retaining a fixed DC supply.

The simplified relationship given is:

\[

V_\text{avg} = D V_\text{in}

\]

where:

• \(V_\text{avg}\) is the average applied voltage.

• \(D\) is duty cycle, expressed as a fraction from 0 to 1.

• \(V_\text{in}\) is the DC supply voltage.

For example:

\[

D = 0.5,\qquad V_\text{in} = 12\ \text{V}

\]

gives:

\[

V_\text{avg} \approx 6\ \text{V}

\]

This is an averaged approximation. Actual motor current, torque, and speed depend on inductance, resistance, back EMF, load, switching frequency, and commutation timing.

PWM must be synchronised with BLDC commutation:

• The Hall signals identify the active commutation state.

• The controller selects the correct conducting phases.

• PWM is applied to the appropriate switch or switches in that state.

• The floating phase should not be PWM-switched as though it were an active current-carrying phase.

• The controller changes the PWM-controlled switch when the rotor enters the next commutation sector.

Speed, torque, power, and operating point

The lecture identifies speed and torque as the two primary controlled motor variables.

The motor’s mechanical output power is approximately:

\[

P_\text{mech} \approx T\omega

\]

where:

• \(P_\text{mech}\) is mechanical output power.

• \(T\) is motor torque.

• \(\omega\) is angular velocity.

The transcript also refers to \(VI\) as an approximation for power:

\[

P \approx VI

\]

This relationship represents electrical input power, not necessarily mechanical output power. Losses mean that electrical input power exceeds useful mechanical output power.

Relevant losses include:

• Winding copper losses.

• Switching losses.

• Magnetic losses.

• Mechanical friction.

• Windage.

• Other motor and inverter losses.

Changing the PWM duty cycle changes the effective applied voltage and shifts the motor torque-speed characteristic. The operating point is found where the motor characteristic intersects the load characteristic.

The preferred operating point is generally near:

• The rated voltage.

• The rated speed.

• The rated torque.

• The motor’s high-efficiency region.

Operating away from the rated point may reduce efficiency, although practical disturbances and load requirements mean that the motor cannot always remain at the optimum point.

Duty-cycle examples and speed response

The lecturer discusses waveform examples at several duty cycles, including 100%, 75%, and 50%.

At 100% duty cycle:

• The motor receives the maximum available average voltage in the simplified model.

• The motor approaches its maximum operating speed for the given conditions.

• This may not be suitable for continuous operation.

• A design may be rated below maximum, for example around 80%, to avoid excessive current or thermal stress.

At 75% duty cycle:

• The general waveform shape remains similar.

• Additional switching features or spikes may become visible.

• The motor operates at a lower speed than at 100% duty cycle.

At 50% duty cycle:

• The motor speed decreases further.

• The electrical waveform period increases because the rotor rotates more slowly.

• A longer period is expected when comparing the same electrical cycle at lower speed.

The lecturer uses an extreme-case reasoning method: if the rotor moved extremely slowly, the electrical cycle would take much longer to complete.

Inductive switching, ringing, and diode behaviour

The winding inductance attempts to maintain current when a switch is turned off. The current therefore requires another path.

The lecturer attributes a voltage drop or reversed-polarity feature to:

• Winding inductance.

• Diode conduction.

• The need to maintain current flow.

• The resulting transient path through the inverter.

Additional observed features include:

• LC resonance.

• Damped ringing.

• A voltage drop associated with diode conduction.

• Faster decay when damping is higher.

• Switching spikes and non-ideal transitions.

These effects are absent or simplified in ideal diagrams but appear in practical oscilloscope measurements and simulations.

Zero-crossing and sensorless control

The floating-phase back EMF can be used to estimate rotor position without Hall sensors.

A common method is to detect the zero crossing of the floating-phase back EMF relative to a reference, such as the midpoint of the DC link or another defined voltage reference.

The lecture warns that zero-crossing detection becomes difficult when:

• The duty cycle is low.

• The back EMF slope is shallow.

• Switching transients obscure the waveform.

• The signal must be filtered.

• Filtering introduces phase delay.

• The exact crossing location is ambiguous because of noise and ringing.

The lecturer identifies this as a disadvantage of back-EMF-based sensorless control. Hall sensors provide direct position information, whereas back-EMF sensing requires signal interpretation and timing compensation.

Pole terminology

• A pole is one magnetic north or south pole.

• A pole pair consists of one north pole and one south pole.

• A four-pole motor has two pole pairs.

• Electrical cycles per mechanical revolution equal the number of pole pairs.

Floating phase

A floating phase is the phase intentionally excluded from the main current path during a commutation interval. It has approximately zero intended current but can still exhibit a measurable voltage due to back EMF and switching transients.

Commutation

Commutation is the timed changeover between inverter switching states. It redirects current between the three motor phases to maintain the rotating stator magnetic field.

Zero crossing

A zero crossing is the point at which a measured voltage waveform crosses its defined zero or reference level. In sensorless BLDC control, the floating-phase back EMF zero crossing can provide an estimate of rotor position.
