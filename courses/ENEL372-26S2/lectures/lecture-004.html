Overview

Lecture 4 introduces the ENEL372 solar car project and its engineering requirements.

The project involves designing and building a closed-loop buck converter between a solar panel and a permanent-magnet DC motor. The converter must allow the solar panel to operate near its maximum power point while providing suitable voltage and current to the motor. The completed system is intended for a straight-line solar car drag race of approximately 10 m or slightly more.

The lecture also covers:

• Project hardware and design constraints.

• The required controller, switching devices, diode, inductor core, and capacitance limit.

• Laboratory, simulation, inspection, report, and peer-assessment requirements.

• Solar-panel and DC-motor characteristics.

• Why direct connection of the panel to the motor is unsuitable.

• The buck converter as an approximate “DC transformer” and impedance-matching device.

• The intended maximum-power-point voltage-control loop.

The project is completed in groups of three. The race is described as an end-of-year activity rather than weighted course assessment, while the project simulation, hardware inspection, report, and peer assessment are assessed.

Key concepts

• Solar car system:

• Solar panel → buck converter → permanent-magnet DC motor.

• The motor drives the rear wheels through a gearbox.

• Steering is controlled by an RC module and servo.

• The battery is only for the RC steering/control system; the propulsion system is supplied by the solar panel.

• Main project objective:

• Make the solar panel provide its maximum available power.

• Feed as much of that power as possible to the motor.

• Maintain useful converter efficiency, but prioritise operation of the panel at its maximum power point.

• Maximum power point:

• A solar panel has a nonlinear current-voltage characteristic.

• Panel power is \(P = VI\).

• The maximum power point occurs near the “knee” of the panel’s I–V curve.

• The maximum-power-point voltage changes with operating conditions, particularly illumination and temperature.

• Buck converter:

• Reduces input voltage to a lower output voltage.

• In an ideal converter, input power approximately equals output power:

\[

P_\text{in} \approx P_\text{out}

\]

• A lower output voltage allows a higher output current for approximately the same power.

• By changing the duty ratio, the converter can present a suitable effective load to the solar panel.

• The converter therefore acts conceptually as a DC transformer and performs impedance matching.

• Closed-loop control:

• The solar-panel voltage is measured.

• It is compared with a reference corresponding to the desired maximum-power-point voltage.

• The voltage error is processed by a PI controller.

• The controller output determines the PWM duty ratio.

• The duty ratio controls the buck-converter switch.

• Motor behaviour:

• Motor torque is proportional to armature current.

• As motor speed increases, back EMF increases.

• Starting requires substantially more current than running.

• The motor’s voltage/current requirements differ significantly from the solar panel’s natural operating characteristics.

• Project implementation:

• LTspice is used for initial buck-converter simulation.

• Breadboards may be used for early prototyping and troubleshooting.

• The final inspected circuit should be transferred to Veroboard or, potentially, a PCB.

• An accessible current-probe loop is required so the inductor current can be measured.

• Engineering constraints:

• Closed-loop feedback is required for full project marks.

• Open-loop operation may be used temporarily to get the converter operating and investigate component values, but it is not the intended final design.

• A specified controller IC, MOSFET gate-drive arrangement, diode, inductor core, and maximum capacitance must be used.

Solar-panel characteristics

The panel is described using an I–V curve.

Important points:

• At approximately zero terminal voltage, the panel can provide a high current, corresponding approximately to short-circuit current.

• As the terminal voltage increases, current eventually decreases.

• At open circuit, the voltage reaches a maximum and current is approximately zero.

• The lecture gives an approximate open-circuit voltage of 21 V for the panel under the stated lighting conditions.

• Lower light intensity reduces the available current.

• The open-circuit voltage may remain approximately similar under the conditions described, although the exact behaviour depends on the panel and operating conditions.

• Temperature has a strong effect:

• Higher temperature may increase current at low voltage.

• However, voltage falls more rapidly as loading changes.

• The maximum-power-point voltage and maximum available power decrease at higher temperature, according to the lecture.

Panel power is the product of voltage and current. The power-voltage curve rises to a peak and then falls to zero at open circuit. The maximum-power point is near the knee of the I–V curve.

For the stated outdoor conditions, the maximum-power-point voltage is estimated at approximately 17 V. This value is not universal and depends on illumination, temperature, and the particular panel.

Permanent-magnet DC motor

The motor is described as a permanent-magnet DC motor:

• Permanent magnets are located on the stator.

• The rotor contains windings.

• Brushes connect the motor to the buck-converter output.

• Current through the rotor windings is the armature current, denoted \(i_a\).

• The interaction between magnetic fields produces torque.

• As the rotor spins, a back EMF is generated according to Faraday’s law.

• Back EMF opposes the applied voltage and limits the armature current.

The transcript gives approximate operating values:

• Starting from standstill may require approximately 1.5–2 A under the expected mechanical load.

• The motor may require only about 2 V to begin starting, according to the lecture.

• Once running, the motor may require approximately 1 A at about 6–7 V.

• The values are approximate and depend on the mechanical load, inertia, lighting conditions, and motor characteristics.

The distinction between starting and running is important. At standstill, back EMF is approximately zero, so current can be high. As speed increases, back EMF rises, reducing the current required for a given applied voltage.

Why direct connection is unsuitable

The panel and motor have incompatible electrical characteristics.

The lecture gives approximate panel characteristics of:

• Around 200 mA.

• Around 21 V open-circuit voltage.

• A maximum-power-point voltage of approximately 17 V under the stated conditions.

The motor requires:

• A much larger starting current, approximately 2 A.

• Approximately 1 A while running.

• A much lower operating voltage than the panel’s open-circuit voltage.

If the solar panel is connected directly to the motor, the operating point is determined by the intersection of the panel’s I–V curve and the motor’s I–V requirement. The resulting point is below the motor’s starting requirement and far from the panel’s maximum-power point. Therefore:

• The motor may fail to start.

• The panel will not deliver its maximum available power.

• The car will not achieve the desired performance.

The buck converter solves this mismatch by transforming the voltage/current combination while controlling the effective load seen by the panel.

Buck converter as impedance matching

Conceptually, the buck converter can convert:

• Higher panel voltage and lower panel current

• Into lower motor voltage and higher motor current

For an ideal converter:

\[

V_\text{in} I_\text{in} = V_\text{out} I_\text{out}

\]

In a real converter:

\[

P_\text{out} = \eta P_\text{in}

\]

where \(\eta < 1\) is the efficiency.

During motor starting, the converter should provide a lower output voltage with sufficient current. As the motor accelerates and its current requirement changes, the duty ratio can be adjusted to provide a higher output voltage at a lower current while keeping the solar panel near its maximum-power point.

The converter is therefore not simply regulating the motor voltage. Its primary control objective in this project is to control the panel operating voltage so that the panel supplies maximum power.

Control-system structure

The intended control structure is:

1. Measure the solar-panel voltage.

2. Compare the measured voltage with a reference voltage representing the maximum-power-point voltage.

3. Form an error signal.

4. Process the error through a PI controller.

5. Feed the controller output to a PWM generator.

6. Use the PWM duty ratio to control the buck-converter switch.

7. Adjust the converter’s effective input loading until the panel voltage is maintained near the reference.

The TL494 is described as providing much of the PWM/control functionality. Additional components are required to establish suitable PI-controller weighting or gains.

The control loop regulates the solar-panel side rather than directly regulating the motor side. The motor is treated as the load and draws current according to its operating condition. The converter changes its duty ratio to keep the panel at the desired operating voltage despite changes in motor behaviour.

Equations and definitions

Electrical power

\[

P = VI

\]

where:

• \(P\) is electrical power.

• \(V\) is voltage.

• \(I\) is current.

For a solar panel, the maximum-power point is the operating point at which \(VI\) is greatest.

Converter power balance

For an ideal buck converter:

\[

P_\text{in} = P_\text{out}

\]

or:

\[

V_\text{in} I_\text{in}

=

V_\text{out} I_\text{out}

\]

For a real converter:

\[

P_\text{out} = \eta P_\text{in}

\]

where \(\eta\) is converter efficiency.

Ideal buck-converter voltage relationship

For an ideal buck converter operating in the usual continuous-conduction model:

\[

V_\text{out} \approx D V_\text{in}

\]

where:

• \(D\) is the PWM duty ratio, between 0 and 1.

• \(V_\text{in}\) is the converter input voltage.

• \(V_\text{out}\) is the average converter output voltage.

This relationship was not explicitly stated in the transcript, but it is the standard relationship relevant to the buck-converter discussion and should be confirmed against the course treatment.

DC-motor back EMF

The lecture gives the relationship:

\[

E_a = K_e \omega

\]

where:

• \(E_a\) is the motor back EMF.

• \(K_e\) is the motor back-EMF constant.

• \(\omega\) is rotor angular speed.

As speed increases, back EMF increases.

DC-motor torque

The lecture gives the relationship:

\[

T = K_t i_a

\]

where:

• \(T\) is motor torque.

• \(K_t\) is the motor torque constant.

• \(i_a\) is armature current.

Thus, motor torque is proportional to armature current.

Motor voltage balance

A standard permanent-magnet DC-motor model is:

\[

V_a = E_a + R_a i_a + L_a \frac{di_a}{dt}

\]

where:

• \(V_a\) is armature voltage.

• \(E_a\) is back EMF.

• \(R_a\) is armature resistance.

• \(L_a\) is armature inductance.

• \(i_a\) is armature current.

Only the back-EMF relationship and torque-current relationship were clearly captured in the transcript. The complete voltage-balance equation should be checked against the course notes.

Maximum-power-point condition

For a panel power-voltage curve:

\[

P(V) = V I(V)

\]

At the maximum-power point:

\[

\frac{dP}{dV} = 0

\]

This condition was not explicitly stated in the transcript but expresses mathematically why the power-voltage curve peaks at the knee of the I–V characteristic.

Feedback error

The control loop can be represented conceptually as:

\[

e(t) = V_\text{MPP,ref} - V_\text{PV}(t)

\]

where:

• \(V_\text{MPP,ref}\) is the desired panel maximum-power-point voltage.

• \(V_\text{PV}\) is the measured panel voltage.

• \(e(t)\) is the voltage-control error.

A PI controller may be represented as:

\[

u(t) = K_p e(t) + K_i \int e(t)\,dt

\]

where:

• \(K_p\) is the proportional gain.

• \(K_i\) is the integral gain.

• \(u(t)\) is the controller output used to determine PWM duty ratio.

The exact implementation and sign convention must be confirmed from the project circuit and lecture material.

Definitions

• Buck converter: A switching DC–DC converter that generally produces a lower average output voltage than its input.

• Duty ratio \(D\): The fraction of each switching period for which the controlled switch is on.

• PWM: Pulse-width modulation; control of average converter behaviour by changing pulse width or duty ratio.

• Freewheeling diode: The diode that provides a current path for the inductor when the main switch is off.

• Back EMF: Voltage generated by a rotating motor that opposes the applied voltage.

• Armature current \(i_a\): Current through the motor’s rotor windings.

• Maximum power point: The solar-panel operating point at which \(VI\) is maximised.

• PI controller: A proportional-integral feedback controller.

• Veroboard: A perforated prototyping board with copper strips that can be cut and interconnected to form a circuit.

• Current-probe loop: A deliberate conductor loop included on the board so that a current probe can measure circuit current, particularly inductor current.
