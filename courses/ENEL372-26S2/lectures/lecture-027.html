Introduction to BLDC motors

The lecturer introduced BLDC motors as a special form of synchronous motor using permanent magnets. In the basic arrangement:

• Permanent magnets are associated with the rotor.

• Stator windings generate a magnetic field.

• The stator field is switched or commutated so that it attracts and repels the rotor magnets in sequence.

• This sequence produces continuous rotation without mechanical brushes.

The lecture emphasised that the timing of the switching is important. The controller must know, or estimate, the rotor position and energise the appropriate windings at the appropriate time. Delays, sensing, and the possibility of stall make BLDC motor control relatively complex.

BLDC motors as electronically commutated AC machines

A BLDC motor may be supplied from a DC battery, but the controller switches the DC supply between motor phases. Consequently:

• The current in each phase changes with time.

• The motor produces a rotating magnetic field.

• The phase waveforms may be trapezoidal or approximately sinusoidal, depending on the motor and drive method.

• The motor is physically and electrically related to AC machines even though its source may be a DC battery.

The lecturer contrasted BLDC motors with traditional brushed DC motors. A brushed motor uses a mechanical commutator and brushes to reverse current in the windings. A BLDC motor performs the equivalent commutation electronically.

The key principle is relative motion between magnetic fields and windings. Depending on the motor geometry:

• Permanent magnets may be on the rotor and the windings on the stator.

• Other configurations may place magnetic elements differently.

• The essential requirement is changing relative magnetic position between the rotor field and the stator windings.

Magnetic attraction, repulsion, and phasing

The lecture reviewed motor operation using simple two-pole and possibly four-pole examples. Motor torque results from the interaction between magnetic fields:

• Opposite poles attract.

• Like poles repel.

• Sequential energisation of stator windings creates a rotating magnetic field.

• The rotor follows this field because of the attraction and repulsion forces.

• The phase sequence is central to producing continuous torque.

Back EMF

As the motor rotates, the changing magnetic flux through the windings induces a voltage. This induced voltage opposes the applied voltage and is called back EMF.

The lecturer’s explanation was:

• At low speed, back EMF is small.

• The applied voltage is then less opposed, so current can become large.

• Large current causes copper losses and heating.

• As speed increases, back EMF increases.

• The increased back EMF reduces the current for a given applied voltage.

• This is one reason motors can operate more safely and efficiently at higher speed than at very low speed.

Electrical power and mechanical power

The motor converts electrical power into mechanical power:

• A battery or other DC source supplies electrical energy.

• Current in the windings produces magnetic fields.

• The magnetic fields interact with permanent magnets or another rotor field.

• The resulting torque produces mechanical rotation.

Losses occur in the windings, magnetic materials, bearings, electronics, and any gearbox. If gearing is used, the gearbox also introduces losses.

The lecturer noted that motors often run efficiently at relatively high speed, while the mechanical load may require lower speed. For example, a large propeller may need to rotate more slowly than the motor’s preferred speed. A gearbox can reduce speed, but each reduction stage introduces additional losses. Motor and load selection should therefore consider both torque and speed rather than torque alone.

Torque, speed, and application matching

The motor must provide enough torque to:

• Overcome the static and running resistance of the load.

• Accelerate the load when required.

• Maintain the desired operating speed.

If the load requires more torque than the motor can provide, the motor slows or stalls. At low speed, the resulting reduction in back EMF can cause high current and severe heating.

The desired motor operating point depends on the application. For a solar glider, for example, the motor and propeller must be selected to provide the required speed and torque while minimising energy consumption.

The lecturer stated that the complete design of a BLDC motor for a particular application would not be required in the examination, but students should understand how to select or assess torque-speed characteristics for a load.

Torque-speed curves and steady-state operation

A motor torque-speed curve shows the torque the motor can provide at different speeds. A load torque-speed curve shows the torque required by the mechanical load at different speeds.

The operating point is the intersection of the two curves:

• If motor torque exceeds load torque, the system accelerates.

• If motor torque is less than load torque, the system decelerates.

• At the intersection, motor torque equals load torque and the speed is steady-state.

• A transient may occur before the system settles at the intersection.

This intersection is the design or operating point. Ideally, it should occur in a region of good efficiency and within the motor’s thermal and mechanical limits.

Increasing the available electrical power, using a larger battery, or selecting a larger motor can shift the motor characteristic so that a higher speed and/or torque can be achieved. The mechanical load is often more constrained by the application, so selecting a motor that matches the load is usually more practical than changing the load.

This means:

• The exact relationship depends on the geometry, air density, and operating conditions.

Hoisting loads

A hoist or crane lifting a load has a different torque-speed characteristic:

• A major component of the required torque is associated with overcoming gravity.

• This component is approximately independent of speed during steady lifting.

• Additional torque is required for acceleration.

• Friction and other real-world losses add a speed-dependent component.

• Bearing and viscous friction were described as approximately linear with speed in the relevant operating range.

The lecture distinguished steady-state lifting from acceleration. Initially, the motor may need extra torque to accelerate the load. Once the load is moving at constant speed, the principal requirement is to balance gravity and losses.
