Overview

Lecture 9 develops a practical procedure for designing a power inductor for a power-electronic converter.

The lecture connects:

• Converter circuit analysis to the required inductance and peak current.

• Magnetic-circuit analysis to physical inductor parameters.

• Faraday’s law to the inductance expression.

• Core material properties to saturation limits.

• The required turns-area product to the number of turns.

• RMS current to the minimum conductor size.

• Required reluctance to the air-gap length.

The representative design process is:

1. Determine the required inductance and peak current from converter analysis.

2. Calculate the minimum turns-area product.

3. Select the number of turns.

4. Select a suitable conductor size.

5. Check that the turns and wire fit on the core.

6. Calculate the required magnetic reluctance.

7. Implement the reluctance predominantly using an air gap.

The lecture uses a constrained solar-car project example in which the core type is specified.

Key concepts

• Magnetomotive force (MMF):

• The MMF produced by a winding is \(NI\), where \(N\) is the number of turns and \(I\) is the winding current.

• Magnetic-circuit analogy:

• MMF corresponds to EMF.

• Reluctance corresponds to resistance.

• Magnetic flux corresponds to electrical current.

• Magnetic flux and reluctance:

• \(\Phi = NI/\mathcal{R}\).

• Flux density:

• \(B = \Phi/A\), so \(\Phi = BA\).

• Inductance from magnetic reluctance:

• \(L = N^2/\mathcal{R}\).

• Inductor design is driven by both:

• Electrical requirements, such as inductance, average current, ripple current, and peak current.

• Physical and magnetic constraints, such as core area, core permeability, saturation flux density, wire size, and air-gap dimensions.

• Saturation must be avoided:

• The selected maximum flux density must remain below the region where the core’s \(B\)-\(H\) curve begins to bend significantly.

• Effective core area:

• \(A_E\) is the effective cross-sectional area for the complete magnetic circuit. It is not necessarily the minimum physical cross-sectional area.

• Air-gap dominance:

• The air gap has a much lower permeability than ferrite and therefore usually dominates the total reluctance.

• Energy storage:

• When the air gap dominates the reluctance, most of the magnetic energy is stored in the air gap rather than in the ferrite.

• Design headroom:

• The lecture recommends designing below the absolute saturation limit to allow for construction errors and parameter variation.

• Larger wire:

• A larger conductor reduces winding resistance and resistive losses, but increases mass and may make winding more difficult.

• Physical feasibility:

• The required number of turns and conductor diameter must fit within the bobbin/core window.

Detailed notes

Magnetic-circuit relationships

The winding produces a magnetomotive force:

\[

\text{MMF} = NI

\]

For a simple magnetic circuit, the flux is:

\[

\Phi = \frac{NI}{\mathcal{R}}

\]

where \(\mathcal{R}\) is the magnetic reluctance.

The units of reluctance are ampere-turns per weber:

\[

[\mathcal{R}] = \frac{\text{A-turn}}{\text{Wb}}

\]

The lecture compares this with an electrical circuit:

| Magnetic circuit | Electrical circuit |

|---|---|

| MMF, \(NI\) | EMF, \(V\) |

| Reluctance, \(\mathcal{R}\) | Resistance, \(R\) |

| Flux, \(\Phi\) | Current, \(I\) |

Flux density is related to magnetic flux by:

\[

\Phi = BA

\]

Using this in the magnetic-circuit expression gives:

\[

\mathcal{R} = \frac{NI}{BA}

\]

This relationship is later combined with the inductance equation.

Deriving inductance from the magnetic circuit

Faraday’s law gives the induced voltage across an \(N\)-turn winding:

\[

v = N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}

\]

The usual sign from Lenz’s law is not included in the transcript’s expression.

Using:

\[

\Phi = \frac{NI}{\mathcal{R}}

\]

and assuming that \(N\) and \(\mathcal{R}\) are constant:

\[

v

= N\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{NI}{\mathcal{R}}\right)

= \frac{N^2}{\mathcal{R}}\frac{dI}{dt}

\]

Since the voltage across an inductor is also:

\[

v = L\frac{dI}{dt}

\]

the inductance is:

\[

L = \frac{N^2}{\mathcal{R}}

\]

This expression relates the analytically required inductance to the physical magnetic circuit.

Relating inductance, flux density, and turns

Starting with:

\[

L = \frac{N^2}{\mathcal{R}}

\]

and:

\[

\mathcal{R} = \frac{NI}{BA}

\]

gives:

\[

L

= \frac{N^2BA}{NI}

= \frac{NBA}{I}

\]

Rearranging:

\[

NA = \frac{LI}{B}

\]

For the design limit, the lecture uses the maximum current and maximum allowable flux density:

\[

(NA)_{\min} = \frac{L I_{\max}}{B_{\max}}

\]

This is the minimum turns-area product. The \(A\) used in the practical core calculation is the effective core area \(A_E\):

\[

N_{\min} = \frac{(NA)_{\min}}{A_E}

\]

The number of turns must be rounded up, never down, because rounding down would reduce the turns-area product below the design requirement.

\geq \frac{I_{\text{RMS}}}{J}

A larger wire, such as approximately 0.7 mm or 1 mm diameter, may be selected because it:

• Reduces the winding resistance.

• Reduces resistive loss.

• Improves efficiency.

• Increased mass.

• Potentially more difficult winding.

• Increased risk that the winding will not fit in the available volume.

The 5 A/mm² value is presented as a simplified design rule. A complete design would also consider insulation, thermal conditions, winding depth, and other construction details.

A full design may use a packing equation that accounts for:

• Bare conductor diameter.

• Insulation thickness.

• Packing efficiency.

• Circular wire geometry.

• Multiple winding layers.

Calculating the required reluctance

Once \(N\) and \(L\) are known:

\[

\mathcal{R}_{\text{required}}

= \frac{N^2}{L}

\]

For the representative values \(N=60\) and \(L=1\ \text{mH}\):

\[

\mathcal{R}_{\text{required}}

= \frac{60^2}{1\times10^{-3}}

= 3.6\times10^6\ \text{A-turns/Wb}

\]

Core reluctance without an air gap

The reluctance of a core section is:

\[

\mathcal{R}_{\text{core}}

= \frac{l_E}{\mu_0\mu_r A_E}

\]

where:

\[

\mu_0 \approx 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{H/m}

\]

and:

\[

\mu_r \approx 1600

\]

If the two ferrite halves are clamped together with no intentional air gap, the effective permeability of the core is high and the resulting inductance is much larger than required for the representative design. The lecture uses this to demonstrate that the core alone does not provide the required reluctance in a practical physical size.

An intentional air gap is therefore introduced.

Air-gap design

The relative permeability of air is approximately one:

\[

\mu_{r,\text{air}} \approx 1

\]

The reluctance of the air gap is approximately:

\[

\mathcal{R}_g

= \frac{l_g}{\mu_0 A_E}

\]

For the illustrated E-core arrangement, the magnetic flux passes through the air gap twice. Therefore, if \(l_g\) is the physical gap at each relevant interface, the total effective air-gap length is approximately:

\[

l_{\text{total}} = 2l_g

\]

The total reluctance is then approximated by:

\[

\mathcal{R}

\approx \frac{2l_g}{\mu_0 A_E}

\]

Solving for the individual air-gap length:

\[

l_g

= \frac{\mathcal{R}\mu_0 A_E}{2}

\]

Using the representative reluctance and core area, the lecture obtains an individual gap of approximately:

\[

l_g \approx 142\ \mu\text{m}

\]

and a total effective air-gap length of approximately:

\[

2l_g \approx 285\ \mu\text{m}

\]

The lecturer compares approximately 142 μm with the thickness of ordinary paper and suggests using a paper layer to separate the core halves. The practical construction must maintain the intended separation and avoid an accidental short magnetic path.

Energy storage in the air gap

The magnetic-circuit analogy is used to explain energy storage.

Because the air gap dominates the reluctance:

• Most of the MMF drop occurs across the air gap.

• Most of the magnetic energy is stored in the air gap.

• The ferrite core mainly guides the flux.

The lecture describes the stored energy as being concentrated in a very small region, approximately a 120–130 μm zone for the representative design. This is counterintuitive because most of the physical volume of the inductor contains little of the stored magnetic energy.
