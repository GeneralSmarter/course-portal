Key concepts

• Rectifier: A circuit or converter that changes AC into DC.

• Inverter: A circuit or converter that changes DC into AC.

• Transformer rectifier unit (TRU): A unit that combines transformer and rectifier functions. In the example, it converts three-phase AC into a multi-phase rectifier input and produces 28 V DC.

• Three-phase AC: Three sinusoidal voltages separated by 120° electrical phase.

• Six-phase rectification: The lecture describes obtaining six rectifier phases by offsetting three additional phases by 30° from the original three phases.

• Pulse count: A six-phase rectifier is described as producing 12 pulses per input period. More pulses increase the ripple frequency and make filtering easier.

• Ripple: The residual AC variation present in a rectified DC waveform.

• RMS voltage: The effective value normally quoted for an AC supply. It is not the peak amplitude of the sine wave.

• 400 Hz aircraft power: A higher-frequency AC supply used in the aircraft example. Higher frequency allows smaller filtering components and makes ripple easier to filter.

• DC bus: A distribution point or network for DC power.

• Battery bus: A distribution path connected to the aircraft battery system. It allows battery power to support critical loads when generator power is unavailable.

• Static inverter: An electronic converter that changes DC back into AC without a mechanically rotating machine.

• Vertical gyro: A critical flight instrument using a rapidly spinning rotor to provide a stable reference for aircraft attitude, including pitch and roll.

• Split-phase induction motor: The vertical gyro motor is described as a single-phase AC induction motor using a capacitor to create a phase offset and a rotating magnetic field.

• Redundancy: Multiple power paths and sources are used so that the loss of one generator or engine does not immediately disable a critical instrument.

• Circuit-breaker and terminal-board references: Wiring diagrams can be traced by following repeated circuit-breaker designators and terminal-board numbers across pages.

Why rectifiers matter

A mains-powered cellphone charger or computer power supply receives AC from the mains but must provide DC to the electronic device. A rectifier is therefore required to convert AC into DC.

The lecturer gives broader examples:

• AC generated at a power station can be rectified for high-voltage DC transmission.

The central practical point is that the rectifier is not an isolated circuit topic. It is part of a larger power-conversion chain involving generators, transformers, buses, batteries, inverters, and loads.
