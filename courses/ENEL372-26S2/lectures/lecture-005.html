Overview

Lecture 5 develops practical design considerations for buck converters, with emphasis on the ENEL372 solar car project.

Main topics:

• Maintaining continuous inductor current and avoiding discontinuous conduction mode (DCM).

• Selecting the minimum inductance required for continuous conduction.

• Choosing a practical switching frequency.

• Understanding the effects of discontinuous conduction on converter gain, control, current stress, noise, and efficiency.

• Estimating output-voltage ripple and sizing the output capacitor.

• Understanding input-capacitor requirements for a non-ideal source, particularly a solar panel.

• Considering RMS and peak component currents, voltage ratings, losses, and thermal management.

• Applying the design process to an example 12 V to 8 V, 2 A buck converter.

The lecture repeatedly uses a worst-case duty ratio of approximately 0.5 for design calculations because the product \(D(1-D)\) is maximised there.

Key concepts

• Continuous conduction mode (CCM): The inductor current remains greater than zero throughout the switching cycle.

• Discontinuous conduction mode (DCM): The inductor current reaches zero and remains at zero for part of the switching cycle.

• The boundary between CCM and DCM occurs when the minimum inductor current is exactly zero.

• For triangular inductor-current ripple, the boundary condition is:

\[

I_{\text{out}} > \frac{\Delta I_L}{2}

\]

where \(\Delta I_L\) is the peak-to-peak inductor-current ripple.

• CCM is preferred because it generally provides:

• Better efficiency.

• More predictable and stable feedback-control behaviour.

• Lower peak and RMS current magnitudes.

• Smaller filtering components.

• Lower \(di/dt\), \(dv/dt\), and associated electrical noise.

• Higher switching frequency reduces the required inductance and capacitance, but increases switching losses, magnetic losses, electromagnetic noise, and layout sensitivity.

• The lecture recommends approximately 20–100 kHz for the project:

• Below about 20 kHz, audible switching noise may become problematic.

• Around and above 100 kHz, breadboard parasitics and layout become increasingly important.

• In CCM, the ideal buck-converter voltage conversion relationship is approximately:

\[

V_{\text{out}} = D V_{\text{in}}

\]

• In DCM, the voltage conversion ratio is no longer simply proportional to duty ratio. Output voltage becomes dependent on load current and other circuit parameters.

• A finite output capacitor produces voltage ripple because it alternately charges and discharges.

• For the solar-car application, an input capacitor is needed to reduce current and voltage ripple seen by the solar panel.

• The solar panel is described as behaving more like a current source than an ideal voltage source. Maintaining approximately constant panel current and low voltage ripple helps preserve operation near the maximum power point.

• Component voltage ratings, peak currents, RMS currents, conduction losses, switching losses, and thermal management must all be considered in a physical design.

2. Continuous inductor current

When the controllable switch is on, the inductor current ramps upward and the inductor stores energy. When the switch is off, the inductor releases energy to the load and its current ramps downward.

The current is continuous if the downward ramp does not reach zero before the next switching cycle begins.

At the CCM/DCM boundary:

• The minimum inductor current is zero.

• The average inductor current is approximately the output current.

• The ripple waveform is triangular.

• The average current at the boundary is half the peak-to-peak ripple:

\[

I_{\text{out}} = \frac{\Delta I_L}{2}

\]

• To remain safely in CCM:

\[

I_{\text{out}} > \frac{\Delta I_L}{2}

\]

The lecturer recommends designing with margin rather than operating exactly at the boundary.

3. Why CCM is preferred

The lecture identifies several advantages of CCM relative to DCM.

#### Efficiency

Continuous current reduces the severity of current peaks and generally improves converter efficiency.

#### Feedback control and stability

The converter is normally operated with measurements used for feedback control. CCM makes the converter behaviour more predictable and generally easier to control.

If the inductor is too small and the converter enters DCM, the converter exhibits nonlinear gain. This makes the control problem more difficult.

#### Filter-component size

Lower current ripple and lower peak currents can permit smaller filtering components.

#### Electrical noise

Large \(di/dt\) and \(dv/dt\) values are sources of electrical noise. CCM reduces the current and voltage transition severity compared with operation involving larger discontinuities.

Switch-mode converters are inherently generally noisier than linear converters, so reducing current and voltage transition magnitudes is useful.

4. Minimum inductance for CCM

The inductor-current change can be calculated during either the switch-on or switch-off interval. In steady state, the magnitude of the current increase during one interval equals the magnitude of the current decrease during the other interval.

Using the switch-off interval, the transcript gives the magnitude of the ripple as approximately:

\[

\Delta I_L = \frac{V_{\text{out}}(1-D)}{L f_s}

\]

where:

• \(V_{\text{out}}\) is the output voltage.

• \(D\) is the duty ratio.

• \(L\) is the inductance.

• \(f_s\) is the switching frequency.

• \(\Delta I_L\) is the peak-to-peak inductor-current ripple.

Substituting this into the CCM boundary condition gives the minimum inductance required to avoid DCM:

\[

L_{\min} \geq \frac{V_{\text{out}}(1-D)}{2 I_{\text{out}} f_s}

\]

The inequality direction and exact boundary convention should be checked against the course notes. The design principle is that the selected inductance should be greater than the calculated boundary value.

For a buck converter:

\[

V_{\text{out}} = D V_s

\]

where \(V_s\) is the input/source voltage. Therefore:

\[

L_{\min} \geq \frac{V_s D(1-D)}{2 I_{\text{out}} f_s}

\]

The product \(D(1-D)\) is:

• Zero when \(D=0\).

• Maximum at \(D=0.5\).

• Zero when \(D=1\).

• Equal to \(1/4\) at \(D=0.5\).

Thus, a worst-case design value is:

\[

L_{\min,\text{worst}} \geq \frac{V_s}{8 I_{\text{out}} f_s}

\]

This worst-case expression is appropriate when the converter may operate near \(D=0.5\). If the expected duty-ratio range is known and does not include 0.5, the full \(D(1-D)\) expression can provide a smaller required inductance.

For the solar car:

• At startup, the output voltage is expected to be low while the input is approximately 17 V according to the lecture.

• This results in a small initial duty ratio.

• As the car speeds up, the required output voltage may increase and the duty ratio may approach 0.5.

• The design should therefore consider the duty-ratio range and its worst case.

5. Switching-frequency selection

Increasing \(f_s\) reduces the inductance required for a specified current ripple because the minimum inductance is inversely proportional to switching frequency.

Advantages of higher switching frequency:

• Smaller inductor.

• Smaller capacitor for a specified ripple requirement.

• Potentially lower cost and mass.

• Less magnetic material.

• Reduced material-related environmental impact, as described in the lecture.

Disadvantages:

• Increased switching losses.

• Increased magnetic-component losses.

• Greater influence of parasitic elements.

• Increased \(di/dt\) and \(dv/dt\).

• More electrical noise and electromagnetic interference.

• Reduced efficiency if the additional losses become significant.

• More demanding physical layout.

The lecturer describes the practical choice as a trade-off with a frequency “sweet spot” determined by:

• The application.

• Available switching devices.

• Magnetic materials.

• Required efficiency.

• Prototyping method.

• Layout quality.

Project guidance stated in the lecture:

• Avoid switching frequencies below approximately 20 kHz because the converter may produce audible noise.

• Keep the project converter below approximately 100 kHz to make breadboard or Vero-board prototyping more manageable.

• A well-laid-out PCB can operate at much higher frequencies, but breadboard parasitics become increasingly problematic at higher frequencies.

• At approximately 100 kHz and above, layout quality and parasitic inductance/capacitance require more attention.

6. Consequences of entering DCM

If the inductor is too small, the current may reach zero before the next cycle. The lecture describes the resulting consequences:

• The voltage gain is no longer linear with duty ratio.

• Output voltage becomes dependent on load current.

• The duty ratio must change when the load changes, even if the desired output voltage is unchanged.

• Peak current increases for a given average power flow.

• Switches require higher current ratings.

• Higher-rated switches may be more expensive and slower.

• Harmonics increase.

• Electrical noise increases.

• Losses increase.

• Feedback control becomes more difficult because the converter has nonlinear gain.

The lecturer therefore strongly recommends avoiding DCM for the project converter.

7. Output capacitor and output-voltage ripple

The idealised buck-converter analysis assumes a constant output voltage. In reality, the output capacitor has finite capacitance and charges and discharges during each switching cycle.

The capacitor current is related to voltage change by:

\[

i_C = C\frac{dv_C}{dt}

\]

Therefore:

\[

\Delta V_C = \frac{1}{C}\int i_C\,dt

\]

For CCM, the capacitor current is associated with the inductor-current ripple. The positive and negative areas of the capacitor-current waveform are equal over a switching cycle, so the average capacitor current is zero in steady state.

The capacitor charges when the defined capacitor current is positive and discharges when it is negative.

Approximating the relevant capacitor-current waveform as a triangle gives:

\[

\Delta V_{\text{out}} \approx \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 C_{\text{out}} f_s}

\]

Using the inductor-ripple expression:

\[

\Delta V_{\text{out}} \approx

\frac{V_{\text{out}}(1-D)}

{8 L C_{\text{out}} f_s^2}

\]

The transcript indicates that the equation was derived from the triangular area under the capacitor-current waveform. The exact notation and waveform assumptions should be checked against the lecture slides.

Important implications:

• Increasing \(C_{\text{out}}\) reduces output-voltage ripple.

• Increasing \(L\) reduces output-voltage ripple by reducing \(\Delta I_L\).

• Increasing \(f_s\) reduces output ripple strongly because the expression contains \(f_s^2\).

• The capacitance can therefore be reduced as switching frequency increases, although practical capacitor ESR, ripple-current rating, and switching losses still need consideration.

• The lecture uses approximately 1% of the output voltage as a typical small-ripple target.

For the solar-car design, the capacitance should be selected using the worst-case duty ratio, identified as \(D=0.5\).

8. Input capacitor and solar-panel source

A real voltage source may not maintain a perfectly constant voltage at the converter input. Voltage drops can occur because of:

• Source impedance.

• Wiring resistance and inductance.

• Distance between the source and converter.

• The dynamic behaviour of the source.

An input capacitor placed close to the buck converter helps maintain a stable local input voltage.

The solar panel requires additional consideration because it is described as behaving more like a current source than an ideal voltage source. The design aims to:

• Keep the solar-panel current approximately constant.

• Minimise current ripple.

• Minimise voltage ripple.

• Maintain operation near the maximum power point.

• Reduce losses associated with source-current variation.

For the simplified input-capacitor analysis, the source-current ripple is assumed to be zero.

Applying Kirchhoff’s current law at the input node:

\[

i_C = I_S - i_1

\]

where:

• \(I_S\) is the approximately constant solar-panel/source current.

• \(i_1\) is the pulsating current drawn by the buck converter.

• \(i_C\) is the input-capacitor current.

During the switch-on interval, the converter input current follows the inductor-current ramp. During the switch-off interval, the converter input current is approximately zero, so the input capacitor supplies or absorbs the difference between the constant source current and the converter input current.

9. Component ratings and thermal management

The design must consider both current and voltage stress.

Relevant ratings include:

• Maximum switch voltage.

• Maximum switch current.

• Inductor saturation current.

• Diode or synchronous-switch current.

• Capacitor voltage rating.

• Capacitor ripple-current rating.

• RMS current ratings.

• Peak current ratings.

At higher switching frequencies, both conduction and switching losses can become significant. Heat must then be removed through thermal management, such as:

• Heat sinks.

• Appropriate physical layout.

• Other methods of transferring heat away from components.
