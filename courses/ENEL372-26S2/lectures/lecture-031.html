Detailed notes

Open-loop brushless DC motor operation

The lecture begins by continuing the treatment of brushless DC motors. A motor can be characterised experimentally by applying different voltages or PWM duty cycles and measuring the resulting rotational speed.

The measured relationship is not perfectly linear, but it provides an approximate mapping between:

• Applied voltage and RPM.

• PWM duty cycle and RPM.

• A commanded RPM and an approximate initial duty cycle.

This mapping can be implemented as a lookup table. It prevents the controller from starting with an arbitrary duty cycle when, for example, a target speed of 6000 RPM is requested. Instead, the controller begins near a previously observed operating point.

The lookup table is load-dependent. A motor operating under one load may require a different duty cycle from the same motor operating under another load. It is therefore most useful when the load is approximately constant or when separate characterisations are available for different loads.

Open-loop control can be useful for starting a motor or for simple, temporary operation. However, it is not generally advisable for long-term operation because the motor current can become excessive and cause heating. A conservative commutation rate may allow temporary operation, but the motor can become inefficient and may eventually burn out if current remains high.

PWM and duty cycle

The lecture contrasts a 100% duty cycle with a low duty cycle. At 50% duty cycle, the switching signal is on for half of each PWM period and off for the other half.

The PWM period is described as being very short because the switching frequency is high. The transcript refers to millisecond and microsecond time scales, but the precise numerical examples are not clearly recoverable from the audio.

The duty cycle affects the average electrical input to the motor. It does not by itself guarantee a particular RPM because speed also depends on load, motor characteristics, commutation timing, and transient behaviour.

Open-loop commutation problems

A brushless DC motor requires the applied magnetic field to be commutated in synchronism with rotor position.

If the switching or commutation occurs too quickly, the magnetic field can be changed before the rotor has moved into the expected position. The motor may then fail to rotate, stop, oscillate, or lose synchronism.

If commutation occurs too slowly, the motor may still move, but it can behave like a poorly controlled servo: it may stop and start, move in discrete steps, and draw high current. The lecturer describes this as unsuitable for sustained operation, although it can sometimes work briefly or conservatively.

At low speed, the motor has low back EMF. The resulting high current contributes to heating and makes open-loop operation particularly vulnerable.

Back EMF and commutation timing

Back EMF is the voltage generated by the motor as its rotor moves through the magnetic field. The lecturer uses back EMF as an indicator of rotor position and commutation timing.

The lecture introduces a commutation time, abbreviated in the transcript as “CET”, and discusses comparing measured back EMF values at fractions of the assumed commutation interval. The transcript specifically refers to measuring a quantity labelled “VE1” at 25% of the assumed commutation time and then considering another point around three-quarters of the interval.

The intended principle is:

• If the assumed commutation timing is appropriate, the back EMF waveform should have a suitably symmetrical relationship to the commutation interval.

• The zero crossing should occur near the expected midpoint.

• If the zero crossing occurs too early or too late relative to the assumed interval, the commutation timing is incorrect.

• A normalised measure such as VE1 can be compared with one-half to determine whether the timing is too fast or too slow.

The exact definition and sign convention for VE1 are unclear in the native transcript. The lecturer states that the quantity is normalised and compares it with one-half, but the transcript contains contradictory fragments about whether the relevant inequality should be greater than or less than one-half. The qualitative timing principle is clearer than the precise inequality.

If commutation is too fast, the motor may be commutated before the rotor reaches the required zero crossing. If it is too slow, the rotor has already progressed too far before the next commutation event. Back EMF therefore provides a way to adjust commutation timing without a direct position sensor.

Electrical and mechanical time scales

The electrical cycle responds much faster than the mechanical system. Mechanical motion is delayed by rotor inertia and by the time required for torque to change the rotational speed.

This creates a dangerous feedback situation. For example:

1. The motor loses some load and begins accelerating.

2. The electrical logic detects behaviour suggesting that the existing commutation rate is too slow for the new speed.

3. The controller increases the commutation rate.

4. The mechanical system has not yet fully responded to the earlier disturbance.

5. The controller continues adjusting based on delayed mechanical information.

6. When the mechanical system catches up, the commutation may now be too fast.

7. The motor can overshoot, lose synchronism, or enter runaway behaviour.

The lecturer calls this effect “runaway” and emphasises that the controller must account for the time lag between an electrical command and its mechanical effect.

The same issue applies to speed control more generally. Current and voltage can change almost immediately, whereas speed changes more slowly because of mechanical inertia. A controller may therefore need deliberate delay or filtering so that it does not react too aggressively to disturbances.

Hall-effect sensors and sensorless control

Hall-effect sensors provide rotor-position information and can simplify commutation. The lecturer states that the timing problem is less severe when Hall sensors are used, although electrical and mechanical timing issues do not disappear completely.

The principal advantage of avoiding Hall sensors is reduced cost and hardware complexity. The disadvantage is that rotor position must be inferred indirectly.

The lecturer refers to this as “senseless control”, which is almost certainly intended to mean “sensorless control”. Sensorless control does not use a dedicated encoder or Hall sensor to directly measure rotor angle. Instead, it estimates rotor position from electrical measurements.

Sensorless control is described as common in applications such as UAVs and radio-controlled aircraft, where cost, weight, and compactness are important.

Current sensing and the motor model

A shunt resistor can be placed in the motor current path. The voltage across the shunt provides a measurement of current.

The estimation process may use:

• Measured current.

• Applied voltage.

• Motor resistance.

• Motor inductance.

• An estimate of back EMF.

The motor resistance must be treated carefully. The shunt resistor is only one part of the total resistance. The motor windings and other circuit elements contribute additional resistance, so the model may use a lumped resistance parameter rather than simply the nominal shunt resistance.

The lecturer also notes that capacitance and stray capacitance may affect the real circuit. The simple resistance-inductance model is therefore idealised. Experimental characterisation may be required to estimate the effective parameters accurately.

Rotor flux estimation

Once a suitable electrical model has been obtained, the measured quantities can be integrated to estimate rotor flux, particularly the flux associated with phase A.

The zero crossings of the estimated flux can be used as consistent timing points for generating virtual Hall-effect sensor outputs. An advantage claimed in the lecture is that the flux-based method is independent of rotor speed, unlike a direct back EMF method whose signal quality and magnitude vary strongly with speed.

The trade-off is that flux estimation requires a good motor model and careful signal processing. Back EMF measurement is conceptually straightforward because it uses the directly observed voltage, whereas flux estimation requires modelling, filtering, and integration.

Filtering and integration

Integration attenuates high-frequency noise, but it also accumulates any DC offset or sensor bias. If the input to an integrator contains a constant error, the integrated output contains a ramping error.

The lecture expresses this concept verbally as the integral of a function plus a constant producing the integral of the original function plus a term proportional to time. This accumulated term eventually shifts the estimated flux enough to destroy the expected zero crossings.

Round-off error in a microcontroller can have a similar cumulative effect, especially when the numerical representation has limited precision.

A high-pass filter is therefore required to remove low-frequency bias before integration or as part of the overall flux-estimation process. The lecturer describes the practical implementation as involving several filters and software processing stages.

The transcript also states that combining a high-pass filter with integration can be viewed as equivalent, in effect, to a low-pass filter. The precise filter topology and transfer function are not given clearly enough in the transcript to reconstruct them reliably.

Continuous and discrete representations

Analogue electronics is associated with continuous-time signals and continuous mathematical models. The physical world is also generally continuous, but practical electronic systems often use discrete components and sampled measurements.

Sampling and processing have become sufficiently fast that many systems can be approximated as continuous for control-design purposes. However, if sensors or actuators are slow, the sample-and-hold interval must be included in the analysis.

When the sampling interval is sufficiently short relative to the relevant system dynamics, continuous-time mathematics can provide a useful approximation. The lecturer contrasts this with discrete control, noting that some high-performance systems are designed so that the sampled implementation behaves effectively like a continuous system.

Equations and definitions

• PWM duty cycle:

The fraction of each PWM period for which the switching signal is on.

At 50% duty cycle, the signal is on for approximately half the period and off for approximately half the period.

• RPM:

Revolutions per minute. In the lecture, RPM is treated as a mechanical speed measure, not an electrical-cycle frequency.

• Back EMF:

The voltage generated by the motor as the rotor moves through the magnetic field. It can provide indirect information about rotor position and commutation timing.

• Commutation time:

The time interval associated with changing the energised motor phases. The transcript refers to this as “CET”, but the exact acronym expansion is not fully certain.

• Rotor flux:

A quantity representing the magnetic flux associated with the rotor. In the lecture, estimated phase flux is obtained by integrating a model-based electrical signal.

• Sensorless control:

Motor control without a dedicated encoder or Hall-effect position sensor. Rotor position is inferred from electrical measurements such as current, voltage, back EMF, or estimated flux.

• Shunt resistor:

A deliberately inserted low-value resistor used to infer current from the voltage developed across it.

• Idealised motor model:

The lecture discusses resistance, inductance, voltage, current, back EMF, and possible capacitance. The effective resistance may be a lumped parameter including the shunt and motor resistance. Stray capacitance may also need to be considered.

• Integration with bias:

If a measured signal contains a constant bias term b,

∫(f(t) + b) dt = ∫f(t) dt + bt + C

The term bt grows with time and can shift or destroy the zero crossings of an integrated signal.

• Filter phase lead:

The timing difference between corresponding features of two signals, expressed as an angular phase difference. The lecturer gives approximately 10 degrees as an experimentally based target for the relevant filter response.

• Weighted filtering:

The lecture describes combining a previous filtered or estimated value with a new measured value using a weighting coefficient. The exact equation is not clearly stated, but the method reduces the effect of noisy zero-crossing measurements.

• Continuous-time system:

A system whose signals and state variables are treated as functions defined continuously in time.

• Sample-and-hold interval:

The interval during which a sampled digital controller holds a value before the next update. If this interval is not sufficiently short, it must be included in the system analysis.
