Overview

This lecture introduces the isolated forward converter, a DC–DC converter derived from the buck converter.

The forward converter:

• Transfers energy from input to output primarily through the transformer while the controllable switch is on.

• Uses the transformer in a conventional power-transfer mode rather than storing substantial energy in its magnetic core.

• Can provide either step-down or step-up conversion through the transformer turns ratio.

• Has an output stage that behaves similarly to a buck converter.

• Requires transformer-core demagnetisation every switching cycle to prevent flux accumulation and saturation.

• Uses a third, reset winding in the single-switch forward converter to return the magnetising energy to the source.

• Provides the conceptual basis for more common multi-switch converters such as push-pull and bridge converters.

The lecture analyses the converter during the switch-on and switch-off intervals, examines the relevant current and voltage waveforms, derives design restrictions, and briefly introduces a two-switch forward converter.

The forward converter differs from a flyback converter in its main energy-transfer mechanism:

• In a flyback converter, energy is intentionally stored in the transformer’s magnetic field while the switch is on and released to the output while it is off.

• In a forward converter, energy is transferred through the transformer to the output while the switch is on. Only a relatively small amount of magnetising energy is stored in the core.

The third winding is not part of the main input-to-output power-transfer path. Its purpose is to reset the transformer core by removing the magnetising current and returning the stored magnetising energy to the source.

Detailed notes

Converter structure

The single-switch forward converter contains:

• A primary winding, \(N_1\), connected to the input through a controllable switch.

• A secondary winding, \(N_2\), connected to a rectifier and buck-type output filter.

• A reset winding, \(N_3\), connected through a diode to the input source.

• A magnetising inductance, \(L_M\), representing the magnetising behaviour of the transformer.

• An output inductor, \(L_X\).

• A rectifier diode, \(D_1\).

• A freewheeling diode, \(D_2\).

• A reset-winding diode, referred to as \(D_3\) in the homework discussion.

The transformer has three windings on a common core. The dot convention specifies the relative polarity of the winding voltages. If a winding has a positive voltage at its dotted terminal, the other windings have positive voltage at their dotted terminals, subject to the chosen voltage-reference directions.

Switch closed

When the controllable switch closes:

1. The input source applies \(V_S\) across the primary winding.

2. The transformer produces corresponding voltages on the secondary and reset windings according to their turns ratios.

3. The secondary rectifier diode \(D_1\) is forward-biased and conducts.

4. The freewheeling diode \(D_2\) is reverse-biased.

5. Power is transferred from the input to the output.

6. The output inductor \(L_X\) is energised and its current ramps upward.

7. The magnetising inductance \(L_M\) is also energised, so the magnetising current ramps upward.

8. The reset-winding diode is reverse-biased and does not conduct.

The voltage applied to the secondary-side buck stage is the input voltage scaled by the transformer turns ratio:

\[

V_X = \frac{N_2}{N_1}V_S

\]

The output-inductor voltage while the switch is on is therefore:

\[

v_{L_X,\text{on}} = \frac{N_2}{N_1}V_S - V_O

\]

The output-inductor current ramps upward according to:

\[

\Delta i_{L_X,\text{on}}

=

\frac{\left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}V_S - V_O\right)DT}{L_X}

\]

The magnetising current rises because \(V_S\) is applied across \(L_M\):

\[

\Delta i_{LM,\text{on}}

=

\frac{V_S}{L_M}DT

\]

The switch current contains both:

• The magnetising current.

• The primary current associated with the transferred output power.

The lecture gives this relationship conceptually as:

\[

i_S = i_{LM} + i_1

\]

where \(i_1\) is the primary current associated with the load-power transfer.

Switch open

When the controllable switch opens:

1. The primary current associated with power transfer stops.

2. The magnetic flux in the transformer core begins to collapse.

3. The voltage polarity across the magnetising inductance reverses.

4. The voltages across the transformer windings reverse according to the dot convention.

5. The secondary rectifier diode \(D_1\) becomes reverse-biased.

6. The freewheeling diode \(D_2\) becomes forward-biased.

7. The output inductor continues supplying current to the load through \(D_2\).

8. The reset-winding diode becomes forward-biased.

9. The magnetising current flows through the reset winding and its diode, returning the magnetising energy to the source.

10. The magnetising current decreases to zero during the reset interval.

The output stage operates like the off-state of a buck converter. The output-inductor voltage is approximately:

\[

v_{L_X,\text{off}}=-V_O

\]

and the output-inductor current ramps downward according to:

\[

\Delta i_{L_X,\text{off}}

=

-\frac{V_O(1-D)T}{L_X}

\]

The reset winding has a voltage magnitude related to the input voltage by the primary-to-reset turns ratio:

\[

|v_{N_1}|

=

\frac{N_1}{N_3}V_S

\]

The magnetising current therefore ramps downward at a magnitude of approximately:

\[

\left|\frac{di_{LM}}{dt}\right|_{\text{reset}}

=

\frac{N_1}{N_3}\frac{V_S}{L_M}

\]

If \(\Delta t_X\) is the time required to reduce the magnetising current to zero, then:

\[

\Delta i_{LM,\text{off}}

=

-\frac{N_1}{N_3}\frac{V_S}{L_M}\Delta t_X

\]

Core reset and flux walking

The magnetising current and core flux must return to their initial values every switching cycle.

If the core is not fully demagnetised:

• A residual flux remains after each cycle.

• The residual flux accumulates from cycle to cycle.

• This phenomenon is called flux walking.

• Eventually, the transformer core may saturate.

• Saturation can cause excessive current and switch stress.

The reset winding provides a path for the magnetising current after the main switch opens. It ensures that the magnetising current and associated core flux return to zero.

The reset interval must fit inside the switch-off interval:

\[

\Delta t_X \leq (1-D)T

\]

where \(T\) is the switching period.

Steady-state output voltage

Applying volt-second balance to the output inductor gives the same relationship as for a buck converter, except that the buck-stage input voltage is the transformer-scaled input voltage:

\[

V_O

=

D\left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}V_S\right)

\]

Therefore:

\[

\boxed{

V_O = D\frac{N_2}{N_1}V_S

}

\]

Unlike a non-isolated buck converter, the forward converter can achieve a voltage greater than \(V_S\) by selecting a suitable secondary-to-primary turns ratio.

Output-inductor current

The output-inductor current:

• Ramps upward while the controllable switch is closed.

• Ramps downward while the switch is open.

• Should normally remain continuous for the intended design.

• Must not fall to zero if continuous conduction is required.

The boundary between continuous and discontinuous output-inductor current occurs when the minimum inductor current just reaches zero. The lecture relates this boundary to the load resistance, duty ratio, output voltage, and output-inductor ripple.

The exact boundary expression was not fully stated in a clean form in the transcript, but the method is the standard buck-converter method:

1. Determine the peak-to-peak output-inductor current ripple.

2. Set the minimum inductor current equal to zero.

3. Relate the average output current to the load using:

\[

I_O=\frac{V_O}{R}

\]

4. Solve for the limiting inductance, load resistance, or duty ratio.

Magnetising-current waveform

The magnetising current:

• Ramps upward during the switch-on interval.

• Reverses its primary-side reference direction when the switch opens.

• Ramps downward during the reset interval.

• Should reach zero before the next switching cycle begins.

The reset-winding current is zero while the main switch is closed. When the switch opens, the reset-winding diode conducts and carries the magnetising current.

If \(N_3<N_1\):

• The reset voltage magnitude is larger.

• The magnetising current falls more quickly.

• The reset interval is shorter.

• The reset-winding current magnitude is higher.

The source current waveform has:

• A positive component during switch conduction, approximately \(i_1+i_{LM}\).

• A negative component during reset, because magnetising energy is returned to the source.

• A zero or inactive interval after the core has been reset, depending on the operating conditions.

Duty-ratio restriction from core reset

The magnetising current increase during the on-time must equal the magnetising current decrease during the reset interval:

\[

\frac{V_S}{L_M}DT

=

\frac{N_1}{N_3}\frac{V_S}{L_M}\Delta t_X

\]

After cancellation:

\[

\Delta t_X

=

\frac{N_3}{N_1}DT

\]

For complete reset, this must satisfy:

\[

\frac{N_3}{N_1}DT

\leq

(1-D)T

\]

Thus:

\[

D\frac{N_3}{N_1}

\leq

1-D

\]

and:

\[

D\left(1+\frac{N_3}{N_1}\right)\leq 1

\]

Therefore, the maximum duty ratio is:

\[

\boxed{

D_{\max}

=

\frac{1}{1+\frac{N_3}{N_1}}

=

\frac{N_1}{N_1+N_3}

}

\]

A smaller reset-winding turns ratio \(N_3/N_1\) permits a larger duty ratio and reduces the reset time. However, it also increases the reset-winding current and increases the voltage stress on the main switch.

Main-switch voltage stress

While the main switch is closed, its voltage is approximately zero for an ideal switch.

Immediately after the main switch opens, the reset winding reflects a voltage onto the primary. The switch voltage becomes approximately:

\[

V_{S,\text{switch}}

=

V_S+\frac{N_1}{N_3}V_S

\]

Therefore:

\[

\boxed{

V_{S,\text{switch}}

=

V_S\left(1+\frac{N_1}{N_3}\right)

}

\]

This voltage can substantially exceed \(2V_S\) when \(N_3\) is much smaller than \(N_1\).

After the core has been fully reset:

• No current flows in the transformer windings.

• There is no induced winding voltage.

• The switch voltage falls to approximately \(V_S\) while the switch remains open.

• When the switch closes again, the switch voltage falls to approximately zero.

The design therefore involves a trade-off:

• A smaller \(N_3/N_1\) allows higher duty ratio and faster reset.

• A smaller \(N_3/N_1\) increases reset current.

• A smaller \(N_3/N_1\) increases main-switch voltage stress.

An alternative design strategy is to select the transformer turns ratio \(N_2/N_1\) so that the required output voltage is obtained without using an unnecessarily high duty ratio.

Reset-diode voltage

The homework discussion examines the voltage across the reset-winding diode.

When the main switch is closed:

• The reset-winding diode is reverse-biased.

• The voltage across the reset winding is determined by the input and the transformer turns ratio.

• The lecture states the diode voltage magnitude as involving:

\[

V_S\left(1+\frac{N_3}{N_1}\right)

\]

for the stated voltage reference.

When the main switch opens:

• The reset diode conducts while the magnetising current is being removed.

• Its ideal forward voltage is approximately zero during conduction.

• After the core is completely reset, the diode stops conducting and the voltage returns to the source-related level.

The exact sign of the diode voltage depends on the reference polarity selected in the circuit diagram.

Two-switch forward converter

A two-switch forward converter uses two controlled switches operated synchronously.

Advantages:

• The reset winding is not required.

• The magnetising energy is returned to the source through the switches and their associated paths.

• The transformer can use two windings rather than the three-winding arrangement of the single-switch design.

• The transformer core is reset by applying the opposite voltage polarity during the switch-off interval.

Disadvantages:

• Two controlled switches are required.

• One switch is located on the high side.

• The high-side switch requires more complicated gate-drive and power-supply circuitry.

• The control and drive arrangement is more complex than the single-switch converter.

The two-switch converter retains the same basic forward-converter output behaviour while replacing the reset-winding mechanism with synchronous switching.

Equations and definitions

Switching period and duty ratio

\[

T=\frac{1}{f_s}

\]

\[

D=\frac{t_{\text{on}}}{T}

\]

\[

t_{\text{off}}=(1-D)T

\]

where:

• \(T\) is the switching period.

• \(f_s\) is the switching frequency.

• \(D\) is the duty ratio.

• \(t_{\text{on}}\) is the switch-on time.

• \(t_{\text{off}}\) is the switch-off time.

Transformer voltage ratio

For ideal transformer operation:

\[

\frac{V_2}{V_1}=\frac{N_2}{N_1}

\]

Thus, during the switch-on interval:

\[

V_X=\frac{N_2}{N_1}V_S

\]

\[

\boxed{

V_O=D\frac{N_2}{N_1}V_S

\]

v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}

\[

\Delta i_L=\frac{v_L}{L}\Delta t

\boxed{

\boxed{

D_{\max}

=

\frac{N_1}{N_1+N_3}

Main-switch voltage immediately after turn-off

V_{\text{switch,max}}

Magnetising energy

\[

\boxed{

E_M=\frac{1}{2}L_Mi_{LM}^2

The lecture emphasises that the stored energy depends on the square of the magnetising current. A larger \(L_M\) reduces the magnetising-current ramp for a given applied voltage and time, so the stored energy can remain small despite the larger inductance.

Output-inductor current continuity

For continuous output-inductor current:

\[

i_{L_X,\min}>0

\[

i_{L_X,\min}=0

\]

Load relationship

\[

\boxed{

R=\frac{V_O}{I_O}

}

\]

where \(R\) is the load resistance, \(V_O\) is the output voltage, and \(I_O\) is the average output current.
