Detailed notes

Decoupling and noise reduction

The lecture began by completing the previous lecture’s discussion of capacitors used for power-supply and ground-noise decoupling.

Different capacitor technologies have different high-frequency performance. Circuit diagrams and application notes may show several capacitors connected in parallel because each capacitor is useful over a different frequency range:

• A larger capacitor, such as a roughly 10 µF electrolytic capacitor, provides bulk capacitance and is more useful for lower-frequency variations.

• A medium-value capacitor, such as a roughly 1 µF ceramic or film capacitor, provides higher-frequency decoupling.

• A small capacitor, commonly around 0.1 µF, is suitable for high-frequency pin-level decoupling. Ceramic or film types are appropriate.

The exact capacitor values are application-dependent; the values above were examples used in the lecture.

Pin-level decoupling places a capacitor directly between an IC’s supply and ground pins. The capacitor should be located as close as possible to the IC pins during PCB layout. This helps:

• Prevent noise generated by the IC from propagating into the power-supply and ground traces.

• Prevent noise already present on the incoming supply from reaching the IC.

• Supply transient current locally when the IC switches.

Global decoupling is applied across the power supply and ground at the circuit input and at other locations where noise susceptibility is expected. Examples include:

• The PCB power-entry point.

• Regions where analogue and digital traces are close together.

• Areas with large changes in current or voltage.

• Switching devices such as CMOS inverters.

• The supply pins of a TL494 controller IC.

For the TL494, a stable and low-noise supply is important because it contains the oscillator that establishes the switching frequency and control circuitry. Rapid switching in a CMOS inverter can also generate supply and ground noise, so local global-decoupling capacitors can be beneficial.

Decoupling capacitors also help reduce the inductive loop area in power-electronic circuits. This is particularly important for switching loops, where parasitic inductance can produce voltage transients and electromagnetic interference.

Why electrical isolation is required

Many DC–DC converter applications require the input and output to be electrically isolated. The lecture gave the following examples:

• Motor speed controllers, where isolation can prevent large ground loops associated with motor drives.

• Medical equipment, where a patient-connected output must be isolated from the mains supply.

• Sensitive electronics that could be damaged or disrupted by high-voltage transients on the input side.

• Communication systems distributed over a large area, where different ground potentials may exist.

Switching-frequency transformers are an efficient way to obtain isolation while still allowing power conversion. They operate at much higher frequencies than conventional 50 Hz transformers and therefore can use smaller ferrite-core magnetic components.

The transformer also provides voltage transformation. Selecting the appropriate primary-to-secondary turns ratio allows the converter to step the voltage up or down, reducing the need to achieve the same transformation through an extreme duty ratio.

There are many isolated converter configurations. The lecture limits the treatment to several common and comparatively understandable topologies.

Transformer model

A simple ideal transformer has primary and secondary windings with turns \(N_1\) and \(N_2\), respectively. The primary is conventionally treated as the input side and the secondary as the output side.

The lecture adds a magnetising inductance to the ideal-transformer model. This inductance:

• Is modelled in parallel with the primary winding.

• Represents the magnetic behaviour of the transformer core.

• Does not physically exist as a separate component.

• Allows the circuit analysis to account for the magnetic flux and magnetisation current.

The magnetising inductance could equivalently be referred to the secondary side, but placing it on the primary simplifies analysis when the primary is treated as the input.

The transformer core flux must be periodic. The net flux change over one switching period must be zero. Otherwise, a residual flux component accumulates cycle by cycle. The resulting flux density may exceed the core’s allowable maximum, causing saturation.

When the core saturates:

• Effective permeability decreases dramatically.

• The effective inductance and impedance decrease.

• The primary may behave approximately like a short circuit.

• Current can rise to destructive levels.

The lecture described effective permeability as potentially falling from a value in the approximate range of 1000 to near 1 during severe saturation. This was an illustrative statement rather than a universal material specification.

The corresponding steady-state requirement can be expressed through either:

• Zero net change in magnetising current over a switching cycle.

• Zero average voltage across the magnetising inductance.

• Zero net change in core flux over a switching cycle.

Transformer dot convention

Dots on transformer windings indicate relative winding polarity and phasing.

If a current enters the dotted terminal of one winding and produces a positive voltage at the corresponding reference polarity, the induced voltage on another winding is positive at its dotted terminal. Conversely, reversing the current reference reverses the associated voltage polarities.

The dot convention applies to all windings sharing the same magnetic core, including transformers with more than two windings. It is used to determine:

• Relative voltage polarity.

• Current direction relationships.

• Whether an induced voltage forward-biases or reverse-biases a diode.

Flyback converter topology

The flyback converter is the first isolated DC–DC converter considered in the lecture. Its main components are:

• A DC input source.

• A controlled switch.

• A two-winding transformer used as an energy-storage element.

• A secondary freewheeling/output diode.

• An output filter capacitor.

• A load.

The flyback converter is functionally similar to a buck-boost converter. Depending on the duty ratio and transformer turns ratio, the output voltage can be lower or higher than the input voltage.

The lecture notes that, in the idealised buck-boost-like relationship, a duty ratio below 0.5 corresponds to step-down operation and a duty ratio above 0.5 corresponds to step-up operation when the turns ratio is unity. The transformer turns ratio can provide step-up or step-down conversion separately, allowing operation at a more practical duty ratio.

The switch is shown as a low-side switch because it is easier to drive from a limited control supply. A high-side switch is theoretically possible, but its gate-drive requirements are more complicated.

Flyback transformer as an energy-storage element

Most isolated converter transformers are designed primarily to transfer energy efficiently, with little energy stored in the core. The flyback converter is different:

• Energy is intentionally stored in the magnetic field during the switch-on interval.

• The stored energy is released to the output during the switch-off interval.

• The transformer therefore behaves as a coupled, two-winding inductor.

The energy-storage requirement introduces losses at higher power levels. The lecture states that flyback converters are generally not used beyond approximately 200 W and are particularly popular at power levels around 100 W or below.

Because the flyback transformer stores energy, its ferrite core requires an air gap. The air gap increases the energy-storage capability and reduces the risk of core saturation.

Assumptions used for analysis

The converter analysis is performed under the following assumptions:

• Steady-state operation.

• Periodic voltage and current waveforms.

• Ideal switching.

• Input power equal to output power for the ideal converter.

• Additional losses considered only when non-ideal behaviour is discussed.

• The output capacitor is sufficiently large that output voltage is treated as constant.

• The analysis generally considers continuous magnetising current unless otherwise stated.

The modelled transformer currents require care. The source current is physically measurable at the input terminals. The model separates the primary winding current from the magnetising current, but the individual model currents cannot necessarily be measured directly in a physical four-terminal transformer. The physically accessible primary current is the combined current represented by the model.

Switch closed

When the controlled switch is closed:

• The input source is applied to the primary side.

• The magnetising inductance is energised.

• The magnetising current increases linearly.

• Energy is stored in the transformer core.

• The secondary voltage polarity reverse-biases the output diode.

• Secondary or diode current is zero.

• The output load is supplied by the output capacitor.

The source current is equal to the magnetising current in this interval under the model used in the lecture.

For a constant input voltage \(V_S\) across the magnetising inductance \(L_M\), with switch-on time \(DT\):

\[

\Delta I_{LM,\text{on}}

\frac{V_SDT}{L_M}

This is the same inductor-current ramp relationship encountered previously for boost and buck-boost converters.

While the diode is reverse-biased, the capacitor supplies the load. The capacitor therefore discharges during this interval, producing a negative capacitor-current reference if positive capacitor current is defined as current entering the capacitor.

Switch open

When the switch opens:

• The source current becomes zero.

• The magnetic flux begins to collapse.

• The magnetising inductance attempts to maintain current in the same direction.

• The winding voltage polarities reverse.

• The secondary diode becomes forward-biased.

• Stored magnetic energy is transferred to the output.

• The diode current supplies the load and recharges the capacitor.

The voltage induced on the secondary is determined by the collapsing flux and the output voltage. With the dot convention used in the lecture, the corresponding primary-side voltage is scaled by the turns ratio and carries the appropriate negative sign:

Current and voltage waveforms

The indicative waveforms are:

• Magnetising current: ramps upward while the switch is closed and ramps downward while the switch is open.

• Source current: equal to the magnetising current during switch-on and zero during switch-off.

• Diode current: zero during switch-on and a decreasing transferred current during switch-off.

• Capacitor current: approximately negative while the capacitor alone supplies the load, then changes sign as the diode supplies the load and recharges the capacitor.

• Primary winding voltage: approximately \(V_S\) during switch-on and a turns-ratio-scaled negative output voltage during switch-off.

• Magnetic flux: ramps upward during switch-on and ramps downward during switch-off, returning to its initial value each cycle.

The lecture notes that the capacitor absorbs the current ripple so that the load sees an approximately constant current and voltage.

Continuous-current and capacitor design

The magnetising inductance must be sufficiently large, or the switching frequency sufficiently high, to maintain continuous magnetising current if continuous-current operation is required.

The boundary condition occurs when the magnetising current just reaches zero at the end of an interval. The minimum required value depends on factors including:

• Magnetising inductance.

• Switching frequency.

• Duty ratio.

• Input and output voltages.

• Transformer turns ratio.

• Power level and load current.

If the transformer is already specified, switching frequency may be adjusted to satisfy the continuous-current requirement. If the frequency is constrained, the magnetising inductance or transformer design must be selected appropriately.

The output-capacitor design follows the same general approach as for the buck-boost converter because the output stage and capacitor-current behaviour are equivalent in the idealised analysis. The capacitance is selected to keep voltage ripple below the required limit.

Definitions

• Decoupling: The use of capacitors to reduce supply and ground noise, provide local transient current, and reduce the effects of parasitic inductance.

• Pin-level decoupling: Local high-frequency capacitive bypassing placed directly between an IC supply pin and ground, close to the IC pins.

• Global decoupling: Capacitive bypassing applied across power and ground at the circuit input and at noise-sensitive circuit regions.

• Electrical isolation: Separation of input and output such that there is no direct conductive electrical connection between them.

• Magnetising inductance: A circuit model representing the core’s magnetising behaviour and associated current.

• Dot convention: A transformer-winding notation indicating the relative polarity and phase of voltages and currents.

• Flyback converter: An isolated DC–DC converter that stores energy in a coupled magnetic element during switch-on and transfers that energy to the output during switch-off.

• Continuous magnetising current: Operation in which the magnetising current remains above zero throughout the switching cycle.

• Volt-second balance: The requirement that the integral of voltage across an inductor over a complete steady-state switching period is zero.
