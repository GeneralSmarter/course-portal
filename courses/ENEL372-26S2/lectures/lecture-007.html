Overview

This lecture completes the analysis and basic design considerations for the boost converter, introduces the buck-boost converter, and begins examining non-ideal behaviour in switch-mode converters.

Main topics:

• Boost-converter inductor-current continuity

• Boost-converter output-capacitor sizing and voltage ripple

• Buck-boost-converter operation and voltage conversion ratio

• Buck-boost inductor-current continuity and output-voltage ripple

• Effects of non-ideal switches, including conduction and switching losses

• Capacitor parasitics: equivalent series resistance (ESR) and equivalent series inductance (ESL)

• Inductor winding resistance and its effect on boost-converter performance

• Start-up transients and soft starting

The analysis generally assumes ideal components and steady-state periodic operation. Near the end, the lecture introduces practical non-ideal effects and transient behaviour qualitatively.

Key concepts

• A boost converter increases the output voltage relative to the input voltage. Under ideal continuous-conduction operation:

\[

\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_s}=\frac{1}{1-D}

\]

• In a boost converter, the inductor is always connected to the input. The average inductor current is therefore associated with the input or source current, not directly with the output current.

• The boost-converter inductor-current ripple is determined from the inductor voltage during the switch-on interval.

• Continuous-conduction operation requires the minimum inductor current to remain above zero. At the boundary of discontinuous conduction, the average inductor current is equal to half the peak-to-peak current ripple.

• The worst-case duty ratio for the boost-converter inductance requirement occurs at \(D=0.5\), because the factor \(D(1-D)\) is largest there.

• Discontinuous conduction has several disadvantages:

• Lower efficiency

• Higher peak currents

• Higher switch-current ratings

• Increased electrical noise

• Discontinuous conduction can slightly increase the voltage-boosting ratio, but this benefit normally does not outweigh the disadvantages.

• For boost-converter output-capacitor sizing, the capacitor supplies the load while the diode is not conducting. The capacitor discharges during this interval, producing output-voltage ripple.

• The buck-boost converter can either step the voltage down or step it up, depending on duty ratio:

• \(D<0.5\): output magnitude is less than the input magnitude

• \(D>0.5\): output magnitude is greater than the input magnitude

• The basic inverting buck-boost converter produces an output voltage with polarity opposite to the input voltage.

• For the buck-boost converter, the switch-closed interval behaves like the boost converter, while the switch-open interval has similarities to the buck converter.

• Ideal-converter analysis assumes:

• Zero voltage across a conducting switch

• Zero forward voltage across a conducting diode

• Instantaneous switching

• Ideal inductors and capacitors

• Real semiconductor switches have:

• Finite voltage drop or on-state resistance

• Non-zero turn-on and turn-off times

• Conduction losses

• Switching losses

• Conduction loss occurs when current flows through a device with a finite voltage drop.

• Switching loss occurs during transitions when voltage and current are simultaneously non-zero.

• Conduction losses tend to dominate at lower switching frequencies. Switching losses become increasingly important as switching frequency increases.

• Real capacitors have parasitic ESR and ESL:

• ESR produces additional voltage drop, power loss, heating, and voltage ripple.

• ESL can form a lightly damped resonant circuit with the capacitance and cause oscillations or abnormal voltage waveforms.

• Real inductors have winding resistance. In a boost converter, this resistance reduces the achievable output voltage, especially at high duty ratios.

• Start-up transients can produce large voltage and current spikes. Soft starting reduces these stresses by gradually increasing the duty ratio from zero.

Detailed notes

Boost-converter inductor current

The lecture begins by reviewing the boost converter. Unlike the buck converter, where the power inductor is always connected to the output, the boost-converter inductor is always connected to the source.

Consequently, the average inductor current is associated with the average source current, \(I_s\). Under the ideal 100%-efficient assumption:

\[

V_s I_s=V_{\text{out}} I_{\text{out}}

\]

Using the ideal boost-converter voltage relationship:

\[

\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_s}=\frac{1}{1-D}

\]

gives:

\[

I_s=\frac{I_{\text{out}}}{1-D}

\]

The current rise is found from the inductor relationship:

\[

v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}

\]

During the switch-on interval, the inductor voltage is approximately \(V_s\). Therefore, the peak-to-peak inductor-current ripple is:

\[

\Delta i_L=\frac{V_s}{L}\Delta t

\]

The current-rising interval is \(DT_s\), where \(T_s=1/f_s\). Thus:

\[

\Delta i_L=\frac{V_sD}{f_sL}

\]

The lecture then considers the boundary between continuous and discontinuous inductor-current operation. At the boundary, the current waveform just reaches zero. For a triangular ripple:

\[

I_{s,\text{avg}}=\frac{\Delta i_L}{2}

\]

Maintaining continuous conduction requires the average source/inductor current to be greater than this boundary value:

\[

I_s>\frac{\Delta i_L}{2}

\]

Substitution gives the minimum-inductance condition:

\[

L>\frac{V_sD(1-D)}{2f_sI_{\text{out}}}

\]

This expression is based on the ideal power-balance relationship and the ideal boost voltage ratio.

The factor \(D(1-D)\) is largest at \(D=0.5\), so a converter expected to operate around a 50% duty ratio has the worst-case inductance requirement. In practice, the selected inductance should be greater than the boundary value so that the minimum current remains comfortably above zero.

Why continuous conduction is preferred

The lecture identifies several disadvantages of discontinuous operation:

• The converter has lower efficiency.

• The peak current is higher for a given average power transfer.

• Higher-rated switches may be required.

• Higher peak currents increase electrical noise and electromagnetic interference.

The textbook apparently discusses designing for discontinuous-current operation, but the lecturer presents this as an unusual design approach in the context of the course. The normal design emphasis is continuous-current operation.

One possible advantage of discontinuous operation is a slightly improved voltage-boosting ratio. The lecture compares a continuous-current curve with a discontinuous-current curve and notes that the discontinuous curve provides somewhat more boosting for a given duty ratio. However, the improvement is not considered sufficient to compensate for the disadvantages.

Boost-converter output-capacitor sizing

The input source is treated as an ideal voltage source, so input-capacitance sizing is not considered in this lecture. The focus is on the output capacitor and the required output-voltage ripple.

The output stage contains:

• The diode

• The output capacitor

• The resistive load

• The output current \(I_{\text{out}}\)

When the diode is not conducting, the capacitor supplies the load. The capacitor current is opposite to the defined positive charging-current direction, so the capacitor is discharging.

For a sufficiently large capacitor, the output voltage changes slowly over one switching interval. The load current can therefore be approximated as constant during the capacitor-discharge interval. The exact capacitor voltage follows an RC exponential, but over the short switching interval it is approximated as a linear ramp.

Starting from:

\[

i_C=C\frac{dv_C}{dt}

\]

the voltage change during a constant-current interval is:

\[

\Delta v_C=\frac{I_{\text{out}}}{C}\Delta t

\]

For the boost converter, the capacitor supplies the load while the switch is conducting. This interval is approximately \(DT_s\), so the output-voltage ripple magnitude is:

\[

\Delta V_{\text{out}}\approx\frac{I_{\text{out}}D}{f_sC}

\]

The capacitor voltage has zero average change over a complete steady-state switching period. Therefore, it is sufficient to calculate the voltage change during the interval where the capacitor current is simple and approximately constant.

Introduction to the buck-boost converter

The lecture next introduces the third fundamental DC-DC converter: the buck-boost converter.

The buck-boost topology is closely related to the boost converter. The lecture states that the main topological change is the direction of the diode. This change produces substantially different operation.

The buck-boost converter can:

• Step the output voltage below the input voltage

• Step the output voltage above the input voltage

This makes it useful when the input voltage varies above and below a desired regulated output. The lecture gives a battery example:

• A fully charged battery may provide approximately 14 V.

• The desired output may be 12 V.

• As the battery discharges, its voltage may fall below 12 V, perhaps to approximately 10 V.

• A buck-boost converter can maintain a nominally constant 12 V output across this input range.

The conventional buck-boost converter discussed here is inverting. Its output voltage polarity is opposite to the input voltage polarity.

Buck-boost switch-closed interval

The analysis assumes steady-state operation, where the converter waveforms repeat identically each switching period.

When the controllable switch is closed:

• The inductor is connected directly to the source.

• Energy is transferred into the inductor.

• The inductor current ramps upward.

The inductor-current slope is:

\[

\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{V_s}{L}

\]

Over the switch-closed interval \(DT_s\):

\[

\Delta i_{L,\text{on}}=\frac{V_sD}{f_sL}

\]

This is the same current-rise expression obtained for the boost converter.

Buck-boost switch-open interval

When the switch is opened, the inductor is disconnected from the source and releases its stored energy through the diode and output circuit.

The inductor voltage reverses as the inductor attempts to maintain current in the same direction. This reversal causes the output voltage to have polarity opposite to the input voltage.

The lecturer emphasises that the sign convention may show one polarity across the inductor, while the actual inductor voltage during energy release has the opposite physical polarity. The negative sign in the final voltage-conversion expression represents the inverted output polarity.

Non-ideal switches

Real switches do not behave as ideal open or closed circuits.

Important non-ideal properties include:

• A finite on-state voltage drop or resistance

• A finite turn-on time

• A finite turn-off time

• Power dissipation

The lecturer introduces \(v_Q\) as the switch voltage and \(v_D\) as the diode voltage. The symbol \(Q\) is commonly used to represent a transistor or semiconductor switch.

For an ideal buck converter:

\[

V_{\text{out}}=V_sD

\]

With finite switch and diode voltage drops, the output voltage is reduced. The lecture gives the relationship in the form:

\[

V_{\text{out}}

=

V_sD

-

D\,V_Q

-

(1-D)V_D

\]

where \(V_Q\) and \(V_D\) are interpreted as the relevant on-state voltage drops. The switch drop acts during the fraction \(D\) of the period when the switch conducts. The diode drop acts during the remaining fraction \(1-D\).

These effects are particularly important when:

• The converter operates at low voltage

• The output voltage is only a few volts

• The converter handles high current

• The converter power is in the hundreds-of-watts or kilowatt range

In such cases, device voltage drops can become a significant fraction of the output voltage.

Conduction loss

Conduction loss occurs when a device has a finite voltage drop while current is flowing through it.

The instantaneous dissipated power is:

\[

p=v i

\]

If the non-ideal device is represented by a resistance, the loss is commonly expressed as:

\[

p_{\text{cond}}=i^2R

\]

These losses produce heat and reduce converter efficiency.

Switching loss

Switching loss occurs during turn-on and turn-off transitions when the switch voltage and current are both non-zero.

During turn-on:

• The switch voltage falls.

• The switch current rises.

• There is a finite transition interval during which both voltage and current exist.

During turn-off:

• The switch voltage rises.

• The switch current falls.

• Again, voltage and current overlap for a finite time.

The instantaneous switching power is the product \(v_Qi_Q\). The energy dissipated in one transition is the integral of this power over the transition interval:

\[

E_{\text{sw}}=\int v_Q(t)i_Q(t)\,dt

\]

The average switching loss increases with switching frequency:

\[

P_{\text{sw,avg}}\approx f_sE_{\text{sw}}

\]

The lecture contrasts two transition shapes:

• A relatively short voltage-current overlap, producing smaller switching loss

• A transition in which current rises while voltage remains high before the voltage falls, producing a larger overlap area and greater loss

Parasitic inductance and capacitance make ideal simultaneous transitions unlikely in practical circuits. The exact transition shape depends on the circuit and the stored energy in parasitic elements.

At low switching frequencies, conduction losses tend to dominate because the switch spends more time in its conducting state. As switching frequency increases, switching transitions occur more often, so switching losses become increasingly important and can dominate.

Non-ideal capacitors

A real capacitor includes parasitic elements:

• Equivalent series resistance (ESR)

• Equivalent series inductance (ESL)

ESR is usually small but non-zero. Current through ESR causes:

• An additional voltage drop

• Power dissipation

• Heating

• Increased output-voltage ripple

The loss associated with ESR is approximately:

\[

P_{\text{ESR}}=I_{\text{rms}}^2R_{\text{ESR}}

\]

ESL is also usually small but non-zero. Together, the ideal capacitance, ESR, and ESL form a lightly damped series-resonant network.

Fast switching produces large \(dv/dt\) and \(di/dt\) transients. These transients can excite the parasitic resonant circuit. Possible consequences include:

• Ringing or oscillating waveforms

• Unexpected voltage excursions

• Substantial attenuation or distortion near resonance

The lecture indicates that this behaviour is particularly relevant in the hundreds-of-kilohertz switching-frequency range. The lecturer also mentions avoiding switching frequencies above approximately 100 kHz for the solar-car buck-converter project, although this project-specific recommendation should be checked against the course design requirements.

Non-ideal inductors

A power inductor is physically made from a coil of wire wound around magnetic material. The winding has finite resistance, commonly represented by \(R_L\).

This resistance causes:

• Voltage drop

• \(i^2R\) conduction loss

• Heating

• Reduced conversion efficiency

• Reduced output voltage

The effect is especially significant in a boost converter operated at a high duty ratio. The ideal boost relationship predicts very large output voltage as \(D\) approaches one, but inductor resistance limits this behaviour.

The lecture states that including \(R_L\) produces an output-voltage expression that depends on:

• Duty ratio \(D\)

• Inductor resistance \(R_L\)

• Load resistance \(R\)

• The input voltage

The exact displayed equation was not captured clearly by the local ASR. The qualitative result is clear:

• Increasing \(R_L\) reduces the output voltage.

• At high duty ratios, the output voltage eventually decreases as duty ratio is increased.

• The high-duty-ratio behaviour is approximately scaled by a factor involving \(1-D\), rather than continuing to increase according to the ideal \(1/(1-D)\) relationship.

• A smaller \(R_L\) allows the converter to follow the ideal boost characteristic for a wider duty-ratio range, but the high-duty-ratio limitation remains.

This is a major practical limitation on attempting to obtain very high boost ratios from a conventional boost converter.

Start-up transients and soft starting

Most of the preceding analysis assumes steady state. It does not describe what happens when the converter is first turned on from zero stored energy.

During start-up, the converter can experience:

• Large current spikes

• Large voltage spikes

• Temporary abnormal or oscillatory behaviour

• Component stress or possible damage

The lecturer notes that simulations may look unusual immediately after the converter is started. The steady-state waveform should be considered after the transient has settled.

A common mitigation technique is soft starting. Instead of immediately applying the final duty ratio, the control system gradually increases the duty ratio from zero. This ramps the energy delivered to the converter and limits the rate at which current and voltage build up.
