Overview

Lecture 2 introduces the calculations and physical principles needed to analyse power-electronic converters, with emphasis on:

• Instantaneous and average power.

• Energy as the time integral of power.

• Ideal inductors and capacitors as lossless energy-storage elements.

• Periodic steady-state conditions, especially inductor volt-second balance and capacitor charge balance.

• RMS values for periodic, non-sinusoidal waveforms.

• Duty ratio and the average/RMS values of a rectangular pulse.

• The behaviour of an inductor under switched positive and negative voltages.

• Recovery of stored inductor energy through diodes.

• The destructive voltage transient produced when an energised inductor is abruptly open-circuited.

The principal takeaway is that an inductor’s current cannot change instantaneously. Applying a constant voltage produces a linear current ramp, while interrupting the current without providing an alternative path can generate a dangerously large voltage.

Key concepts

• Power electronics relies on repeated switching and often involves periodic but non-sinusoidal waveforms.

• The sign of power depends on the selected voltage polarity and current reference direction.

• Ideal inductors and capacitors store and release energy without dissipating net average power.

• An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field; a capacitor stores energy in its electric field.

• In periodic steady state:

• The average voltage across an ideal inductor is zero.

• The net change in inductor current over each period is zero.

• The average current through an ideal capacitor is zero.

• The net change in capacitor voltage over each period is zero.

• Zero net change over a period does not imply zero average current or voltage.

• RMS values represent the equivalent heating or power-producing effect of a waveform.

• For a pulse of amplitude \(V_s\) and duty ratio \(D\):

• \(V_{\text{avg}}=DV_s\).

• \(V_{\text{RMS}}=V_s\sqrt{D}\), assuming \(V_s\geq 0\).

• A constant voltage across an ideal inductor produces a constant \(di/dt\), hence a linear current ramp.

• When an energised inductor is disconnected from its source, it reverses its terminal voltage as necessary to maintain current in the same direction.

• Diodes can provide a controlled current path that returns the inductor’s stored energy to the source.

• An energised inductor must not be open-circuited without a flyback, freewheeling, clamp, snubber, or other suitable current path.

Detailed notes

Role of inductors in power electronics

Inductors are central to power-electronic converters because their voltage-current relationship enables controlled storage, transfer, and recovery of energy. Capacitors act as complementary storage elements, while semiconductor switches and diodes determine the paths through which energy flows.

Introductory converter analysis repeatedly applies a small set of procedures:

1. Identify each switch state.

2. Replace semiconductor devices with their ideal open- or closed-switch equivalents.

3. Draw the equivalent circuit for each state.

4. Determine the voltage across each energy-storage element.

5. Use the inductor or capacitor constitutive equation to determine the waveform slope.

6. Enforce periodic steady-state conditions over a complete switching cycle.

Instantaneous power and sign convention

Instantaneous power is the product of instantaneous voltage and current:

\[

p(t)=v(t)i(t)

\]

The lecture used a source-oriented sign interpretation:

• Positive power was associated with a source delivering energy when current leaves the terminal marked positive.

• Negative power was associated with energy being absorbed or returned.

This differs from the passive sign convention commonly used in circuit theory, where current entering the positive terminal gives positive absorbed power. The physical interpretation must therefore be tied explicitly to the reference directions shown in the circuit.

For a DC source driving a resistor, the source delivers power and the resistor absorbs it. For an AC source driving a resistive load, voltage and current change sign together, so the direction of power transfer remains from the source to the resistor throughout the cycle.

Energy and average power

Power is the time derivative of energy, so energy can be obtained by integrating power:

\[

W=\int p(t)\,dt

\]

The symbol \(W\) may denote work or energy, whereas upright \(\mathrm{W}\) denotes the unit watt. Energy is measured in joules.

For a periodic waveform of period \(T\), average power is:

\[

P_{\text{avg}}

=

\frac{1}{T}

\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}p(t)\,dt

\]

If \(W_T\) is the energy transferred during one period:

\[

P_{\text{avg}}=\frac{W_T}{T}=fW_T

\]

where:

\[

f=\frac{1}{T}

\]

Average power may also be called real power in power-system contexts.

Ideal inductors and capacitors

Ideal inductors and capacitors are lossless storage elements:

• They can absorb energy temporarily.

• They can subsequently return that energy.

• Their average power over periodic steady-state operation is zero.

• Real components have parasitic resistance and therefore incur losses, but these were initially neglected.

For an inductor:

\[

v_L(t)=L\frac{di_L(t)}{dt}

\]

A corresponding integral form is:

\[

i_L(t)=i_L(t_0)+\frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t}v_L(\tau)\,d\tau

\]

For a capacitor:

\[

i_C(t)=C\frac{dv_C(t)}{dt}

\]

A corresponding integral form is:

\[

v_C(t)=v_C(t_0)+\frac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t}i_C(\tau)\,d\tau

\]

The stored energies are:

\[

W_L(t)=\frac{1}{2}Li_L^2(t)

\]

\[

W_C(t)=\frac{1}{2}Cv_C^2(t)

\]

An inductor’s energy is associated with its magnetic field, which is produced by current. A capacitor’s energy is associated with its electric field, which is produced by voltage across the capacitor.

Periodic steady-state conditions

For an ideal inductor operating periodically:

\[

\frac{1}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}v_L(t)\,dt=0

\]

Equivalently:

\[

\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}v_L(t)\,dt=0

\]

This is inductor volt-second balance. Positive and negative areas under the inductor-voltage waveform must cancel over one complete period.

Since:

\[

\Delta i_L=\frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}v_L(t)\,dt

\]

periodic steady state requires:

\[

i_L(t_0+T)=i_L(t_0)

\]

and therefore:

\[

\Delta i_L\big|_{\text{over one period}}=0

\]

This does not mean that:

• The instantaneous current is constant.

• The current ripple is zero.

• The average inductor current is zero.

The current may have a non-zero DC average with a triangular ripple superimposed on it. The peak-to-peak ripple, commonly written \(\Delta I_L\), is finite even though the net current change from the beginning to the end of a period is zero.

For an ideal capacitor in periodic steady state:

\[

\frac{1}{T}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}i_C(t)\,dt=0

\]

Equivalently:

\[

\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}i_C(t)\,dt=0

\]

This is capacitor charge balance. Since:

\[

\Delta v_C=\frac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}i_C(t)\,dt

\]

periodic steady state requires:

\[

v_C(t_0+T)=v_C(t_0)

\]

and hence zero net change in capacitor voltage over a period. The capacitor may nevertheless have a non-zero average voltage and a finite voltage ripple.

RMS values

RMS means root mean square:

1. Square the waveform.

2. Take its mean over one period.

3. Take the square root.

For voltage:

\[

V_{\text{RMS}}

=

\sqrt{

\frac{1}{T}

\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}v^2(t)\,dt

}

\]

For current:

\[

I_{\text{RMS}}

=

\sqrt{

\frac{1}{T}

\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}i^2(t)\,dt

}

\]

RMS values are useful because they directly describe the heating or power-producing effect of periodic waveforms. For a resistor:

\[

P_{\text{avg}}=V_{\text{RMS}}I_{\text{RMS}}

=\frac{V_{\text{RMS}}^2}{R}

=I_{\text{RMS}}^2R

\]

The simplified product \(V_{\text{RMS}}I_{\text{RMS}}\) applies directly to a resistor, where voltage and current have the same waveform relationship. It is not generally equal to real power for arbitrary phase-shifted or differently shaped voltage and current waveforms.

Duty ratio and rectangular pulse example

Let a rectangular pulse have:

• Amplitude \(V_s\).

• Switching period \(T\).

• On-time \(DT\).

• Off-time \((1-D)T\).

• Duty ratio \(D\), with \(0\leq D\leq1\).

The waveform is:

\[

v(t)=

\begin{cases}

V_s, & 0\leq t<DT\\

0, & DT\leq t<T

\end{cases}

\]

The duty ratio is the fraction of each period for which the switch or waveform is on:

\[

D=\frac{t_{\text{on}}}{T}

\]

Thus:

• \(D=0\): always off.

• \(D=1\): always on.

• \(0<D<1\): on for a corresponding fraction of each period.

The average value is:

\[

V_{\text{avg}}

=

\frac{1}{T}\int_0^{DT}V_s\,dt

=

DV_s

\]

The RMS value is:

\[

V_{\text{RMS}}

=

\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^{DT}V_s^2\,dt}

=

|V_s|\sqrt{D}

\]

For a positive pulse amplitude:

\[

V_{\text{RMS}}=V_s\sqrt{D}

\]

For \(0<D<1\):

\[

\sqrt{D}>D

\]

Therefore:

\[

V_{\text{RMS}}>V_{\text{avg}}

\]

for a positive rectangular pulse with a nontrivial duty ratio. The RMS value, rather than the average voltage, determines resistive heating.

Common RMS results

For a sinusoid with peak amplitude \(V_p\):

\[

V_{\text{RMS}}=\frac{V_p}{\sqrt{2}}

\]

A full-wave rectified sinusoid has the same RMS value because squaring removes the sign:

\[

V_{\text{RMS}}=\frac{V_p}{\sqrt{2}}

\]

For a triangular or sawtooth waveform described in the lecture as having peak amplitude \(V_p\):

\[

V_{\text{RMS}}=\frac{V_p}{\sqrt{3}}

\]

The lecturer stated that this result is independent of the rise/fall asymmetry or “form factor” of the triangular waveform. The exact amplitude reference and waveform offset should be checked against the slide, because RMS results depend on whether \(V_p\) denotes a unipolar peak, bipolar peak, or peak deviation from a DC level.

Analysis of the inductor energy-recovery circuit

The example circuit contained:

• A DC source \(V_{CC}\).

• An inductor.

• Two synchronously controlled N-channel enhancement MOSFETs.

• Two diodes arranged to conduct when the MOSFETs turn off.

• Gate-drive details omitted to focus on power-stage behaviour.

The general analysis method was to separate operation into two switch states.

State 1: MOSFETs on

When both MOSFETs are on:

• They are treated as ideal closed switches.

• Both diodes are reverse-biased.

• The source is connected across the inductor.

• The inductor voltage is approximately:

\[

v_L=+V_{CC}

\]

• The switch current equals the inductor current:

\[

i_S=i_L

\]

Using:

\[

v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}

\]

the current slope is:

\[

\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{V_{CC}}{L}

\]

Therefore the current rises linearly:

\[

i_L(t)=i_L(t_0)+\frac{V_{CC}}{L}(t-t_0)

\]

If the initial current is taken as zero and the on-state starts at \(t=0\):

\[

i_L(t)=\frac{V_{CC}}{L}t

\]

During this state, current enters the inductor terminal identified as positive. The inductor absorbs energy, and its magnetic field grows.

State 2: MOSFETs off

When both MOSFETs turn off:

• The inductor attempts to maintain current in the same physical direction.

• Its terminal voltage reverses.

• The changed node voltages forward-bias the two diodes.

• A path is created from the inductor back into the DC source.

• The source now absorbs the energy previously stored in the inductor.

While the diodes conduct, the lecture treated the inductor voltage as:

\[

v_L=-V_{CC}

\]

Therefore:

\[

\frac{di_L}{dt}=-\frac{V_{CC}}{L}

\]

The current remains in the same direction but decreases linearly. The negative sign applies to the rate of change of current, not necessarily to the current direction itself.

If the off-state begins at \(t=t_1\):

\[

i_L(t)=i_L(t_1)-\frac{V_{CC}}{L}(t-t_1)

\]

The source current changes sign relative to its delivering-power direction because energy is being returned to the source.

With equal positive and negative voltage magnitudes, equal time intervals are required to produce equal-magnitude increases and decreases in current:

\[

V_{CC}t_{\text{on}}-V_{CC}t_{\text{off}}=0

\]

so:

\[

t_{\text{on}}=t_{\text{off}}

\]

for the particular idealised waveform shown. This is also a consequence of inductor volt-second balance.

Why the inductor current ramps

For either switch state:

\[

\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{v_L}{L}

\]

If \(v_L\) and \(L\) are constant, then \(di_L/dt\) is constant. A function with a constant derivative is linear, so the inductor current is a ramp:

• Positive constant voltage gives a positive slope.

• Negative constant voltage gives a negative slope.

• Zero voltage gives constant current in the ideal model.

This explains why triangular inductor-current waveforms occur frequently in switched converters.

Abruptly open-circuiting an energised inductor

Suppose an inductor carries a non-zero current and its current path is suddenly opened. An instantaneous fall from \(I_L\) to zero would require:

\[

\frac{di_L}{dt}\rightarrow-\infty

\]

Because:

\[

v_L=L\frac{di_L}{dt}

\]

the ideal model predicts a correspondingly extreme voltage of the polarity needed to keep the current flowing.

In a real circuit, the voltage rises until some available path conducts. That path may be:

• An intended flyback or freewheeling diode.

• A clamp or snubber.

• Parasitic capacitance.

• An electrical arc.

• Avalanche breakdown of the switching device.

• Destructive insulation or semiconductor breakdown.

Without a deliberate energy-release path, the switching device can be permanently damaged. Avoiding this condition is a fundamental requirement in power-electronic circuit design.
