Overview

This lecture continues the analysis of rectifiers, focusing on:

• Total harmonic distortion (THD) of rectifier input current.

• A full-bridge rectifier with an inductively smoothed, approximately constant-current load.

• Comparison of constant-current and constant-voltage source/load cases.

• Fourier-series symmetry used to calculate the fundamental current component.

• The effect of practical source inductance on diode commutation, waveforms, and THD.

• The use of LTspice as a learning and analysis tool.

The main exam emphasis is on plotting rectifier voltage and current waveforms, recognising waveform symmetry, calculating or interpreting THD, and explaining qualitatively why one waveform has more distortion than another.

Key concepts

• THD measures how far a periodic waveform differs from a purely sinusoidal waveform due to harmonic components.

• THD is independent of frequency when the waveform shape is unchanged. Changing the period or frequency does not change the ratio of harmonic content to fundamental content.

• A full-bridge rectifier with a large inductance in series with the load can be modelled as having a constant-current load.

• The large inductor resists sudden changes in current. In the ideal mathematical model, the current is treated as constant and the AC-side current is approximated by a square wave.

• The square-wave approximation is not physically exact. A real inductor produces finite transition slopes, a short transition interval, and some ripple.

• A square-wave current contains primarily odd harmonics because of its symmetry. Even Fourier components cancel.

• For a constant-current full-bridge rectifier, the THD is independent of the DC load current because the current magnitude cancels from the ratio used to calculate THD.

• The constant-voltage case produces a substantially more distorted current waveform than the constant-current case discussed.

• Source inductance causes current commutation to occur over a finite interval rather than instantaneously.

• Source inductance causes the current waveform to have slopes during commutation and modifies the voltage waveform.

• The added slope can make the current waveform more sinusoidal near zero crossings and can reduce THD, but the improvement depends on the actual source inductance.

• Source inductance varies with the electrical connection and location on the grid, so a product should not rely on an assumed grid inductance to meet harmonic standards.

• RMS values are used for current and harmonic limits because they are related to heating and resistive losses.

• LTspice can be used to inspect waveforms, perform FFT analysis, and read RMS and fundamental values, but exam calculations must be reproducible by hand.

Detailed notes

THD and frequency

The lecturer began by checking the meaning of THD. THD is concerned with the difference between the actual waveform and its fundamental sinusoidal component, expressed through the harmonic content.

If a waveform is stretched or compressed in time without changing its shape, the THD does not change. Therefore, the THD calculation does not depend on the time period or frequency of the waveform. A waveform at 400 Hz has the same THD as an identically shaped waveform at another frequency.

Inductively smoothed load

The lecture moved to a full-bridge rectifier with a constant-current load. An inductively smoothed load is modelled using a sufficiently large inductor.

The ideal model treats the inductor as effectively infinite:

• The inductor maintains approximately constant current.

• The load current is represented as a current source.

• The current does not instantly change direction or magnitude.

• The current remains continuous through the rectifier.

An infinite inductance cannot be achieved physically. Large inductors are bulky, heavy, difficult to wind, and particularly undesirable in aerospace applications. However, a finite inductor can be sufficiently large for the idealised constant-current model to be a useful approximation.

The lecturer gave approximately 1 mH as a rule-of-thumb scale that may be sufficient for the mathematical assumption in some situations, while also mentioning larger values such as 10 mH, 100 mH, or approximately 200 mH in larger applications. These values were illustrative rather than universal design criteria.

The lecturer also noted that active filtering can use op-amps to emulate filtering behaviour while avoiding a physically large inductor. This topic was identified as material for a later part of the course.

Ideal full-bridge rectifier waveforms

For the full-bridge rectifier:

• During one half-cycle, diodes D1 and D2 conduct.

• During the other half-cycle, diodes D3 and D4 conduct.

• The load current always flows in the same direction.

• The DC-side current is approximately constant because of the large inductance.

• The AC supply current changes polarity every half-cycle and is therefore approximately a square wave.

The supply voltage is sinusoidal. The rectifier output voltage is the rectified waveform associated with the conducting diode pair. The AC current waveform is not sinusoidal, despite being supplied from a sinusoidal voltage source.

The square-wave current is an ideal mathematical approximation. In a real circuit:

• The current cannot transition instantaneously.

• The inductor causes a finite transition time.

• The current transition has a slope.

• There may be a small amount of ripple.

• The ideal square-wave model is used because the transition is sufficiently rapid for many analytical calculations.

Fourier-series symmetry

The AC current waveform has useful symmetry over one period, which can simplify Fourier analysis.

The lecture used an angular variable such as:

• σ = ωt

The notation was inconsistent in the transcript, but the lecturer explicitly stated that the symbol used for the phase variable represented ωt.

For the waveform over 0 to 2π:

• The current is approximately +ID over one half-cycle.

• The current is approximately −ID over the other half-cycle.

• The waveform can be treated geometrically as odd about a vertical centre line through the middle of the selected period.

• The second half of the integral can therefore be related to the first half using symmetry.

For the sine coefficient associated with the fundamental, the contributions from the two halves reinforce because both the waveform and the relevant trigonometric function change sign. This allows the integral over 0 to 2π to be replaced by twice an integral over 0 to π.

For the cosine coefficient, the contributions cancel because the waveform symmetry produces equal and opposite areas. Thus, the lecturer stated that the first cosine coefficient can be set to zero by recognising the odd symmetry rather than calculating the full integral.

The lecture used a geometric interpretation of odd symmetry about a vertical centre line rather than relying only on the standard mathematical definition about the origin. The key practical idea is to identify symmetry over the chosen interval and use it to simplify the Fourier coefficients.

Constant-current THD calculation

For the ideal constant-current full-bridge rectifier:

• The total AC-side current RMS value is equal to ID because the current has constant magnitude, even though its sign alternates.

• The fundamental component is obtained from the Fourier coefficient.

• The fundamental RMS current is proportional to ID.

• When the ratio of total RMS current to fundamental RMS current is formed, ID cancels.

• THD approximately equal to 0.48, or approximately 48%.

The result is therefore independent of the value of ID within the ideal constant-current model.

The important qualitative conclusion is that the constant-voltage-source/load waveform is highly dependent on the operating voltage or load. As the output voltage changes, the width of the zero-current or zero-voltage interval changes. The waveform can become increasingly unlike a sine wave, increasing its harmonic content and THD.

By contrast, the ideal constant-current case has a fixed waveform shape and therefore a THD that is independent of ID.

The lecturer encouraged an intuitive comparison:

• The approximately square constant-current waveform is not sinusoidal, but it follows a regular alternating shape.

• The constant-voltage waveform contains a longer flat interval and is further from a sine wave.

• A waveform with a longer zero interval and more abrupt departures from a sine wave is expected to have greater THD.

Scaling and THD

The absolute amplitude of the waveform does not affect THD if every component scales by the same factor.

If the waveform amplitude is doubled:

• The total RMS current doubles.

• The fundamental RMS current doubles.

• The harmonic RMS currents also double.

• Their ratios remain unchanged.

• THD remains the same.

Therefore, the waveform shape and relative harmonic content are more important than the absolute amplitude when comparing THD.

FFT interpretation

The lecturer used LTspice FFT output to inspect the harmonic spectrum.

For an ideal square wave:

• The fundamental is present.

• The third, fifth, seventh, and higher odd harmonics are present.

• Even harmonics are absent or cancel because of waveform symmetry.

• An infinite series of sine waves at odd multiples of the fundamental frequency can approximate a square wave.

For a 50 Hz waveform, the odd harmonic frequencies shown include:

• 50 Hz: fundamental.

• 150 Hz: third harmonic.

• 250 Hz: fifth harmonic.

• Higher odd multiples continue similarly.

When reading LTspice results, the lecturer distinguished between:

• The RMS value of the fundamental component.

• A Fourier coefficient or peak/amplitude quantity.

The fundamental value read from LTspice FFT was an RMS value, not necessarily the raw Fourier coefficient. This distinction is important when calculating γ and THD.

RMS values and practical standards

The lecturer stated that electrical safety and harmonic standards generally specify RMS values rather than waveform amplitudes.

RMS values are used because they relate to:

• Heating in resistive components.

• Resistive power losses.

• Current-carrying requirements.

• Practical equipment stress and safety limits.

Consequently, the fundamental and total current values used in THD calculations must be compared on a consistent RMS basis.

Source inductance and current commutation

The next topic was source inductance, denoted in the transcript as LS. The transcript occasionally misrecognised inductance as “conductance,” but the context clearly refers to source inductance.

A real supply and grid connection always contain some inductance. The lecturer gave a typical source-inductance range of approximately 1–3 mH, although the exact value depends on the supply network.

With zero source inductance, diode commutation is treated as instantaneous:

• One diode pair stops conducting.

• The next diode pair immediately begins conducting.

• The current waveform has abrupt transitions.

With non-zero source inductance:

• Current transfer between diode pairs takes a finite time.

• More than one diode pair may conduct during the commutation interval.

• The inductor prevents the current from changing instantaneously.

• The AC current waveform acquires a finite slope during the transition.

• The rectifier voltage waveform is modified during commutation.

• When all four bridge diodes conduct, the bridge can temporarily form a short-circuit path, producing the characteristic voltage behaviour shown in the lecture.

The lecturer stated that the main exam requirement for this part is to plot the relevant voltage and current waveforms.

Effect of source inductance on THD

The lecturer stated that source inductance improved the calculated THD in the example:

• Without the source-inductance effect, THD was approximately 0.48.

• With source inductance, THD was approximately 0.35.

The qualitative explanation is that the finite transition slope makes the current waveform more similar to a sine wave near the zero crossing. The abrupt vertical edge of an ideal square wave is replaced by a sloped transition, reducing some high-frequency harmonic content.

However, source inductance should not be treated as a guaranteed design solution:

• Its value varies between grid locations.

• Network impedance and harmonic conditions vary.

• A design that meets a harmonic limit under one assumed source inductance may fail under another.

• The rectifier itself should be designed to satisfy the relevant requirements rather than relying on an incidental grid effect.
