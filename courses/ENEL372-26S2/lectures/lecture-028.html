Detailed notes

Four-quadrant operation and motor-load matching

The lecture begins by revisiting torque-speed operation.

A motor acts as a generator when the rotational direction is opposite to the direction of the applied motor torque. Consequently, generation occurs in two of the four torque-speed quadrants rather than only one. An electric vehicle can use this behaviour during regenerative braking to return energy to the battery.

To operate in all four quadrants, the drive electronics must support the required combinations of torque direction and rotational direction.

The motor operating point is found from the intersection of:

• The motor torque-speed curve.

• The load torque-speed curve.

This intersection determines the speed and torque at which the motor and load reach equilibrium. The lecture identifies the high-efficiency operating region as an important design target, rather than simply maximising torque or speed independently.

The lecturer identifies the following BLDC advantages:

• Fast acceleration and deceleration.

• High efficiency and reduced battery energy consumption.

• Long operating life because there are no brushes to wear out.

• Quiet operation.

• High torque-to-size ratio.

• High power-to-weight ratio.

The lecture gives examples involving:

BLDC physical configurations

A common BLDC arrangement has:

• A rotor containing permanent magnets.

• A stator containing the windings.

The lecturer also discusses an alternative arrangement in which the rotor is on the outside and the windings are on the inside. This can be useful in high-speed applications such as RC aircraft because centrifugal forces can help retain the permanent magnets in position.

If permanent magnets are mounted on an inner rotor, high rotational speed can create a risk that the magnets will detach or fly off. An outer-rotor arrangement can use the centrifugal loading to press the magnets outward against their supporting structure.

The transcript refers to several geometrical configurations and notes that geometry may be selected according to the application, motor size, required speed, and required torque characteristics.

Back EMF

Back EMF is generated as the rotor’s permanent-magnet field moves relative to the stator windings.

Its waveform depends on:

• Permanent-magnet geometry.

• Winding geometry.

• Magnetic-field distribution.

• Rotor and stator geometry.

The lecturer describes back EMF as opposing the applied voltage and limiting current as speed increases. At higher speed, the increased back EMF reduces the current available for producing torque.

The back EMF waveform is therefore important both for motor performance and for commutation and control strategies.

Torque ripple

Constant torque is difficult to achieve in a real BLDC motor. As the rotor moves, the magnetic interaction varies:

1. The magnetic field reaches a position producing strong attraction or repulsion.

2. The rotor moves away from that position.

3. The electromagnetic force decreases or changes.

4. The applied phase currents are switched.

5. The cycle repeats.

These variations produce torque ripple.

Torque ripple is affected by:

• Permanent-magnet shape.

• Winding geometry.

• Number of poles or pole pairs.

• Commutation timing.

• Magnetic saturation and other non-ideal magnetic effects.

The lecturer emphasises that the motor can have a high peak torque while still having a different average torque and a significant torque ripple.

Rotor-position sensing and commutation

BLDC operation depends on switching the stator magnetic fields at the correct rotor positions. This process is called electronic commutation.

The controller must know, or estimate, the rotor position so that it can:

• Energise the correct phase windings.

• Produce the desired north and south stator poles.

• Maintain attraction and repulsion forces that continue rotating the rotor.

• Switch phases before the rotor reaches a stationary equilibrium position.

Possible rotor-position methods include:

• Hall-effect sensors.

• Rotary shaft encoders.

• Back-EMF-based sensorless estimation.

The lecturer states that direct measurement is easier than estimation, although position can be estimated. Three Hall-effect sensors are described as a simple common arrangement.

The physical spacing of the Hall sensors must correspond appropriately to the motor’s electrical phasing and number of pole pairs. Between sensor transitions, the controller can estimate velocity and predict the next switching event. The lecturer refers to this as a form of dead reckoning over short time intervals, potentially microseconds or milliseconds.

Idealised torque-speed characteristic

• A transition near the rated speed.

• A decreasing-torque region at higher speeds.

• A maximum speed at which torque approaches zero.

The flat part is an idealisation. Real motors do not produce a perfectly constant torque over the entire low-speed region.

At low speed, the motor can produce high torque continuously if thermal limits are respected. At higher speeds, back EMF increases and reduces current, causing the available torque to decrease.

Continuous and intermittent torque

Continuous torque is the torque that the motor can sustain indefinitely, subject to the specified thermal conditions.

Intermittent torque is a higher torque that can be applied for a limited period. If excessive current is maintained:

• Winding temperature rises.

• Insulation may be damaged.

• The motor may eventually overheat or burn out.

Temperature sensing can be used to improve control. A controller can monitor the winding or motor temperature and manage the allowed operating time in the high-torque region.

Rated speed, maximum speed, and duty cycle

The rated speed is associated with the intended operating region of the motor. The maximum speed is limited by both electrical and mechanical factors.

At high speed:

• Back EMF is higher.

• Current and torque fall.

• Mechanical stresses become more important.

• The motor may reach a mechanical speed limit before reaching the ideal theoretical speed.

The lecture gives an example in which a theoretical speed of approximately 8,500 rpm is not reached because the practical mechanical limit is approximately 8,000 rpm. These values refer to the illustrated example, not to BLDC motors generally.

Duty cycle controls the effective power delivered by the drive. A 100% duty cycle is described as applying the maximum available drive command, while reducing the duty cycle reduces the torque available at a given speed.

Changing duty cycle can move the operating point through different torque-speed regions. However, duty-cycle control alone may not provide the desired combination of speed and torque in every application. Gearing may be required to match the motor to the load.

BLDC motors compared with brushed DC motors

The major advantage of a BLDC motor over a brushed DC motor is the absence of brushes and a mechanical commutator.

Advantages include:

• No brush wear.

• Longer operating life.

• Less maintenance associated with brush replacement.

• No mechanical brush sparking or commutator wear.

• Potentially quieter operation.

• High efficiency and high-speed capability.

The main disadvantage is greater control complexity. A brushed DC motor is comparatively easy to control because the mechanical commutator performs the phase switching. A BLDC motor requires:

• Electronic switches.

• Rotor-position information or estimation.

• Correct commutation timing.

• A suitable control system.

The cost may be concentrated in the controller, sensing system, power electronics, and permanent magnets rather than only in the motor’s mechanical hardware.

BLDC motors compared with induction motors

The lecturer identifies several trade-offs between BLDC and induction motors.

BLDC advantages include:

• High torque-to-size ratio.

• High power-to-weight ratio.

• High efficiency.

• No rotor slip in the same sense as an induction motor.

• Strong performance over a wide speed range.

Induction motor advantages include:

• Simpler construction.

• Lower dependence on permanent magnets.

• Less complex hardware for fixed-speed operation.

• Fewer components that can fail in some applications.

The induction motor requires slip: the rotor must differ in speed from the rotating magnetic field for torque production. The lecture describes this qualitatively as the rotating magnetic field trying to move the rotor while the rotor remains slightly out of phase or behind the field.

Electronic switching and phase currents

The lecturer presents a schematic showing electronic switches connected to the motor phases. The switches control the direction of current through the windings.

Current direction determines the polarity of the stator electromagnets:

• One current direction produces one magnetic polarity.

• Reversing the current reverses the magnetic polarity.

• Correct switching sequences create a rotating stator magnetic field.

Magnetic mechanism of rotation

The basic mechanism is:

1. The rotor contains permanent magnets.

2. Current is applied to stator windings.

3. The windings create temporary north and south magnetic poles.

4. Attraction and repulsion forces act between the stator field and rotor magnets.

5. The controller changes the stator current direction as the rotor moves.

6. The changing magnetic field prevents the rotor from settling at a static alignment.

7. Continuous rotation results.

If the stator field is not switched, the rotor moves to an equilibrium position where the magnetic attraction and repulsion forces no longer produce continuous torque. The rotor then stops.

The lecturer uses a simple compass demonstration to illustrate the principle: manually switching the magnetic field direction at the correct times can keep the magnetic element rotating.

The switching should occur so that the electromagnetic torque remains appropriately directed. The lecture describes the torque peak as occurring symmetrically around the relevant switching interval.

Floating phase and sensorless operation

In a three-phase BLDC motor, the commutation sequence can leave one phase floating while the other two phases are energised.

The floating phase has no applied phase current during that part of the sequence. Its voltage can be measured to detect the back EMF.

This enables a controller to estimate rotor position without Hall sensors. The lecturer notes that the floating phase is particularly useful because its voltage can be observed directly for back-EMF detection.

Pole pairs and commutation intervals

The lecture compares motors with different numbers of pole pairs.

The stated examples are:

• One pole pair: commutation every 60 degrees.

• Two pole pairs: commutation every 30 degrees.

• More pole pairs: smaller mechanical angle between commutation events.

As the number of pole pairs increases:

• The magnetic field is switched more frequently for a given mechanical rotation.

• Torque ripple can decrease because the torque-producing interaction is refreshed more often.

• The motor can maintain torque more consistently over mechanical rotation.

However, increasing the pole-pair count indefinitely is not beneficial. Excessive pole counts can cause:

• Magnetic saturation.

• More complicated magnetic interactions.

• Reduced torque.

• Increased design difficulty.

• Other undesirable effects.

The lecturer mentions that motors with approximately 20 pole pairs or more can experience deterioration rather than continued improvement, but no general optimum is given.

Two-pole-pair sequence

The final section illustrates the switching sequence for a two-pole-pair motor.

The phase currents are switched so that:

• Positive and negative currents create the required stator magnetic poles.

• One phase may be floating during a commutation state.

• The resulting magnetic field rotates around the stator.

• The permanent-magnet rotor follows the rotating field.

The transcript indicates that the illustrated rotor rotates anticlockwise, although the direction depends on the chosen phase sequence and sign convention.

Torque ripple

Commutation

Pole pair

Continuous torque

Continuous torque is the torque that can be maintained for an extended or indefinite period without exceeding the motor’s thermal limits.

Intermittent torque
