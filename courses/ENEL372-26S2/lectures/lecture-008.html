Overview

This lecture introduces magnetic materials and magnetic circuits as they apply to power electronics. The main application is the design and construction of the power inductor for the buck converter.

Topics covered:

• The role of magnetic components in power supplies and converters.

• How current produces magnetic flux.

• Magnetising force, magnetic flux density, permeability, and saturation.

• The hysteresis curve, residual flux density, coercive force, and hysteresis loss.

• Eddy-current losses and methods of reducing them.

• Magnetic-circuit equivalents of electrical circuits.

• Magnetomotive force, reluctance, and flux.

• The effect of core geometry and air gaps on total reluctance.

• A simplified reluctance expression for the inductor core to be used in the following design process.

The lecture used an older power supply as an example. Although it contains ordinary resistors, capacitors, and semiconductor switching devices, a substantial part of its operation depends on magnetic components, including transformers, inductors, and toroidal inductors.

Key concepts

• Magnetic fields are produced by moving electric charge, usually represented in circuit analysis by electric current.

• A current-carrying coil produces magnetic flux. The right-hand rule determines the direction of the magnetic field and identifies the north pole of the coil.

• Unpaired electron spins can give an atom a net magnetic moment. The lecture used iron as the principal example of a ferromagnetic material.

• A magnetic domain is a region in a bulk ferromagnetic material in which atomic magnetic moments are aligned. Each domain behaves approximately like a small permanent magnet.

• In an unmagnetised bulk material, domains generally have different orientations, so the material has little or no net magnetic flux.

• An external magnetic field causes domains to rotate and align, increasing the net magnetic flux.

• Magnetic flux density \(B\) measures magnetic flux per unit cross-sectional area.

• Magnetising force \(H\) is the applied magnetic field quantity used to align magnetic domains.

• Relative permeability \(\mu_r\) indicates how readily a material responds to an applied magnetic field compared with free space.

• Magnetic saturation occurs when most or all domains are aligned. Further increases in \(H\) then produce only a small increase in \(B\).

• Power-inductor cores should normally be operated in the approximately linear region of the \(B\)-\(H\) characteristic and not driven into saturation.

• When the magnetising force is cycled, the magnetic material follows a hysteresis loop rather than retracing the initial magnetisation curve.

• Residual flux density remains when \(H\) returns to zero after magnetisation.

• Coercive force is the reverse magnetising force required to reduce the residual flux density to zero.

• The area enclosed by the hysteresis loop represents energy lost per magnetisation cycle.

• Hysteresis loss increases with operating frequency because the hysteresis loop is traversed more often.

• Eddy currents are induced within conductive core materials when magnetic flux changes.

• Eddy currents cause \(I^2R\) losses and heating in the core.

• Ferrite has relatively poor electrical conductivity and therefore relatively low eddy-current loss at power-electronic frequencies.

• Conductive steel cores are laminated. Electrical insulation between laminations restricts eddy-current paths and increases their effective resistance.

• A magnetic circuit can be analysed analogously to an electrical circuit:

• Magnetomotive force corresponds approximately to voltage.

• Reluctance corresponds approximately to resistance.

• Magnetic flux corresponds approximately to current.

• An air gap has much greater reluctance than a comparable length of ferrite because air has \(\mu_r \approx 1\), whereas the example ferrite has \(\mu_r = 1600\).

• In the example E-shaped core, the air-gap reluctance dominates the total reluctance, allowing the reluctance of the ferrite core and limbs to be neglected as a first approximation.

Atomic magnetic behaviour

At the atomic scale, magnetic behaviour can be associated with the spin of unpaired electrons. If the magnetic contributions of unpaired electrons do not cancel, the atom has a net magnetic field or magnetic moment.

Iron was used as an example. The lecturer stated that iron has four unpaired electrons in its outer shell and that these electrons have the same spin. In the lecture’s conceptual model, an individual iron atom therefore behaves like a very small bar magnet with north and south ends.

When neighbouring iron atoms interact, their magnetic moments can align locally. Groups of aligned atoms form magnetic domains. Each domain behaves approximately as a small permanent magnet.

Domains and magnetisation

In an unmagnetised bulk sample, domains have different orientations. Their magnetic effects largely cancel, giving the material little or no net north-south magnetic polarity.

When an external magnetic field is applied:

1. Domains begin to rotate or otherwise change orientation toward the applied-field direction.

2. The material develops a net magnetic flux.

3. Increasing the applied field produces progressively more domain alignment.

4. At sufficiently high applied field, the domains become almost completely aligned.

5. Once the domains are aligned, additional magnetising force produces little further increase in flux density.

This final condition is magnetic saturation.

\(B\)-\(H\) characteristic and permeability

The lecture considered a plot of magnetic flux density \(B\) against magnetising force \(H\).

In the approximately linear region:

• Increasing \(H\) causes an approximately proportional increase in \(B\).

• The slope is related to the material permeability.

• A higher relative permeability means that the domains align more readily.

• A higher-permeability material reaches a given flux density with less magnetising force.

At high values of \(H\), the curve bends over as the material approaches saturation. The effective relative permeability falls toward approximately one in the saturated region, so the slope approaches the permeability of free space, \(\mu_0\).

For the power-inductor design, the core flux density must remain below the selected material’s saturation limit. Exceeding this limit is undesirable because the core no longer behaves in the intended linear manner.

Magnetic materials

The lecture identified ferrite as the main magnetic material used in power electronics because of the frequencies involved. Ferrite was described as ceramic-like rather than metallic, with substantial iron-containing ferromagnetic content.

Approximate relative-permeability values stated in the lecture were:

• Ferrite: approximately \(300\) to \(5000\).

• Special steel used in lower-frequency power systems: approximately \(77{,}000\).

• Mu-metal: up to approximately \(100{,}000\).

The lecturer noted that the higher-permeability steel and mu-metal materials are frequency-limited. Mu-metal is also highly specialised and expensive. Steel is generally used at lower frequencies, from a few kilohertz down to low-frequency power-system operation, rather than in typical high-frequency power electronics.

Hysteresis

The initial \(B\)-\(H\) curve assumes that a magnetising force is applied and held at a value. In a real power converter, the current in the winding changes, so the magnetising force and flux also vary.

Starting from an initially unmagnetised state:

1. \(H\) increases in the positive direction.

2. The flux density rises, eventually reaching saturation if the force is made sufficiently large.

3. \(H\) is reduced back toward zero.

4. The path does not retrace the initial magnetisation curve.

5. At \(H=0\), a residual flux density remains.

6. Applying a reverse \(H\) is required to bring \(B\) back to zero.

7. The reverse field at \(B=0\) is the coercive force.

8. Continuing into negative \(H\) and then returning produces the opposite residual flux density.

9. Repeated cycling produces a closed hysteresis loop.

The material does not return to the original domain configuration without energy being supplied. The area enclosed by the hysteresis loop represents the energy lost during a complete magnetic cycle. Therefore, bipolar operation of a magnetic core introduces an inherent loss mechanism.

At higher switching frequency, the hysteresis loop is traversed more times per second, increasing the associated power loss. This contributes to the trade-off involved in selecting a suitable converter switching frequency.

Eddy-current loss

A changing current in the winding produces a changing magnetic flux in the core. The lecture linked this process to Faraday’s law and Lenz’s law:

• A changing flux induces a voltage.

• If the core has finite electrical conductivity, the induced voltage drives circulating currents within the core.

• These circulating currents are called eddy currents.

• The eddy currents cause \(I^2R\) loss and heating.

Ferrite has poor electrical conductivity because of its ceramic-like structure, so eddy-current loss is relatively small.

Steel is more conductive. To reduce eddy currents, steel cores are made from thin laminations:

• Each lamination is electrically insulated from the next.

• Eddy-current paths are restricted primarily to the thickness of each lamination.

• The smaller conducting path and insulation increase the effective resistance of the eddy-current loops.

• Eddy-current magnitude and loss are therefore reduced.

The two core-loss mechanisms explicitly discussed were:

1. Hysteresis loss.

2. Eddy-current loss.

Magnetic circuits

A magnetic circuit provides a convenient engineering model for analysing magnetic components. The lecturer compared it with an electrical circuit containing a voltage source, resistors, and current.

The analogous quantities are:

| Electrical circuit | Magnetic circuit |

|---|---|

| Voltage | Magnetomotive force (MMF) |

| Resistance | Reluctance |

| Current | Magnetic flux |

The MMF is produced by current flowing through the winding. For a winding with \(N\) turns carrying current \(I\), the MMF is \(NI\).

Reluctance represents opposition to the establishment of magnetic flux. It depends on:

• The length of the magnetic path.

• The cross-sectional area.

• The material permeability.

The magnetic flux is the MMF divided by the total reluctance.

Equations and definitions

Magnetic flux density

\[

B=\frac{\Phi}{A}

\]

where:

• \(B\) is magnetic flux density in teslas, \(\text{T}\).

• \(\Phi\) is magnetic flux in webers, \(\text{Wb}\).

• \(A\) is cross-sectional area in square metres, \(\text{m}^2\).

The equivalent unit relationship is:

\[

1\text{ T}=1\text{ Wb}/\text{m}^2

\]

Constitutive relation for a linear magnetic material

\[

B=\mu_0\mu_r H

\]

where:

• \(B\) is magnetic flux density.

• \(H\) is magnetising force or magnetic field strength, in \(\text{A}/\text{m}\).

• \(\mu_0\) is the permeability of free space.

• \(\mu_r\) is the relative permeability of the material.

The lecture gave:

\[

\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{H/m}

\]

The slope of the linear \(B\)-\(H\) curve is:

\[

\frac{\Delta B}{\Delta H}\approx\mu_0\mu_r

\]

This relation is only applicable as an approximation in the material’s linear operating region. Near saturation, the effective slope changes significantly.

Faraday’s law

The lecture transcribed the induced-voltage relation as:

\[

v_{\text{induced}}=N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}

\]

The polarity is determined using Lenz’s law. Depending on the chosen voltage and flux reference directions, the conventional signed form is:

\[

v_{\text{induced}}=-N\frac{d\Phi}{dt}

\]

The negative sign indicates that the induced voltage opposes the change in flux.

Magnetomotive force

\[

\mathcal{F}=NI

\]

where:

• \(\mathcal{F}\) is magnetomotive force, in ampere-turns.

• \(N\) is the number of winding turns.

• \(I\) is winding current.

The lecturer described MMF as analogous to voltage in an electrical circuit.

Reluctance

\[

\mathcal{R}=\frac{l}{\mu A}

=

\frac{l}{\mu_0\mu_r A}

\]

where:

• \(\mathcal{R}\) is magnetic reluctance.

• \(l\) is magnetic path length.

• \(A\) is cross-sectional area.

• \(\mu=\mu_0\mu_r\) is the material permeability.

Reluctance is analogous to electrical resistance.

Magnetic-circuit form of Ohm’s law

\[

\Phi=\frac{\mathcal{F}}{\mathcal{R}}

=

\frac{NI}{\mathcal{R}}

\]

where magnetic flux \(\Phi\) is analogous to electrical current.

Air-gap reluctance

For an air gap:

\[

\mu_r\approx1

\]

Therefore:

\[

\mathcal{R}_{\text{air gap}}

\approx

\frac{l_{\text{gap}}}{\mu_0 A}

\]

Because ferrite has a much higher \(\mu_r\), the air-gap reluctance can dominate the reluctance of the complete magnetic circuit.

Definitions

• Magnetic flux \(\Phi\): The total magnetic field passing through a specified area.

• Magnetic flux density \(B\): Magnetic flux per unit area.

• Magnetising force \(H\): The magnetic field quantity that drives domain alignment.

• Permeability \(\mu\): A measure of how readily a material supports magnetic flux.

• Relative permeability \(\mu_r\): Material permeability relative to free-space permeability.

• Magnetic domain: A region in which atomic magnetic moments are aligned.

• Saturation: The condition in which further increases in magnetising force produce little additional flux density.

• Residual flux density: Flux density remaining when magnetising force returns to zero after prior magnetisation.

• Coercive force: Reverse magnetising force required to reduce residual flux density to zero.

• Hysteresis: Dependence of the magnetic state on the material’s previous magnetisation history.

• Eddy current: A circulating current induced within a conductive core by changing magnetic flux.

• Magnetomotive force: The quantity \(NI\) that drives magnetic flux through a magnetic circuit.

• Reluctance: Opposition to magnetic flux in a magnetic circuit.
